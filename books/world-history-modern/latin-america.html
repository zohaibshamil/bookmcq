📖 Latin America

Problems in Latin America: Inequality, Debt and Political Instability

During the twentieth century, Latin American countries faced persistent economic inequality, political instability, foreign intervention, military dictatorships, and conflicts over national development. Brazil, Mexico, Venezuela, Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Ecuador, and Central American states experienced different struggles over political sovereignty and economic independence. The 1980s debt crisis intensified poverty and weakened economic growth, while socialist movements, nationalist governments, and democratic opposition challenged established political arrangements. Civil wars in Guatemala and Nicaragua further demonstrated the region's political divisions and international Cold War involvement.

Background: Colonial Economies, Land Inequality and US Influence

Students should understand Spanish and Portuguese colonisation, Latin American independence movements during the nineteenth century, and the emergence of export-oriented economies dependent on agricultural products and minerals. Knowledge of unequal land ownership, foreign investment, American influence under the Monroe Doctrine, and the Roosevelt Corollary provides essential historical context. Familiarity with the Mexican Revolution of 1910, the Great Depression, the expansion of military governments, and the Cold War is particularly important. Understanding socialism, nationalism, populism, import-substitution industrialisation, and the growth of labour movements helps explain the political and economic alternatives pursued by Latin American governments.

Learning Outcomes: Debt Crisis, Development and Political Change

Students should be able to explain the principal economic, political, and social difficulties confronting Latin American countries and evaluate proposed solutions. They should analyse the causes and consequences of the 1980s debt crisis, compare national development strategies in Brazil, Mexico, Venezuela, and Tanzania's regional counterparts, and assess the influence of American foreign policy. They should explain the origins of the Guatemalan and Nicaraguan conflicts, evaluate military dictatorship and democratic transitions, and distinguish the political experiences of Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, and Ecuador.

Topics: Brazil, Mexico, Venezuela and Military Dictatorships

  • Historical and Structural Problems - Colonial economic legacies, inequality, land concentration, poverty, and political instability.
  • Economic Development and External Dependence - Industrialisation, foreign investment, export dependence, inflation, and development strategies.
  • Brazil, Mexico, and Venezuela - Political transformations, economic policies, social inequalities, and challenges to American influence.
  • Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, and Ecuador - Nationalist movements, military governments, political reform, and foreign intervention.
  • Central American Political Conflicts - Guatemalan civil war, Nicaraguan revolution, and American involvement in regional conflicts.
  • Latin American Debt Crisis, 1980s - External borrowing, rising interest rates, debt restructuring, austerity, and economic stagnation.
  • Political and Economic Solutions - Democratic government, economic diversification, regional cooperation, and development challenges.

Study Sequence: From Colonial Legacies to the Debt Crisis

Begin with the common historical problems inherited from colonialism, particularly unequal land distribution, dependence on primary exports, poverty, and political instability. Next, examine economic expansion and political transformations in Brazil, Mexico, and Venezuela. Continue with nationalist movements, military dictatorships, and American influence in Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, and Ecuador. Study the Guatemalan Civil War and Nicaraguan Revolution to understand the relationship between domestic conflicts and Cold War intervention. Then examine the 1980s debt crisis, its economic and social consequences, and the international responses. Conclude by comparing proposed solutions involving democratic reform, economic diversification, social development, and regional cooperation.

How Export Dependence, Foreign Debt and Intervention Shaped the Region

Colonial economic structures and unequal resource distribution created long-term development problems, while dependence on commodity exports increased vulnerability to international markets. Political instability encouraged military intervention and authoritarian governments, frequently intensified by Cold War rivalries. Nationalist and socialist movements challenged established economic and political arrangements. Heavy foreign borrowing during the 1970s, followed by rising international interest rates, produced the 1982 debt crisis, deepening unemployment, poverty, and political pressure for reform.

Timeline: Borrowing Boom and the 1982 Latin American Debt Crisis

During the 1970s, Mexico and other Latin American countries increased foreign borrowing to finance development and public expenditure. Rising international interest rates and recession made repayment increasingly difficult. In August 1982, Mexican Finance Minister Jesús Silva Herzog informed American and international financial authorities that Mexico could no longer service its external debt. International banks restricted new lending, forcing governments to negotiate restructuring arrangements. The resulting debt crisis spread across the region, producing austerity, unemployment, reduced public spending, and the prolonged economic difficulties commonly described as Latin America's lost decade.

Why Economic Dependence and Political Conflict Matter

Latin America's experience explains the relationship between economic dependence, political instability, foreign intervention, and social inequality in developing countries. Understanding these developments provides essential background for studying Cold War interventions, military dictatorships, democratic transitions, international debt negotiations, and continuing debates about economic sovereignty and sustainable development.

Common Misconceptions: US Intervention and Causes of the Debt Crisis

'All Latin American military dictatorships were established through identical American interventions' overlooks the different domestic political circumstances and external influences in each country. 'The 1982 debt crisis began entirely because of government overspending' ignores international lending practices, rising interest rates, and declining export earnings. Finally, 'the Sandinista Revolution was a communist movement created by the United States' confuses American opposition to the Sandinistas with their independent revolutionary struggle against Somoza's dictatorship.

Exam Focus: Development Strategies, Debt and Revolutionary Movements

Frequently tested questions include matching countries with political leaders, economic policies, military governments, and revolutionary movements. Students should explain the causes and consequences of the Latin American debt crisis, compare development strategies in Brazil, Mexico, and Venezuela, and identify the significance of American intervention in Chile, Nicaragua, and Guatemala. Chronology questions examine civil wars and political transitions, while analytical questions assess economic dependency, authoritarianism, democratic reform, and regional development strategies.

Summary: Unequal Growth and Political Change in Latin America

Latin American countries pursued different political and economic responses to inherited inequality, foreign dependence, and development difficulties. Nationalist governments, socialist programmes, military dictatorships, and revolutionary movements produced contrasting outcomes. Cold War intervention intensified several domestic conflicts, while the 1980s debt crisis deepened economic hardship throughout the region. Democratic transitions created new political opportunities, but poverty, unequal wealth distribution, institutional weaknesses, and dependence on international markets continued to influence regional development.

Key Terms, Economic Policies and Latin American Leaders

  • Import-Substitution Industrialisation - An economic strategy encouraging domestic manufacturing to replace imported industrial products through protection and government support.
  • Latin American Debt Crisis - The financial crisis beginning in 1982 when several countries struggled to meet external debt obligations.
  • Nationalisation - The transfer of privately owned businesses, industries, or natural resources into government ownership.
  • Dependency Theory - An approach explaining how unequal relationships between industrialised and developing economies can reinforce economic dependence.
  • Kwame Nkrumah - Not applicable to Latin American history; important regional figures include Getúlio Vargas, the Brazilian nationalist leader associated with state-led industrialisation.
  • Daniel Ortega - Sandinista political leader who became President of Nicaragua in 1985 following the 1979 revolution.
  • Salvador Allende - Chilean socialist president elected in 1970 who was overthrown in the military coup of 11 September 1973.
  • Augusto Pinochet - Chilean military ruler who led the dictatorship established after the 1973 coup against Allende.

ECLAC and Financial Records on Latin American Development

  • Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC) - Explore historical economic reports, poverty statistics, industrialisation policies, inequality, international trade, and development strategies across Latin American countries.
  • Federal Reserve History: Latin American Debt Crisis - Examine the origins of the 1982 crisis, international bank lending, rising interest rates, Mexico's debt difficulties, and the subsequent Brady Plan.
  • National Security Archive: Chile and US Relations - Investigate declassified documents concerning Salvador Allende, American covert operations, the military coup of 1973, and Pinochet's dictatorship.
  • National Security Archive: Nicaragua 1978–1990 - Research the Sandinista Revolution, overthrow of Somoza, American-sponsored Contra activities, and the 1990 Nicaraguan elections.
  • Guatemala Historical Clarification Commission - Examine the 1999 report Guatemala: Memory of Silence, investigating the origins, political causes, human rights violations, and consequences of the 1960–1996 civil war.
  • Brazilian National Archives - Explore the 1964 military coup, military dictatorship from 1964 to 1985, economic development programmes, political repression, and the return to civilian government.
  • Argentina Declassification Project - Investigate American diplomatic and intelligence records concerning the 1976 military coup, the 1976–1983 dictatorship, and human rights abuses.
  • Mexican National Archives and Historical Collections - Research the Mexican Revolution, twentieth-century political development, the Institutional Revolutionary Party, economic nationalism, and the 1982 debt crisis.
  • Venezuelan and Andean Historical Archives - Examine oil nationalisation in Venezuela, political instability, economic dependence, military governments, and nationalist movements in Bolivia and Ecuador.
  • Inter-American Commission on Human Rights - Explore historical investigations, human rights reports, political repression, disappearances, military dictatorships, and democratic transitions across Latin America.
  • World Bank Latin American Development Reports - Investigate poverty reduction, economic inequality, debt management, agricultural development, public institutions, and strategies for sustainable economic growth.
