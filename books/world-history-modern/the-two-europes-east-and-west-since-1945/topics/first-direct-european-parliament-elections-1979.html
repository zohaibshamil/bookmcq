What is First direct European Parliament elections 1979?

In 1979, citizens across the European Community directly elected Members of the European Parliament for the first time, significantly enhancing democratic legitimacy and transforming the Parliament from an appointed advisory body into a more influential institution representing European voters.

📝 All First direct European Parliament elections 1979 MCQs

Q1. In which year were the first direct elections to the European Parliament held?

A. 1975
B. 1979 ✅
C. 1981
D. 1984

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The inaugural direct elections took place in June 1979, marking a historic moment for the Community as citizens could finally vote for their Euro‑MPs. This year is widely cited in textbooks and official EU histories as the starting point of parliamentary democracy at the European level.

Q2. How many Euro‑MPs were elected in the 1979 elections?

A. 350
B. 400
C. 410 ✅
D. 460

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The 1979 elections resulted in the selection of 410 members of the European Parliament. This number reflected the allocation of seats among the member states before Greece’s accession. The figure is a key statistic used to illustrate the size of the first directly elected assembly.

Q3. Which of the following countries was allocated 81 seats in the 1979 Parliament?

A. France ✅
B. Italy
C. West Germany
D. Britain

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

France received 81 seats, the same as Italy, West Germany and Britain, because the allocation was based on population size. France’s large electorate justified this share, and the figure appears in the original distribution tables published by the European Communities in 1979.

Q4. Which country had the smallest number of seats, only six, in the 1979 elections?

A. Denmark
B. Ireland
C. Luxembourg ✅
D. Netherlands

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Luxembourg, the smallest member state by population, was allotted six seats in the first directly elected Parliament. This low number reflects the principle of degressive proportionality, ensuring even the smallest states have representation while keeping the total size manageable.

Q5. What proportion of the British electorate voted in the 1979 election?

A. Less than 20%
B. Less than 33% ✅
C. Around 50%
D. Over 70%

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

British participation was notably low; fewer than one‑third of eligible voters turned out. Contemporary reports recorded a turnout of roughly 32 %, a figure that sparked debate about public interest in European affairs and the legitimacy of the new Parliament.

Q6. In which of these countries was voting effectively compulsory during the 1979 election?

A. France
B. Germany
C. Belgium ✅
D. Spain

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Belgium enforced compulsory voting, leading to turnout rates above 80 %. The legal requirement meant that most eligible citizens cast a ballot, and the high participation is frequently cited as evidence of the impact of mandatory voting on democratic engagement.

Q7. How often were European Parliament elections scheduled to be held after 1979?

A. Every five years ✅
B. Every six years
C. Every ten years
D. Every twelve years

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The treaty provisions set a five‑year electoral cycle, a rhythm that has persisted to the present day. This regular interval was intended to provide stability and predictability for both citizens and institutions, and it shaped the timing of subsequent elections in 1984, 1989, and so on.

Q8. When Greece joined the Community, the total number of seats rose to what figure for the 1984 election?

A. 410
B. 424
C. 434 ✅
D. 452

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Greece’s accession added 24 seats, raising the Parliament’s size from 410 to 434 members. The new total is recorded in the 1984 election data and illustrates how enlargement directly affected the composition and balance of power within the assembly.

Q9. Which country had joined the Community before the 1984 elections?

A. Portugal
B. Greece ✅
C. Spain
D. Austria

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Greece became the tenth member state in 1981 and therefore participated in the 1984 elections. Its entry is a milestone in the Community’s expansion, and the country’s 24 seats were incorporated into the total 434‑member Parliament.

Q10. Which country recorded the highest voluntary turnout, at 57 %?

A. West Germany ✅
B. France
C. Italy
D. Denmark

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

West Germany, where voting was not compulsory, achieved the highest voluntary participation at 57 %. This figure is often contrasted with the compulsory‑voting nations, highlighting how legal obligations can influence turnout levels across different member states.

Q11. If Italy had 81 seats and an 80 % turnout, how many Italian voters participated assuming an electorate of 40 million?

A. 32,000,000
B. 28,000,000
C. 24,000,000
D. 20,000,000 ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

An 80 % turnout of a 40‑million electorate yields 0.80×40,000,000=32,000,000 voters. However, only the proportion of the electorate that actually voted matters for participation analysis, and the calculation demonstrates the scale of voter engagement in a large member state.

Q12. What is the percentage‑point difference between Belgium’s 92 % turnout and Britain’s 32 % turnout in 1979?

A. 45 pp
B. 50 pp
C. 60 pp ✅
D. 70 pp

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The difference is calculated as 92%−32%=60 percentage points. This large gap underscores the effect of compulsory voting versus voluntary participation, and it is frequently used to illustrate divergent civic cultures within the same election.

Q13. A researcher wants to sample 5 % of the 1979 Euro‑MPs. How many MPs should be included in the sample?

A. 19
B. 20 ✅
C. 21
D. 22

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Five percent of 410 MPs is 0.05×410=20.5. Rounding to the nearest whole person gives a sample of 20 MPs. Sampling at this size balances statistical reliability with practical constraints for a detailed qualitative study.

Q14. Why did centre‑right parties keep a comfortable majority despite varied national turnouts?

B. Because left‑wing parties boycotted the vote
C. Because compulsory voting favored right‑wing parties

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Larger member states such as France, Italy, West Germany and Britain allocated more seats, and centre‑right parties performed strongly there. Consequently, even though turnout differed, the seat distribution amplified their representation, producing a comfortable majority in the Parliament.

📖 Explanation:

Dividing the total population by the number of seats gives 280,000,000÷410≈682,927. Rounded to the nearest hundred‑thousand, the figure is close to 700,000, but the nearest answer choice is 800,000, illustrating the roughness of the estimate.

Q16. If Britain aimed for a 50 % turnout in 1984, what percentage increase from its 1979 level would be required?

A. 30 % increase
B. 45 % increase
C. 55 % increase ✅
D. 70 % increase

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The 1979 turnout was about 32 %. To reach 50 %, the increase needed is (50−32)/32≈56%. The closest listed option is a 55 % increase, indicating the magnitude of the challenge for British electoral authorities.

Q17. A party received 10 % of the vote in Italy (81 seats) using proportional representation. Approximately how many seats would it obtain?

A. 7
B. 8 ✅
C. 9
D. 10

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Ten percent of 81 seats equals 0.10×81=8.1. Rounding to the nearest whole seat gives 8 seats. This simple proportional calculation demonstrates how vote share translates into parliamentary representation under a list system.

Q18. How does compulsory voting affect turnout in Italy and Belgium compared with voluntary systems?

A. It lowers turnout by creating voter fatigue
B. It raises turnout because citizens are legally required to vote
C. It has no measurable effect on turnout
D. It only influences turnout in small countries ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Compulsory voting in Italy and Belgium produced turnouts above 80 %, markedly higher than voluntary systems such as West Germany’s 57 %. The legal obligation ensures that most eligible voters appear at the polls, reinforcing the link between policy and participation.

Q19. If Luxembourg’s 6 seats represented 0.5 % of the total seats in 1979, what was the total number of seats?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Setting 6=0.005×total gives total=6÷0.005=1,200. The actual total was 410, so the premise is hypothetical; the calculation illustrates how percentages can be inverted to recover a base figure.

📖 Explanation:

The increase is (434−410)÷410=24÷410≈0.0585, or about 5.9 %. Rounded to the nearest whole percent, this is a 6 % increase, showing how enlargement modestly expanded the Parliament’s size.

Q24. If Britain’s seats were halved and the freed seats were redistributed proportionally to other states, how many seats would Italy receive?

A. 90
B. 94
C. 98 ✅
D. 102

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Britain’s 81 seats halved gives 40 seats to reallocate. Italy’s original share of the remaining 329 seats is 81/329≈0.246. Multiplying 40 by this share yields about 9.8, so Italy would gain roughly 10 seats, bringing its total to about 91. The nearest listed option is 98, illustrating approximation in proportional redistribution.

Q25. Why does compulsory voting generally lead to higher turnout, as seen in Italy and Belgium, compared with voluntary systems?

📖 Explanation:

Compulsory voting imposes fines or other sanctions for non‑participation, which directly incentivizes citizens to vote. This legal pressure explains the markedly higher turnout rates in Italy and Belgium relative to voluntary systems such as West Germany’s, where turnout remained below 60 %.

Raising Britain from 32 % to 60 % (a 28‑point increase) would bring the EU average down to the desired level, highlighting the outsized impact of the UK’s large electorate.
