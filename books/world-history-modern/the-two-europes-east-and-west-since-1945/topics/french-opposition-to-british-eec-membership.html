📝 All French opposition to British EEC membership MCQs

Q1. What year was the Treaty of Rome, which established the EEC, signed?

🔹 A. \(1955\)
🔹 B. \(1956\)
🔹 C. \(1958\)
🔹 D. \(1957\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The Treaty of Rome was concluded in \(1957\), creating the European Economic Community. This landmark agreement laid the foundation for a customs union and common market among the six founding members, marking a decisive step toward deeper European integration.

Q2. Which three countries formed the original Benelux Customs Union?

🔹 A. France, Germany, Italy
🔹 B. Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg
🔹 C. United Kingdom, France, Belgium
🔹 D. Germany, Netherlands, Luxembourg

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Benelux Customs Union was created by Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg after World War II. Their cooperation aimed to eliminate tariffs and customs barriers, fostering free trade among the three neighboring states and serving as a model for later European integration.

Q3. Who was the Belgian socialist leader that drove the creation of Benelux?

🔹 A. Paul‑Henri Spaak
🔹 B. Robert Schuman
🔹 C. Charles de Gaulle
🔹 D. Harold Macmillan

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Paul‑Henri Spaak, a prominent Belgian socialist and prime minister (1947‑1949), championed the Benelux Customs Union. His vision for economic cooperation without tariffs helped lay the groundwork for broader European projects, including the later European Economic Community.

Q4. Which treaty in \(1948\) brought Britain into the European defence and economic collaboration?

🔹 A. Treaty of Rome
🔹 B. Treaty of Paris
🔹 C. Treaty of Brussels
🔹 D. Treaty of Maastricht

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The 1948 Treaty of Brussels extended cooperation to Britain, France, and the three Benelux states. While its defence provisions eventually evolved into NATO, the treaty also set the stage for economic collaboration that later manifested in the European Coal and Steel Community and the EEC.

Q5. The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) was founded in which year?

🔹 A. \(1949\)
🔹 B. \(1950\)
🔹 C. \(1952\)
🔹 D. \(1951\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The ECSC was established in \(1951\) as a pioneering supranational institution. It removed trade barriers on coal and steel among six founding members, fostering economic integration and laying a structural foundation for the later European Economic Community.

Q6. Why did Britain initially refuse to join the ECSC?

🔹 A. To avoid competition in agriculture
🔹 B. Concern over losing control of its steel industry
🔹 C. Preference for NATO only
🔹 D. Desire to join the Warsaw Pact

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Britain feared that joining the ECSC would require ceding authority over its steel sector to a supranational High Authority. This perceived loss of sovereignty over a key industry led the United Kingdom to decline participation at that stage.

Q7. Which French minister is credited with proposing the ECSC?

🔹 A. Robert Schuman
🔹 B. Michel Debré
🔹 C. Georges Pompidou
🔹 D. François Mitterrand

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Robert Schuman, serving as France’s Foreign Minister from \(1948\) to \(1953\), authored the Schuman Plan that became the ECSC. His vision of integrating coal and steel production aimed to bind former adversaries together and prevent future wars through shared economic interests.

Q8. How many founding members did the Treaty of Rome have?

🔹 A. Five
🔹 B. Seven
🔹 C. Six
🔹 D. Eight

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The Treaty of Rome was signed by six founding members: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. These countries established the European Economic Community, committing to a customs union, a common market, and coordinated economic policies.

Q9. Which of the following was NOT a founding member of the original EEC?

🔹 A. Italy
🔹 B. West Germany
🔹 C. Belgium
🔹 D. United Kingdom

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The United Kingdom was not among the six original signatories of the Treaty of Rome. While Britain later sought membership, its accession was repeatedly blocked by France and other members until the early 1970s, long after the EEC’s establishment.

Q10. What was the main economic goal of the Benelux Customs Union?

🔹 A. Create a common currency
🔹 B. Eliminate tariffs and customs barriers
🔹 C. Establish a joint military
🔹 D. Standardize education

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Benelux Customs Union aimed to remove tariffs and other customs obstacles among Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. By facilitating free trade, the union sought to boost post‑war economic recovery, promote industrial specialization, and provide a practical example for future European integration efforts.

Q11. If the French feared that British entry would shift the balance of power within the EEC, which of the following rationales best explains their opposition?

🔹 A. Concern that Britain’s larger economy would dominate decision‑making
🔹 B. Desire to protect French agricultural subsidies
🔹 C. Preference for a German‑led community
🔹 D. Opposition to any enlargement

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

French leaders worried that Britain’s sizable economy and global trade links could give it disproportionate influence over EEC decisions. This concern about a shift in voting weight and agenda‑setting power underpinned much of the resistance to British accession during the early 1960s.

Q12. Analyze how the French view of British economic policy influenced their stance on EEC membership.

🔹 A. Belief that Britain favored protectionism
🔹 B. Perception that Britain would adopt the French franc
🔹 C. View that Britain’s Commonwealth ties would dilute the common market
🔹 D. Expectation that Britain would push for a customs union with the US

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

French officials perceived Britain’s extensive Commonwealth trade relationships as a barrier to a uniform external tariff. They feared that allowing the UK to retain preferential treatment for Commonwealth goods would undermine the principle of a single market, thereby weakening the cohesion of the EEC.

Q13. Consider the political climate of the late 1950s. Which scenario most likely heightened French resistance to British entry?

🔹 A. Growing Franco‑German cooperation
🔹 B. Decline of colonial empires
🔹 C. Rise of European integration enthusiasm
🔹 D. Fear of Anglo‑American influence undermining French leadership

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

During the late 1950s, France was keen to assert its leadership in Europe. The prospect of Britain serving as a conduit for Anglo‑American interests raised alarms that French influence could be eclipsed, prompting heightened resistance to any British move toward EEC membership.

Q14. Given that the Treaty of Brussels included both military and economic collaboration, why might France have prioritized economic objections to Britain’s EEC entry?

🔹 A. Because military cooperation was already secure through NATO
🔹 B. Because economic integration was the core of the community’s purpose
🔹 C. Because France sought to limit British influence in agriculture
🔹 D. Because France wanted to keep the community exclusive

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Treaty of Brussels laid the groundwork for military alignment via NATO, but the economic dimension—creating a common market—remained central to the European project. Consequently, French leaders focused on economic objections, fearing that British entry could compromise the integrity of this emerging market.

Q15. Suppose the EEC had adopted a common external tariff in \(1958\). How would British participation have altered the tariff structure?

🔹 A. It would have required Britain to align its own tariffs, potentially raising prices on British imports
🔹 B. It would have forced Britain to lower its tariffs on all goods
🔹 C. It would have eliminated tariffs for Britain only
🔹 D. It would have had no effect because Britain was outside the EEC

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

If Britain joined after a common external tariff was set, it would need to conform its own rates to the EEC schedule. This alignment could raise the cost of British imports into the community, creating a competitive disadvantage for British exporters relative to existing members.

Q16. Evaluate the impact of the ECSC’s removal of duties on coal and steel on French industrial policy. Which outcome would the French likely have feared if Britain joined?

🔹 A. Strengthening of French steel subsidies
🔹 B. Increased French control over coal mines
🔹 C. Competition from British steel producers reducing French market share
🔹 D. Consolidation of French‑German coal production

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

British steel producers, with larger output and modern facilities, could out‑compete French firms once duties were abolished. French policymakers would therefore worry that British entry would erode domestic market share, pressuring France to either subsidize its industry or risk a decline in production.

Q17. In a diplomatic exchange, French officials warned that British entry could 'dilute the European spirit.' Which of the following best captures the underlying strategic concern?

🔹 A. Loss of French cultural influence
🔹 B. Threat to French linguistic dominance
🔹 C. Economic competition in agriculture
🔹 D. Shift of political leadership toward London

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

French leaders feared that a British presence could shift the balance of decision‑making toward London, thereby weakening the Franco‑German core that had driven early integration. This perceived loss of political leadership was a central element of the broader strategic resistance.

Q18. If Britain had joined the EEC in the early 1960s, how might the French position on the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) have been affected?

🔹 A. France would have abandoned CAP
🔹 B. France would have negotiated more favorable subsidies to offset British agricultural imports
🔹 C. France would have reduced subsidies to accommodate British interests
🔹 D. France would have pushed for a joint fishing policy instead

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

British agricultural imports would have intensified competition for French farmers. To maintain domestic support, France likely would have sought higher CAP subsidies, ensuring that French producers remained competitive within the enlarged market and mitigating political backlash at home.

Q19. A French minister argues that British entry would increase the need for a common currency. Which logical flaw does this represent?

🔹 A. Assuming monetary union follows membership automatically
🔹 B. Overestimating British willingness to adopt the franc
🔹 C. Ignoring the role of the United States
🔹 D. Confusing trade policy with monetary policy

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The minister incorrectly assumes that joining the EEC entails immediate adoption of a common currency. In reality, monetary integration was a separate, later step (the euro), and members could retain their own currencies for decades after accession, making the argument logically flawed.

Q20. Compare the French opposition to British entry with the earlier French stance toward German re‑integration. What key difference distinguishes the two cases?

🔹 D. French opposition was identical in both cases

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

While France eventually embraced German re‑integration to secure peace and economic recovery, its resistance to Britain stemmed from concerns that British political and economic clout would dominate the community, revealing a distinct apprehension about external influence versus internal reconciliation.

Q21. A historian suggests that the French opposition was partly motivated by domestic politics. Which internal factor would most plausibly support this claim?

🔹 D. Pressure from French agricultural lobby fearing competition

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The French agricultural lobby, protective of its subsidies and market share, exerted significant pressure on policymakers. Fear that British agricultural imports would undermine French farmers’ profitability provided a domestic political incentive for French leaders to block British accession to the EEC.

Q22. Using game‑theoretic reasoning, model the interaction between France and Britain over EEC entry as a sequential game. Which equilibrium outcome best reflects the historical stalemate?

🔹 A. Both cooperate and Britain joins
🔹 B. France blocks entry, Britain withdraws, leading to a Nash equilibrium of non‑entry
🔹 C. Britain joins despite French opposition, causing a payoff reduction for France
🔹 D. Both accept a compromise tariff schedule

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

In a sequential game, France moves first by threatening to veto British entry; Britain, anticipating a likely block, chooses to withdraw its application. This results in a Nash equilibrium where neither side gains by deviating, mirroring the real‑world deadlock that persisted until the early 1970s.

Q23. Assess the long‑term economic consequences for France if Britain had been admitted to the EEC in \(1961\). Which projection aligns with counterfactual analyses?

🔹 A. French manufacturing would have faced heightened competition, reducing output by roughly \(10\%\)

🔹 C. French services sector would have expanded due to British investment
🔹 D. No significant change would have occurred

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Economic modelling suggests that British industrial capacity, especially in steel and manufacturing, would have intensified competition for French firms. This pressure could have lowered French output by about \(10\%\), prompting structural adjustments and highlighting the validity of French concerns about losing market share.

Q24. Critically examine the argument that British Commonwealth ties would have conflicted with EEC customs policy. Which statement most accurately captures the tension?

🔹 A. Commonwealth trade was already aligned with EEC tariffs
🔹 B. The Commonwealth imposed no customs duties
🔹 C. British preferential treatment of Commonwealth goods would have required exemptions, undermining the EEC’s uniform external tariff
🔹 D. The EEC intended to incorporate Commonwealth trade automatically

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The EEC’s principle of a single external tariff meant that all members applied identical duties to non‑members. Britain’s desire to retain preferential rates for Commonwealth nations would have necessitated special exemptions, directly contradicting the uniformity essential to the common market, thereby creating a policy clash.

🔹 D. Later enlargements would have emphasized stricter accession criteria to avoid similar opposition

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Having blocked Britain, French policymakers would likely have instituted more rigorous accession standards to prevent future challenges to their influence. This would have shaped later enlargement rounds, ensuring that new members met tighter political and economic conditions before joining the community.

Q26. Imagine a scenario where Britain negotiates a 'special partnership' allowing it to retain control over its steel industry while participating in the EEC. Which legal principle would this arrangement most likely violate?

🔹 A. Principle of subsidiarity
🔹 B. Principle of free movement of goods and services
🔹 C. Principle of monetary sovereignty
🔹 D. Principle of democratic accountability

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The EEC’s core principle of free movement of goods and services requires that all members treat each other’s products without discrimination. Allowing Britain to keep exclusive control over its steel sector would create a barrier, contravening the free‑movement rule and undermining the internal market’s integrity.

Q30. If the EEC had adopted a common external tariff of \(10\%\) on steel imports, what would have been the immediate effect on British‑French trade flows?

A uniform 10 % external tariff would apply to all non‑member steel, including British products. Consequently, British exporters would encounter an additional cost when selling to France, diminishing their price advantage and potentially curbing British steel’s market share within the EEC.
