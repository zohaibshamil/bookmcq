What is Indian independence and partition background?

Indian independence emerged from decades of nationalist activism led by the Indian National Congress and Muslim League against British rule, but deepening religious tensions between Hindus and Muslims made a unified post-colonial state increasingly difficult to achieve.

Q1. The British foreign secretary Ernest Bevin considered delaying Indian independence to fund a development programme. Which combination of reasons most directly led to dropping this idea?

A. Only economic difficulties faced by Britain
B. Only Indian suspicion of any delay
C. Both economic difficulties and Indian suspicion ✅
D. Neither reason

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The correct answer is C because the text states that Britain could not afford the expense and Indians would be suspicious of any postponement. Option A ignores the Indian reaction, while B ignores Britain’s financial strain. D is wrong because both reasons were decisive. This shows that practical economics and political trust together forced the British to abandon the delay, illustrating how multiple constraints shape policy decisions.

Q2. If the Labour Party had lost the 1945 election, how likely would it have been that Indian independence would occur before 1950?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Option B is correct: the Conservatives were less inclined to grant independence quickly, preferring to maintain imperial control, whereas Labour was motivated by moral and economic reasons. Option A overstates the consensus; many parties were not uniformly in favour of rapid decolonisation. Option C ignores the significant influence of the ruling party on policy. Option D is extreme; even Conservatives eventually faced pressure. This scenario highlights the political contingency of decolonisation.

Q3. Why was Dominion status different from full independence for India after World War II?

Option B is false because Dominion countries still engaged in trade with Britain. Option C misstates the division of powers; legislative authority remained with Indian representatives. Option D confuses international representation with constitutional status. Understanding this nuance shows how the British offered a symbolic link to retain influence while granting substantial autonomy.
