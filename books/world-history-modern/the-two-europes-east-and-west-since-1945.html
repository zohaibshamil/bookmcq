📖 The Two Europes: East and West since 1945

Chapter Overview:

Since 1945, Europe developed along sharply different political and economic paths. In the West, France, West Germany, and Italy rebuilt democratic institutions and expanded cooperation from the European Coal and Steel Community to the European Union. In the East, Soviet-backed communist governments operated through Comecon and the Warsaw Pact, while uprisings exposed tensions within the bloc. The revolutions of 1989, German reunification, and Yugoslav disintegration transformed the continent, while successive European treaties deepened integration and raised questions about membership, sovereignty, and shared policies.

Background Knowledge Needed:

Students should understand the division of Europe following the Second World War, the occupation of Germany, the emergence of American and Soviet influence, and the establishment of communist governments across Eastern Europe. Knowledge of the Marshall Plan, NATO, the Soviet-led Warsaw Pact, and Comecon provides essential background. Familiarity with postwar French political instability, the establishment of the Federal Republic of Germany in 1949, the Italian Republic in 1946, and the Cold War rivalry explains the contrasting political and economic systems. Earlier knowledge of European economic cooperation and the decline of Soviet authority is also important.

Learning Outcomes:

Students should be able to compare political and economic developments in Western and Eastern Europe after 1945, explain the evolution of European cooperation from the original six EEC members to the European Union, and analyse British participation and opposition to integration. They should examine Eastern European uprisings, explain the collapse of communist governments, evaluate the causes of German reunification, and distinguish the Yugoslav conflicts from the peaceful revolutions elsewhere. They should also assess the significance of the Maastricht and Nice Treaties and identify continuing challenges to European integration.

Periods and Themes Covered:

  • Western European Reconstruction, 1945–1960 - French republics, West German recovery, Italian democracy, and cooperation.
  • Eastern Bloc Consolidation, 1949–1968 - Comecon, Warsaw Pact, Poznań protests, Hungarian Revolution, and Prague Spring.
  • Early Western European Integration - Economic cooperation, European Community institutions, and British hesitation towards membership.
  • EEC Enlargement and Policy Development, 1973–1986 - British accession, Lomé Convention, direct elections, CAP, ERM, and budget disputes.
  • Deepening European Integration, 1986–1993 - Single European Act, Maastricht negotiations, and establishment of the European Union.
  • Eastern European Revolutions, 1980–1991 - Solidarity, communist collapse, Gorbachev's reforms, and German reunification.
  • Post-Communist Europe and EU Reform, 1991–2001 - Yugoslav conflicts, Eastern European transitions, and Treaty of Nice negotiations.

Suggested Study Sequence:

Begin with the political reconstruction of France, West Germany, and Italy after 1945, comparing their institutions and economic recovery. Next, examine Soviet control of Eastern Europe, the creation of Comecon and the Warsaw Pact, and the uprisings in Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia. Study the origins of Western European integration before tracing the development of the EEC, British membership negotiations, and subsequent enlargement. Continue with the Single European Act, Maastricht Treaty, and European Union institutions. Then examine Solidarity, Gorbachev's reforms, the revolutions of 1989, and German reunification. Finish with the Yugoslav wars, the Nice Treaty, and future questions concerning European integration.

How the Developments Connect:

The postwar division of Europe produced separate political and economic systems: Western states pursued reconstruction and integration, while Soviet control organised the East through Comecon and the Warsaw Pact. Uprisings in 1956 and 1968 exposed tensions but did not end communist control. Economic stagnation, Solidarity, Gorbachev's reforms, and the revolutions of 1989 enabled political transitions and German reunification. Yugoslavia's breakup followed a different and violent path.

Key Events Timeline:

In August 1980, workers at the Gdańsk shipyard founded Solidarity under Lech Wałęsa, challenging communist rule in Poland. Martial law followed in December 1981, but negotiations resumed in 1989. In the semi-free elections of 4 June 1989, Solidarity won most contested seats; Tadeusz Mazowiecki became prime minister in August. Hungary opened its Austrian border in September, the Berlin Wall fell on 9 November, and Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution ended communist rule by December. East and West Germany reunified on 3 October 1990.

Why This Chapter Matters:

The contrasting experiences of Eastern and Western Europe explain the origins of modern European political cooperation, the collapse of Soviet-controlled communist governments, and the emergence of new independent states. These developments are essential for understanding European integration, post-communist conflicts, German reunification, EU enlargement, and the changing political and economic relationships within Europe.

Common Confusions in History:

'The EEC and EU began in 1957' confuses the Rome Treaty, effective in 1958, with the EU established by Maastricht, effective in 1993. 'Britain joined in 1975' confuses its 1973 EEC accession with the 1975 membership referendum. 'The Velvet Revolution was Yugoslav' confuses Czechoslovakia's peaceful 1989 transition with Yugoslavia's separate breakup and wars.

Exam Focus Areas:

Important questions include matching European treaties with their dates, purposes, and consequences; explaining British hesitation towards EEC membership; and identifying the stages of European enlargement. Chronology questions examine the Poznań protests, Hungarian Revolution, Prague Spring, Solidarity, and German reunification. Comparative questions address Comecon and the EEC, while analytical questions examine Gorbachev's reforms, Yugoslav conflicts, the Maastricht Treaty, and the institutional changes introduced by the Treaty of Nice.

Chapter Summary:

Postwar Europe developed into two contrasting political systems, with Western democracies pursuing economic integration and Eastern communist governments remaining under Soviet influence. Repeated uprisings eventually gave way to peaceful political transitions across much of Eastern Europe. German reunification symbolised the end of continental division, although Yugoslav conflicts demonstrated continuing instability. Meanwhile, European integration advanced through successive treaties, creating a larger and more politically connected European Union.

Key Terms and Figures:

  • Comecon - The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, established in 1949 to coordinate economic cooperation among Soviet-aligned states.
  • Warsaw Pact - The Soviet-led military alliance established in 1955 involving communist states of Eastern and Central Europe.
  • European Economic Community - The organisation established by the 1957 Treaty of Rome to promote economic integration among six Western European countries.
  • Common Agricultural Policy - The European Community's shared agricultural policy, introduced in 1962 to support agricultural markets and farmers.
  • European Exchange Rate Mechanism - The 1979 monetary arrangement designed to reduce exchange-rate fluctuations among participating European currencies.
  • Lech Wałęsa - The leader of the Polish Solidarity movement who later became president of Poland.
  • Velvet Revolution - The peaceful popular movement that ended communist rule in Czechoslovakia during November and December 1989.
  • Maastricht Treaty - The treaty signed in February 1992 that established the European Union and expanded cooperation beyond economic integration.

Further Research:

  • Historical Archives of the European Union, Florence - Explore original European integration documents, founding treaties, institutional records, speeches, and correspondence concerning the development of the European Community.
  • European Parliament Historical Archives - Investigate the first direct elections of 1979, parliamentary expansion, institutional reforms, and debates surrounding British membership and European integration.
  • UK National Archives - Search British Cabinet papers, diplomatic correspondence, parliamentary records, and documents concerning EEC accession, budget contributions, and the 1984 rebate agreement.
  • Polish Solidarity Historical Collections - Examine original trade union documents, Gdańsk agreements of August 1980, martial law records, and negotiations preceding the 1989 elections.
  • Wilson Center Digital Archive - Research Soviet policies towards Eastern Europe, the Hungarian Revolution, Prague Spring, Gorbachev's reforms, and diplomatic records surrounding the collapse of communism.
  • German Federal Archives - Explore records concerning the Berlin Wall, East German political developments, the fall of communism, and German reunification in 1990.
  • International Criminal Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia Archives - Investigate original court judgments, witness testimony, documentary evidence, and historical records concerning the Croatian War, Bosnian War, and Kosovo conflict.
  • European Union Treaty Collections - Compare the original Treaty of Rome, Single European Act, Maastricht Treaty, and Treaty of Nice to understand the changing powers, membership, and institutional structure of European cooperation.
