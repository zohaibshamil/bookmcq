📖 The First World War and its aftermath

The First World War: Campaigns, Stalemate and Peace Settlements

Building on the pre-war alliance and mobilisation crisis, the war moved from opening campaigns in 1914 to the armistice of 1918. Hopes of rapid victory gave way to attritional stalemate on the Western and Eastern Fronts, while Allied blockade and German submarine warfare widened the struggle at sea. Russia's withdrawal and the United States' entry shifted the balance of power. The collapse of the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman Empires then produced contested treaties, new borders, reparations, and disputes over the peace settlement.

Background to the 1914–1918 War: Alliances and Military Plans

Students should understand the European alliance systems, the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the July Crisis of 1914, and the mobilisation decisions that brought the major powers into war. Knowledge of the Schlieffen Plan, German military strategy, British naval supremacy, Austro-Russian rivalry, and the geographical positions of Germany, France, Russia, Belgium, and Austria-Hungary is essential. Familiarity with the Triple Entente, Triple Alliance, and declining Ottoman power also helps explain the military and political developments between 1914 and 1920.

Learning Outcomes: Fronts, Naval Warfare and the 1919 Treaties

Students should be able to compare military developments on the Western and Eastern Fronts, explain the causes and consequences of trench warfare, and analyse the importance of naval warfare, the Battle of Jutland, and unrestricted submarine warfare. They should explain why the United States entered the war, how Russia withdrew, and why Germany failed in 1918. They should also compare the major peace treaties, assess Allied treatment of defeated powers, and analyse German objections to the Treaty of Versailles.

War Phases: From the Marne and Tannenberg to the Armistice

  • Opening Campaigns, 1914 - German advance west and Russian-German clashes in East Prussia.
  • Trench Stalemate, 1915–1916 - Attrition, Verdun, the Somme, and Brusilov's eastern offensive.
  • Naval Warfare, 1914–1918 - Allied maritime supremacy, blockade, mines, U-boats, and Jutland.
  • The 1917 Turning Point - Submarine escalation, American entry, and Russia's withdrawal from war.
  • The 1918 Campaigns - German Spring Offensive, Allied counterattack, and the armistice.
  • Consequences and Peace Aims - Human losses, economic disruption, revolutions, and competing Allied objectives.
  • Paris Peace Settlements, 1919–1920 - Versailles and treaties imposing territorial and military conditions on defeated states.

Study Sequence: Major Fronts, Turning Points and Peace

Begin with the opening campaigns of 1914, examining Germany's advance into Belgium and France, the First Battle of the Marne, and the fighting against Russia in East Prussia. Continue with the development of trench warfare, the Western Front campaigns of 1915–1916, and Russian operations in the East. Next, study Allied naval operations, the blockade, submarine attacks, and Jutland. Move to the strategic changes of 1917, including American intervention and Russian withdrawal. Finish with the 1918 offensives, the armistice, wartime consequences, Allied peace objectives, and the separate treaties imposed on defeated states.

How Trench Stalemate, Submarine Warfare and US Entry Interacted

Early German advances failed to secure quick victories: the Marne checked the westward drive, while Tannenberg and later campaigns shaped the unstable eastern front. Trench stalemate increased reliance on artillery, blockade, and naval warfare. Jutland did not break British sea control; unrestricted U-boat attacks helped bring the United States into the war. Russia's exit freed German forces for 1918, but Allied manpower and coordinated offensives reversed the balance before the armistice.

Timeline: Major Battles, US Entry and the 1918 Armistice

On 26–30 August 1914, Germany defeated Russia at Tannenberg in East Prussia, while the First Battle of the Marne (6–12 September) stopped the German advance in France. At Jutland in the North Sea (31 May–1 June 1916), neither fleet won decisive control. Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February 1917; the United States declared war on 6 April. After the Spring Offensive began on 21 March 1918, Allied forces struck at Amiens on 8 August; the armistice took effect on 11 November.

Why the War and Versailles Settlement Reshaped Europe

Battlefield attrition, blockade, and unrestricted submarine warfare demonstrate how industrial warfare affected armies, economies, and civilians beyond the trenches. Russian withdrawal and American entry explain the changing balance in 1917–1918. The peace treaties reshaped international borders and created political grievances, making their terms essential for understanding interwar instability, German opposition, and later European diplomacy.

Common Misconceptions: Jutland, Brest-Litovsk and Sèvres

'Jutland was a decisive British fleet victory' ignores that Germany withdrew its fleet, but Britain retained control of the North Sea. 'Russia fought until the 1918 armistice' confuses its withdrawal from the conflict; the Bolshevik government signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918. 'The Treaty of Sèvres founded modern Turkey' overlooks its rejection by Turkish nationalists and replacement by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923.

Exam Focus: Fronts, Turning Points and Peace Treaties

Expect chronology questions on the Marne, Tannenberg, Jutland, submarine warfare, American entry, and the 1918 offensives. Comparative questions frequently examine the Western and Eastern Fronts and the effectiveness of naval strategies. Treaty questions may require matching Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Trianon, and Sèvres to defeated states, identifying their major conditions, explaining Allied peace objectives, and assessing German objections to the post-war settlement.

Summary: Military Defeat and the Postwar Settlement

Military stalemate on land and the struggle for control of the seas exhausted the combatants. Russian withdrawal and American intervention altered the strategic balance, while Germany's 1918 offensives failed to prevent Allied advances. The armistice ended the fighting, but imperial collapse and separate peace treaties transformed international borders, imposed political and economic obligations, and left disputes that influenced European relations throughout the interwar years.

Key Battles, Commanders and Peace Treaties of 1914–1920

  • First Battle of the Marne - The September 1914 Allied victory that stopped the German advance towards Paris.
  • Battle of Tannenberg - The August 1914 German victory over the Russian Second Army in East Prussia.
  • Battle of Jutland - The major naval engagement between British and German fleets in the North Sea in 1916.
  • Unrestricted Submarine Warfare - Germany's policy of attacking enemy and neutral merchant shipping without warning in designated waters.
  • Treaty of Brest-Litovsk - The March 1918 peace agreement through which Bolshevik Russia withdrew from the war against the Central Powers.
  • Hundred Days Offensive - The series of Allied offensives beginning in August 1918 that forced German forces into retreat.
  • David Lloyd George - British Prime Minister from December 1916 who represented Britain at the Paris Peace Conference.
  • Article 231 - The Treaty of Versailles provision establishing Germany and its allies' responsibility for war-related losses and damage.

Primary Records on World War I Campaigns and Peace Treaties

  • Treaty of Versailles, 1919 - Examine the complete treaty text, particularly Article 231, military restrictions, territorial provisions, and reparations, through the Avalon Project at Yale Law School.
  • British National Archives - Investigate War Cabinet minutes, Foreign Office correspondence, naval blockade records, and David Lloyd George's wartime government.
  • Imperial War Museums Collections - Explore original soldiers' diaries, battlefield photographs, naval records, trench warfare documents, and contemporary accounts of civilian experiences.
  • Library of Congress World War I Collections - Search official British war photographs, military records, propaganda materials, and documents concerning American intervention.
  • Paris Peace Conference Documents - Compare the original peace treaties, Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, Allied diplomatic correspondence, and records concerning the establishment of the League of Nations.
  • Treaty of Sèvres and Treaty of Lausanne - Examine the Turkish National Movement, the rejection of the 1920 settlement, and the diplomatic negotiations that produced the 1923 Lausanne agreement.
