Q1. In which year did the world population first exceed seven billion people?

🔹 A. 2005
🔹 B. 2011
🔹 C. 2015
🔹 D. 2020

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The correct answer is 2011 because that is the calendar year when the global total passed the seven‑billion mark. The other years are either too early or too late; 2005 and 2015 did not reach that threshold, and 2020 is well after the milestone. Knowing the exact year helps students relate demographic milestones to policy planning, such as when governments began to address sustainability concerns.

Q2. What was the average annual growth rate of the world population between 1850 and 1900?

🔹 A. 0.005
🔹 B. 0.006999999999999999
🔹 C. 0.008
🔹 D. 0.006

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The growth rate of \(0.6\%\) per year is correct for that period, reflecting a modest increase before the modern explosion. The other percentages are higher than the historical average and would imply a faster rise than actually occurred. Understanding this baseline rate lets students see how later periods accelerated, which is essential when comparing historic and current demographic trends.

Q3. If a developing country has a constant growth rate of \(2\%\) per year, approximately how many years will it take for its population to double?

🔹 A. 35
🔹 B. 50
🔹 C. 70
🔹 D. 100

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Using the Rule of 70, doubling time ≈ \(70 ÷ 2 = 35\) years, so answer A is correct. The other choices overestimate the time: 50, 70, and 100 years would correspond to lower growth rates. This calculation is useful for planners estimating future resource needs, because a rapid doubling influences demands for food, housing, and services within a few decades.

Q4. A policymaker wants to cut the annual population increase from \(1.9\%\) to \(1.0\%\). By what percent must the current growth be reduced?

🔹 A. 0.45
🔹 B. 0.5
🔹 C. 0.47
🔹 D. 0.53

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The reduction needed is \((1.9-1.0)/1.9 ≈ 0.4737\), or about \(47\%\); therefore option C is correct. Options A, B, and D represent different reductions that do not match the calculation. Recognizing the required percentage cut helps officials design realistic family‑planning or education programs that target the needed decline in growth.

Q5. A country’s fertility dropped from 5 to 2 children per woman, lowering its growth rate from \(2.5\%\) to \(1.0\%\). What is the approximate percentage decrease in the growth rate?

🔹 A. 0.6
🔹 B. 0.58
🔹 C. 0.55
🔹 D. 0.5

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The percentage decrease is \((2.5-1.0)/2.5 = 0.60\), i.e., \(60\%\); thus option A is correct. The other percentages are close but not exact, reflecting common rounding errors. This illustrates how fertility changes directly affect demographic growth, a key concept for governments aiming to stabilize population size.

Q6. The world added 100 million people in 1995. What was the approximate average daily increase that year?

🔹 A. 274 000
🔹 B. 300 000
🔹 C. 350 000
🔹 D. 400 000

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Dividing 100 million by 365 days yields about \(274{,}000\) people per day, making option A correct. The other figures are higher than the true average and would imply a faster yearly increase. Knowing daily growth rates helps policymakers visualize the scale of population change and plan infrastructure accordingly.

Q7. A region grew at \(0.9\%\) per year from 1950 to 2000. If its 1950 population was 200 million, what was it in 2000?

🔹 A. 300 million
🔹 B. 314 million
🔹 C. 350 million
🔹 D. 400 million

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Using compound growth, \(200 million × (1.009)^{50} ≈ 314 million\); therefore option B is correct. The other answers either underestimate or overestimate the effect of 50 years of growth. This calculation demonstrates how modest annual increases accumulate over decades, influencing long‑term planning for resources and services.

Q8. Why did industrialized nations’ population growth slow after World War I?

🔹 A. Higher mortality rates
🔹 B. Economic downturn and lower birth rates
🔹 C. Mass emigration
🔹 D. Increased disease outbreaks

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The slowdown is mainly due to economic hardship and reduced birth rates after the war, making option B correct. Higher mortality, emigration, and disease were not the primary drivers of the decline. Understanding this helps students see how economic conditions can directly affect demographic trends.

Q9. How does civil strife in poor countries contribute to rapid population growth?

🔹 A. It raises birth rates because of instability
🔹 B. It limits access to family‑planning services
🔹 C. It attracts large numbers of migrants
🔹 D. It improves healthcare delivery

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Conflict often disrupts health services and reduces availability of contraception, so option B is correct. The other choices either misstate the effect (birth rates usually rise due to lack of planning, not directly because of instability) or are unrelated. Recognizing this link is vital for humanitarian aid strategies.

Q10. What primary factor caused the global “population explosion” after 1960?

🔹 A. Improved agricultural yields
🔹 B. A sharp decline in mortality rates
🔹 C. A sudden rise in fertility rates
🔹 D. Mass international migration

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The dramatic fall in death rates, thanks to medical advances and better sanitation, is the main cause, so option B is correct. Improved agriculture, higher fertility, and migration all affect numbers but were not the central driver of the explosion. This insight guides public‑health priorities.

Q11. How does a higher population growth rate affect resource demand in developing regions?

🔹 A. It decreases overall demand
🔹 B. It has no measurable effect
🔹 C. It increases demand proportionally
🔹 D. It improves resource distribution efficiency

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

A faster‑growing population raises the need for food, water, and energy roughly in step with the increase, making option C correct. The other options contradict basic economic principles: demand does not fall or stay unchanged, and distribution efficiency is not automatically enhanced by growth. This relationship is crucial for sustainable development planning.

Q12. Why might Latin American countries experience a slowdown in growth after 1950 while Asian nations continued to increase?

🔹 A. Earlier adoption of contraception
🔹 B. Higher mortality rates persisted
🔹 C. More frequent wars disrupted families
🔹 D. Lower fertility decline compared with Asia

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Latin America introduced family‑planning programs earlier, leading to reduced birth rates and a slowdown, so option A is correct. Higher mortality, wars, and slower fertility declines do not explain the observed pattern. Recognizing policy timing helps explain regional demographic differences.

Q13. What explains the regional variation where Europe’s population growth slowed after World War I while Africa’s accelerated after World II?

🔹 A. Industrialization and demographic transition
🔹 B. Changes in fertility rates alone
🔹 C. Large-scale migration flows
🔹 D. Differences in mortality decline rates

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Industrialized Europe had already entered the demographic transition, lowering fertility and slowing growth, whereas many African societies were still pre‑transition, leading to acceleration after WWII; thus option A is correct. The other options isolate single factors that do not capture the broader socioeconomic shift. This explanation highlights how development stage influences population dynamics.
