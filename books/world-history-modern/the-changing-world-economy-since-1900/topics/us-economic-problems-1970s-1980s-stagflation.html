📝 US economic problems 1970s 1980s stagflation (8 MCQs)

American Economic Weakness After the Postwar Boom

During the 1970s and early 1980s, the United States experienced inflation, unemployment, slower industrial growth and declining confidence in economic policy. This period challenged the prosperity associated with the postwar decades. Stagflation became particularly serious because rising prices occurred alongside weak economic growth and unemployment, making conventional policies for managing inflation and recession difficult to apply.

The Collapse of Bretton Woods and Rising Inflationary Pressure

In August 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold, effectively ending the Bretton Woods fixed-exchange-rate system. Currency instability and domestic inflationary pressures increased. Government spending, monetary expansion and international economic imbalances contributed to price instability, while American manufacturing faced growing competition from Japan and Western Europe.

Oil Shocks and the Development of Stagflation

The 1973 Arab oil embargo and the 1979 Iranian Revolution produced major disruptions in international oil supplies and sharp energy price increases. Higher production and transport costs contributed to inflation and reduced industrial activity. American businesses faced rising expenses, households experienced declining purchasing power and unemployment increased during repeated economic downturns.

Paul Volcker's Monetary Policies and the Fight Against Inflation

In 1979, Paul Volcker became chairman of the Federal Reserve and introduced restrictive monetary policies. Interest rates rose sharply to reduce inflation, contributing to severe recessions in 1980 and 1981–1982. Inflation subsequently declined, but businesses faced expensive borrowing and unemployment increased, demonstrating the difficult trade-off between price stability and economic activity.

Reaganomics, Fiscal Deficits and the Changing American Economy

President Ronald Reagan introduced tax reductions, deregulation and limits on some domestic programmes during the 1980s. Military expenditure increased, contributing to larger federal budget deficits. The decade witnessed economic recovery and financial-sector expansion, but manufacturing employment declined in several industrial regions, while income inequality and international trade imbalances became more prominent.

Misconceptions About American Stagflation

'Stagflation means falling prices during recession' - it combines inflation with weak economic growth and unemployment. 'The 1973 oil crisis was the only cause' - monetary instability and structural economic problems also mattered. 'Volcker reduced inflation without economic costs' - restrictive interest rates contributed to severe recessions.

Examining Oil Shocks, Volcker and Reaganomics

Remember the 1971 suspension of dollar convertibility, the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979, Volcker's appointment in 1979 and the recession of 1981–1982. Explain why stagflation challenged Keynesian demand management. Questions may compare monetary tightening with Reagan's supply-side policies and assess the consequences for employment, inflation and public finances.

Economic Terms of the American Crisis, 1970s–1980s

  • Stagflation - Inflation combined with economic stagnation.
  • Oil shock - Sudden international oil-price increase.
  • Federal Reserve - United States central bank.
  • Monetary tightening - Policies restricting money and credit.
  • Reaganomics - Reagan-era tax and deregulation policies.
  • Fiscal deficit - Government spending exceeding revenue.

Q1. In which year did President Nixon devalue the U.S. dollar by about \(12\%\) and impose a \(10\%\) import duty?

A.1971 ✅
B.1973
C.1975
D.1979
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is 1971 because Nixon announced the devaluation and the 10 % import surcharge that year in response to a balance‑of‑payments crisis. 1973, 1975, and 1979 are incorrect; those years saw other economic events but not the specific devaluation. Understanding the timing helps explain why the dollar lost its gold‑convertibility and why later policies had to address inflation. Recognising the date also shows how quickly the U.S. moved from post‑war stability to monetary challenges.

Q2. What was the primary economic reason that high defense spending in the 1970s and 1980s contributed to a persistent U.S. budget deficit?

A.It increased tax revenues
B.It reduced interest rates
C.It raised government expenditures beyond tax receipts ✅
D.It boosted export earnings
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Correct answer C: high defense spending raised government outlays beyond the revenue collected from taxes, creating a persistent budget gap. Option A is wrong because spending does not increase tax receipts directly; option B is incorrect because defense spending typically pushes interest rates up, not down; option D is false because defense costs do not boost exports. The link between large military budgets and deficits illustrates why the U.S. had to borrow from abroad, weakening confidence in the dollar and prompting later fiscal debates.

Q3. How did Milton Friedman's monetarist policy of tightening the money supply aim to improve business efficiency in the 1980s?

A.By lowering interest rates to encourage borrowing
B.By raising interest rates to limit excess credit ✅
C.By increasing government subsidies
D.By fixing prices of goods
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Correct answer B: Friedman's monetarism called for raising interest rates to restrict the growth of credit, forcing firms to become more efficient with tighter financing. Option A is opposite, as lower rates would encourage borrowing; option C is unrelated because subsidies are fiscal, not monetary; option D misstates price controls, which Friedman opposed. By curbing the money supply, the policy aimed to reduce inflation and compel businesses to cut waste, a strategy later echoed by Thatcher in Britain, showing the ideological spread of monetarist ideas.

Q4. If the U.S. had reduced defense spending by \(20\%\) in 1982 while keeping other expenditures constant, which immediate effect on the budget deficit would be most likely?

A.Deficit would increase
B.Deficit would stay the same
C.Deficit would decrease ✅
D.Deficit would be eliminated
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Correct answer C: Reducing defense spending by \(20\%\) would lower total government outlays, directly shrinking the fiscal gap if revenues stay unchanged, thus decreasing the deficit. Option A suggests the deficit would rise, which contradicts the effect of lower spending. Option B implies no change, ignoring the sizable cut. Option D claims the deficit would disappear, unrealistic because other obligations remain. This scenario highlights how targeted cuts can improve fiscal balances, a lesson relevant to policymakers debating defense budgets today.

Q5. Suppose an economist models the 1987 stock market crash as a function of declining investor confidence due to a weakening dollar. If the dollar depreciates by \(15\%\) and investor confidence index drops from 80 to 60, which policy combination would most likely stabilize the market?

A.Increase money supply and raise interest rates
B.Decrease money supply and lower interest rates
C.Increase money supply and lower interest rates ✅
D.Decrease money supply and raise interest rates
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Correct answer C: Increasing the money supply while lowering interest rates supplies cheap credit, helping to restore investor confidence after a sharp dollar depreciation and a confidence index drop. Option A mixes a higher money supply with higher rates, which would still deter borrowing; option B reduces money and raises rates, worsening liquidity; option D does both, deepening the crisis. The combination in C mirrors typical central‑bank responses to market panics, illustrating why policymakers often use expansionary monetary tools to stabilize stock markets after confidence shocks.

Q6. A small business owner in 1984 notices that high interest rates are making loans expensive. Which of the following actions would best help the business survive the monetary policy environment?

A.Take a larger loan now
B.Delay expansion and focus on cash flow ✅
C.Increase price of products dramatically
D.Lobby for tax cuts
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Correct answer B: Delaying expansion and focusing on cash flow conserves resources when borrowing costs are high, allowing the business to survive until rates fall. Option A worsens debt exposure; option C may alienate customers and reduce demand; option D relies on uncertain legislative outcomes. The choice reflects practical adaptation to tight monetary conditions, showing how firms can adjust strategies rather than confront expensive financing directly.

Q7. Why did rising oil prices in the 1970s worsen the United States' balance-of-payments deficit?

A.They increased domestic production
B.They reduced export competitiveness
C.They raised the cost of imported oil ✅
D.They lowered inflation
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Correct answer C: Rising oil prices raised the cost of imported energy, widening the trade gap because the U.S. had to spend more foreign currency on oil imports. Option A is incorrect; domestic production did not increase enough to offset imports. Option B misstates the effect on exports; higher oil costs do not directly reduce export competitiveness. Option D is false because oil price spikes tend to increase inflation, not lower it. This mechanism explains how external commodity shocks can strain a nation's balance of payments.

Q8. Imagine a country today faces a large trade deficit similar to the U.S. in the 1970s. If it chooses to impose a \(10\%\) tariff on imports, what is the most likely short‑term effect on its currency value?

A.Currency appreciates ✅
B.Currency depreciates
C.No change
D.Currency becomes unstable
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Correct answer A: Imposing a \(10\%\) tariff raises the price of foreign goods, reducing demand for imports and improving the trade balance, which tends to support the domestic currency and cause it to appreciate in the short term. Option B predicts depreciation, opposite to the expected effect of a trade‑improving measure. Option C ignores the market reaction, and option D suggests instability without basis. The scenario mirrors how modern governments use tariffs to influence exchange rates, linking trade policy to currency movements.)
