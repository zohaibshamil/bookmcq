Q1. In which year did the IMF agree to a loan of €110 billion to Greece?

🔹 A. 2008
🔹 B. 2009
🔹 C. 2010
🔹 D. 2011

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The loan was finalized in May 2010, so the correct choice is 2010. 2008 and 2009 are too early, while 2011 is after the agreement. Knowing the timing helps place the crisis within the broader European debt timeline, showing how quickly the emergency financing was needed. This context clarifies why austerity measures were imposed immediately and why public opposition grew during that period.

Q2. What was the primary economic policy Greece implemented to address its budget deficit?

🔹 A. Monetary expansion
🔹 B. Strict austerity
🔹 C. Currency devaluation
🔹 D. Increase of public spending

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Strict austerity—cuts to pensions, wages, and social services—was the primary policy Greece adopted to confront its oversized deficit. Monetary expansion or currency devaluation were not options because Greece did not control its currency; increasing public spending would worsen the deficit. By reducing government outlays, the aim was to lower the budget gap and restore creditor confidence, though it also sparked social unrest. This answer reflects the central role of austerity in the crisis response.

Q3. If a eurozone country with a debt‑to‑GDP ratio of 120 % and short‑term loans due in one year receives a €50 billion bailout, which immediate fiscal effect is most likely?

🔹 A. Increase in primary surplus
🔹 B. Reduction of unemployment
🔹 C. Immediate debt write‑off
🔹 D. Rise in inflation

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

A €50 billion bailout reduces the immediate financing gap, allowing the government to meet short‑term obligations without issuing new debt. The most direct fiscal impact is an improvement in the primary surplus because fewer resources are needed for debt service. Reducing unemployment or writing off debt are longer‑term outcomes, and a rise in inflation is unlikely given the tight fiscal stance. Therefore, the increase in primary surplus best captures the immediate effect of the infusion.

Q4. Why did the European Central Bank have to break its own rules during the crisis?

🔹 A. To lower inflation
🔹 B. To buy sovereign debt of troubled economies
🔹 C. To raise interest rates
🔹 D. To enforce fiscal discipline

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The ECB broke its own statutes by purchasing sovereign bonds of distressed economies, a move known as out‑of‑programme asset purchases. This action was taken to preserve market functioning and prevent a contagion of defaults, not to lower inflation, raise rates, or enforce fiscal rules. By buying the debt, the ECB supplied liquidity and lowered yields, stabilizing the banking sector. Understanding this helps explain the shift from a strict monetary policy to a more interventionist stance during the crisis.

Q5. Suppose Spain's bond yields rise to 6 % after the crisis, while Germany's remain at 1 %. What does this spread indicate about investor perceptions?

🔹 A. Higher growth in Spain
🔹 B. Higher risk in Spain
🔹 C. Lower inflation in Spain
🔹 D. Better fiscal discipline in Spain

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A widening yield gap—Spain at 6 % versus Germany at 1 %—signals that investors view Spanish sovereign debt as riskier. Higher yields compensate for perceived default risk, not for stronger growth or lower inflation. Better fiscal discipline would narrow the spread, while lower inflation would affect real yields differently. Thus, the spread primarily reflects heightened risk perception, guiding investors' portfolio choices and influencing borrowing costs for Spain.

Q6. Which country received a bailout of €85 billion in November 2011?

🔹 A. Ireland
🔹 B. Portugal
🔹 C. Italy
🔹 D. Greece

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Ireland received a €85 billion rescue package in November 2011, distinguishing it from the other listed nations. Portugal’s bailout was smaller, Italy never received a direct IMF package, and Greece’s larger loan was €110 billion. Recognizing Ireland’s bailout highlights how the crisis spread beyond the southern periphery, affecting a country with a previously strong banking sector and demonstrating the interconnectedness of eurozone economies.

Q7. How does the absence of a fiscal union exacerbate a monetary union's vulnerability during a sovereign debt crisis?

🔹 A. It limits ability to transfer funds
🔹 B. It increases exchange rate flexibility
🔹 C. It forces uniform tax rates
🔹 D. It eliminates need for central bank coordination

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

In a monetary union lacking a fiscal union, the central authority cannot redistribute funds among member states to offset asymmetric shocks. This limitation forces each country to rely on its own fiscal capacity, making the union vulnerable when a member faces a sovereign debt crisis. The other options—exchange‑rate flexibility, uniform tax rates, or eliminating central‑bank coordination—do not describe the core problem. Hence, the inability to transfer funds best explains the heightened vulnerability.

Q8. Imagine northern eurozone taxpayers are asked to absorb losses from southern banks' bad loans. Which political economy theory best explains the likely public response?

🔹 A. Supply‑side economics
🔹 B. Class struggle theory
🔹 C. Monetarist theory
🔹 D. Rational expectations

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Class struggle theory argues that economic conflicts arise between dominant capital owners and the working class, predicting mass protest when workers bear the burden of austerity. When northern taxpayers are asked to absorb losses, the theory suggests that collective action by Greek workers and broader social movements would pressure policymakers to shift the burden onto banks. Supply‑side, monetarist, and rational‑expectations frameworks focus on different mechanisms and do not foreground the role of organized labor, making them less suitable.

Q9. If Greece had financed its debt with long‑term bonds instead of short‑term loans, how might the 2010 crisis have differed?

🔹 A. Debt would be larger
🔹 B. Repayment pressure would be lower
🔹 C. Inflation would rise
🔹 D. EU would not need a bailout

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Financing debt with long‑term bonds spreads repayment obligations over many years, reducing the immediacy of cash‑flow pressures that sparked the 2010 crisis. While total debt might still be high, the need to refinance large short‑term portions would be less acute, potentially softening the urgency of a bailout. The other options—larger debt, inflation rise, or eliminating the need for an EU rescue—are not direct consequences of longer maturities. Therefore, lower repayment pressure is the most plausible outcome.

Q10. What was a major social consequence of the austerity measures imposed on Greece?

🔹 A. Decrease in unemployment
🔹 B. Increase in strikes and protests
🔹 C. Rise in foreign investment
🔹 D. Improvement in public services

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Austerity measures in Greece led to widespread social unrest, manifesting as frequent strikes, protests, and two general elections within two years. Unemployment did not fall; foreign investment did not surge, and public services generally deteriorated rather than improved. The increase in collective actions reflects public dissatisfaction with cuts to pensions and wages, illustrating how fiscal tightening can provoke political instability. Recognizing this link helps explain why policymakers faced intense domestic pressure during the crisis.

Q11. Given Portugal's unemployment was around 15 % in March 2012, what fiscal policy would be most appropriate to stimulate growth without breaching EU limits?

🔹 A. Increase taxes
🔹 B. Cut public spending
🔹 C. Implement targeted fiscal stimulus
🔹 D. Raise interest rates

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

With unemployment near 15 %, a narrowly targeted fiscal stimulus—such as temporary tax credits for hiring or sector‑specific infrastructure projects—can boost demand without breaching the EU’s deficit limits. Raising taxes or cutting spending would worsen the slump, while raising interest rates would further dampen growth. A focused stimulus aims to revive employment and output while keeping overall fiscal balance within the stipulated thresholds, aligning with the EU’s fiscal framework during a crisis.
