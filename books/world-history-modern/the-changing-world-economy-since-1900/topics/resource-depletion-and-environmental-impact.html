What is Resource depletion and environmental impact?

Rapid global economic expansion since 1900 has led to significant resource depletion as forests, minerals, and freshwater sources are consumed faster than they can regenerate, raising concerns about long-term sustainability and the ability of future generations to meet their needs.

Q1. What are fossil fuels?

A.Remains of ancient plants and animals ✅
B.Renewable energy sources such as wind and solar
C.Synthetic chemicals produced in factories
D.Minerals extracted from the earth
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Fossil fuels are the remains of plants and animals that lived millions of years ago and transformed into coal, oil, or natural gas under heat and pressure. This definition matches option A. Options B, C, and D describe different energy or material categories that are not fossil fuels. Understanding this basic fact helps students recognize why these fuels are finite and why their depletion matters for future energy planning.

Q2. Why are fossil fuels considered non‑renewable resources?

A.They form over millions of years, far longer than a human lifespan ✅
B.They can be replenished quickly by planting trees
C.They are produced by the Sun each day
D.They are stored in underground water reservoirs
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Fossil fuels require geological time—millions of years—to develop, far exceeding the rate at which humans consume them. Option A correctly captures this long formation period. Options B and C describe renewable processes, while D is unrelated. Recognizing the non‑renewable nature explains why societies must manage consumption and seek alternatives before supplies run out.

Q3. A coastal nation discovers large offshore oil reserves and begins domestic drilling. Which immediate economic effect is most likely?

A.Increased trade surplus due to reduced oil imports ✅
B.Higher unemployment because oil jobs replace other sectors
C.Decreased government revenue from taxes
D.Rapid inflation caused by oil price spikes
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Domestic drilling reduces reliance on imported oil, allowing the country to keep more money within its economy, creating a trade surplus. Option A reflects this outcome. Option B is unlikely because new oil jobs usually add employment, not reduce it. Option C would increase, not decrease, revenue, and option D is unrelated to the immediate balance‑of‑payments effect.

Q4. During which decade did OPEC actively try to conserve oil supplies?

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: OPEC’s conservation efforts are historically tied to the 1970s, a period marked by oil embargoes and heightened awareness of limited supplies. Option B is correct. The 1960s predate the major oil crises, while the 1980s and 1990s saw different market dynamics. Knowing the timeline helps students link policy actions to global energy concerns.

Q5. How does the depletion of oil reserves influence global warming?

A.Less oil use reduces greenhouse‑gas emissions, slowing warming
B.Depletion forces use of higher‑carbon fuels, accelerating warming ✅
C.Oil depletion has no effect on atmospheric temperature
D.More oil leads to cooler oceans, offsetting warming
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: When oil becomes scarce, societies may turn to fuels like coal or tar sands, which emit more CO₂ per unit energy, thus intensifying warming. Option B captures this chain reaction. Option A incorrectly assumes depletion automatically cuts emissions. Option C ignores the link between fuel choice and temperature, and option D confuses oceanic heat dynamics with fuel use. Understanding this relationship shows why resource exhaustion can worsen climate change.

Q6. A city reduces its oil consumption by 20 % and replaces that energy with wind power that emits no CO₂. If the city originally emitted 5 million tons of CO₂ annually from oil, approximately how many tons will be avoided?

A.1 million tons
B.2 million tons ✅
C.3 million tons
D.4 million tons
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A 20 % cut of 5 million tons equals \(0.20 \times 5{,}000{,}000 = 1{,}000{,}000\) tons avoided. However, because the replacement energy is carbon‑free, the entire 20 % reduction translates to 1 million tons saved, leaving 4 million tons still emitted. Option B (2 million) is incorrect; the correct answer is 1 million tons, which corresponds to option A. The explanation clarifies the arithmetic and demonstrates how shifting to renewables directly cuts emissions.

Q7. Why does the exhaustion of raw materials like tin and copper threaten ecosystem health?

A.These metals are essential for building habitats for wildlife
B.Their extraction causes habitat loss, pollution, and reduced biodiversity ✅
C.They are directly consumed by animals as food
D.They regulate soil temperature
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Mining for metals such as tin and copper disrupts land, creates tailings, and releases toxins that degrade habitats and lower biodiversity, making option B accurate. Option A misstates the role of metals; they are not habitats. Option C suggests direct consumption, which is false, and option D confuses physical properties with ecological functions. Recognizing the environmental cost of resource extraction underscores the need for recycling and sustainable use.

Q8. A manufacturing firm replaces its coal‑fired boilers with a nuclear power contract. Which effect on resource exhaustion is most probable?

A.Coal consumption drops dramatically, preserving that finite resource ✅
B.Nuclear fuel will be exhausted faster than coal
C.The firm will increase overall energy use
D.The switch eliminates all environmental impacts
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Switching from coal to nuclear removes the need for coal, a finite fossil fuel, thereby conserving it—option A. Nuclear fuel (uranium) is also finite but lasts longer and is used more efficiently, so option B exaggerates depletion speed. Option C is unrelated to resource exhaustion, and option D ignores nuclear waste and other impacts. This scenario illustrates how energy choices can extend the lifespan of dwindling resources.

Q10. World oil reserves are estimated at 1.7 trillion barrels. Global consumption averages 35 million barrels per day. Approximately how many years of supply remain?

A.≈ 100 years
B.≈ 140 years ✅
C.≈ 200 years
D.≈ 250 years
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: First compute annual consumption: \(35{,}000{,}000 \times 365 = 12{,}775{,}000{,}000\) barrels per year. Divide reserves by yearly use: \(\frac{1{,}700{,}000{,}000{,}000}{12{,}775{,}000{,}000} \approx 133\) years. The nearest answer is ≈ 140 years, option B. Options A, C, and D are far off. This calculation demonstrates how current draw rates can be projected to estimate remaining lifespan of a non‑renewable resource.

Q11. How does a feedback loop between resource depletion and pollution amplify environmental damage?

A.Depletion reduces pollution, creating a stabilizing loop
B.Pollution accelerates depletion, which in turn increases pollution further ✅
C.Both processes occur independently with no interaction
D.The loop only affects economic growth, not ecosystems
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: When resources are exhausted, societies often turn to cheaper, dirtier alternatives, raising pollution levels; higher pollution can degrade ecosystems, making extraction harder and prompting even more intensive exploitation—a reinforcing cycle captured in option B. Option A incorrectly describes a stabilizing effect, while C denies interaction, and D misplaces the focus on economics. Recognizing this loop is vital for designing policies that break the cycle.

Q12. A government introduces a carbon tax of $50 per ton of CO₂. Which of the following is the most likely result regarding resource use?

A.Higher reliance on coal because it becomes cheaper
B.Shift toward lower‑carbon fuels such as natural gas and renewables ✅
C.No change in energy consumption patterns
D.Increase in oil imports from abroad
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A carbon price makes carbon‑intensive fuels like coal more expensive, encouraging firms to adopt cleaner alternatives, making option B correct. Option A is opposite to the tax’s purpose, option C ignores economic incentives, and option D assumes the tax affects trade rather than fuel choice. The scenario shows how fiscal tools can steer societies away from exhausting fossil resources.

Q13. Compare the exhaustion rates of coal and natural gas. Which statement is most accurate?

A.Coal reserves are being depleted faster than natural gas because coal use is growing faster
B.Natural gas reserves are depleting faster due to higher energy density and rising demand ✅
C.Both resources deplete at identical rates worldwide
D.Coal is renewable, so its exhaustion rate is negligible
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Natural gas has a higher energy density and its consumption is rising sharply for electricity and heating, leading to a quicker drawdown than coal, which, despite large reserves, grows more slowly—option B reflects this. Option A reverses the trend, option C overlooks differing market dynamics, and option D falsely labels coal as renewable. Understanding these differences informs strategic planning for energy transition.

C.Shipping routes will expand because oil becomes abundant
D.Governments will increase subsidies for gasoline vehicles

Option A contradicts price trends, option C misreads scarcity as abundance, and option D opposes typical policy responses to rising fuel costs. This scenario illustrates how peak‑oil dynamics can reshape transport energy demand.

Recognizing nuclear’s role clarifies why diversification of energy sources can prolong the life of finite fossil reserves.
