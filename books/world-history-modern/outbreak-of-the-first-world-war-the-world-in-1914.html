📖 Outbreak of the First World War: The world in 1914

European Rivalries and the Road to the First World War

European international relations before 1914 were shaped by imperial competition, nationalist movements, military expansion, and rival alliance systems. Between 1905 and 1914, the Moroccan crises, Anglo-Russian rapprochement, Bosnian annexation, and Balkan conflicts repeatedly challenged the European balance of power. Germany, Britain, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and Serbia pursued competing political and strategic interests. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand transformed these accumulated tensions into an international crisis, demonstrating how diplomatic rivalries and military commitments contributed to the outbreak of the First World War.

Historical Background: Nationalism, Imperialism and Alliances

Understanding nineteenth-century European nationalism, the unification of Germany in 1871, Italian unification, imperial expansion in Africa, and the declining authority of the Ottoman Empire provides essential historical context. Students should also know the Triple Alliance of 1882, Franco-Russian Alliance of 1894, British naval supremacy, German industrial and military expansion, and Austro-Russian competition for influence in southeastern Europe. These developments explain the diplomatic relationships and strategic rivalries that shaped international politics before 1914.

Learning Outcomes: Explaining the Causes of War and the July Crisis

Students should be able to analyse Europe's political and military balance in 1914, explain the causes and consequences of the Moroccan, Bosnian, and Balkan crises, and assess the significance of the Anglo-Russian Agreement. They should distinguish the underlying causes of war from the immediate trigger, reconstruct the diplomatic escalation following the Sarajevo assassination, compare the interests of major European powers, and evaluate different historical interpretations of responsibility for the outbreak of war.

Key Themes: Moroccan, Bosnian and Balkan Crises

  • World War I Prologue and Background - European imperialism, nationalism, militarism, and competing strategic interests.
  • Europe and the World in 1914 - Great powers, political systems, military preparations, and diplomatic divisions.
  • First Moroccan Crisis, 1905–1906 - German opposition to French influence and the Algeciras Conference settlement.
  • Anglo-Russian Agreement, 1907 - Colonial compromises that completed the diplomatic alignment of the Triple Entente.
  • Bosnian Crisis, 1908–1909 - Austro-Hungarian annexation, Serbian opposition, and Russian diplomatic setbacks.
  • Agadir Crisis, 1911 - German intervention in Morocco and renewed tensions with France and Britain.
  • Balkan Conflicts and Sarajevo, 1912–1914 - Ottoman territorial losses, Serbian ambitions, and the assassination that triggered war.

Study Sequence: From European Rivalries to Sarajevo

Start with Europe's political geography, competing alliances, imperial ambitions, and military preparations in 1914. Examine the First Moroccan Crisis to understand German-French rivalry, followed by the Anglo-Russian Agreement and the Bosnian annexation crisis. Study the Agadir confrontation to trace continuing colonial tensions, then examine the First Balkan War and its consequences for Serbia, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire. Finish with the Sarajevo assassination, the July Crisis, and the debate over responsibility for the outbreak of war.

How Alliances and Balkan Crises Escalated Tensions

Imperial competition and nationalism intensified rivalries among European powers, while military expansion increased mutual suspicion. The Moroccan crises exposed German-French tensions and encouraged closer cooperation between France and Britain. The 1907 Anglo-Russian Agreement reduced colonial disagreements and strengthened the diplomatic alignment of the Triple Entente. Meanwhile, Bosnia's annexation and the Balkan Wars increased Austro-Serbian hostility and Russian involvement. These interconnected tensions provided the background for the July Crisis, during which the Sarajevo assassination escalated into a European war.

Timeline: Sarajevo Assassination and the July Crisis of 1914

On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie were assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip. Austria-Hungary delivered an ultimatum to Serbia on 23 July. Serbia replied on 25 July, accepting most demands but rejecting certain provisions. Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July. Russia began general mobilisation on 30 July, followed by Germany's declaration of war against Russia on 1 August and France on 3 August. Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August, prompting Britain to declare war on Germany the same day.

Why the 1914 European Crisis Became a World War

European diplomatic rivalries explain why the Sarajevo assassination developed into a conflict involving the continent's major powers. Knowledge of these relationships is essential for understanding wartime alliances, military campaigns, the collapse of European empires, the Russian Revolution, and the peace settlements that reshaped international politics after 1918.

Common Misconceptions: The Triple Entente and Sarajevo

Students sometimes claim that 'the assassination alone caused the First World War'; it triggered the July Crisis, but imperial rivalry, nationalism, militarism, and diplomatic decisions created deeper conditions for conflict. The statement 'the Triple Entente was one formal military alliance' is misleading because it developed through separate agreements and understandings. Finally, 'Austria-Hungary first occupied Bosnia in 1908' confuses occupation with annexation; Austro-Hungarian administration began in 1878, while formal annexation occurred in 1908.

Exam Focus: Alliances, Crises and Responsibility for War

Important examination tasks include arranging the Moroccan, Bosnian, and Balkan crises chronologically, identifying the countries involved, and explaining their diplomatic consequences. Students should distinguish the Triple Alliance from the Triple Entente, analyse the significance of the Anglo-Russian Agreement, and connect the Balkan Wars with Austro-Serbian rivalry. Source-based and analytical questions may require evaluating the July Crisis, separating long-term causes from immediate triggers, and comparing the responsibility of major European governments.

Summary: How Europe Reached War in 1914

By 1914, European international relations had become increasingly unstable because of imperial rivalry, nationalist ambitions, military competition, and opposing diplomatic alignments. Repeated confrontations in Morocco, Bosnia, and the Balkans weakened international trust and intensified regional hostility. The assassination of Franz Ferdinand activated these accumulated tensions through ultimatums, mobilisation, and declarations of war. The resulting conflict emerged from the interaction of long-term structural pressures and immediate political decisions.

Key Terms and Leaders in Prewar European Diplomacy

  • Triple Alliance - The 1882 political and military alliance between Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy.
  • Triple Entente - The diplomatic understanding among Britain, France, and Russia developed through agreements completed by 1907.
  • Algeciras Conference - The 1906 international conference that addressed the First Moroccan Crisis and limited Germany's diplomatic success.
  • Anglo-Russian Convention - The 1907 agreement resolving British-Russian disputes concerning Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet.
  • Bosnian Annexation - Austria-Hungary's formal incorporation of Bosnia-Herzegovina in October 1908.
  • Agadir Crisis - The 1911 confrontation caused by Germany's deployment of the gunboat Panther to Agadir, Morocco.
  • Balkan League - The alliance of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, and Montenegro formed against Ottoman control in 1912.
  • Gavrilo Princip - The Bosnian Serb nationalist who assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie on 28 June 1914.

Primary Sources for the July Crisis and Prewar Alliances

  • July Crisis Diplomatic Documents - Examine the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum, Serbian reply, diplomatic telegrams, and government communications from July 1914.
  • British National Archives - Search Foreign Office correspondence concerning the Moroccan crises, European alliances, and British intervention in August 1914.
  • German Foreign Office Records - Investigate German diplomatic policy, the Agadir Crisis, and decision-making during the July Crisis.
  • Imperial War Museums Collections - Explore contemporary photographs, newspapers, personal accounts, and documentary evidence concerning pre-war Europe.
  • Europeana 1914–1918 Collections - Investigate digitised letters, photographs, diaries, and archival materials relating to European society before and during the war.
