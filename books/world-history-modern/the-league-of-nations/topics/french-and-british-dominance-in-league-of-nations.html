What is French and British dominance in League of Nations?

France and Britain dominated the League’s Council and policy direction throughout the interwar period, often prioritizing their own national interests and colonial concerns over collective security, which alienated smaller nations and made the League appear as a tool of imperial maintenance rather than genuine internationalism.

📝 All French and British dominance in League of Nations MCQs

Q1. On what date did the League of Nations formally come into existence?

A.10-Jan-19
B.10-Jan-20 ✅
C.11-Nov-18
D.28-Jun-19
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The League was officially established on 10 January 1920, coinciding with the activation of the Treaty of Versailles. This date marks the moment when the League’s Covenant became operative, creating a permanent international body tasked with preventing future wars through collective security and diplomatic cooperation.

Q2. Where were the headquarters of the League of Nations located?

A.Paris, France
B.London, United Kingdom
C.Geneva, Switzerland ✅
D.Brussels, Belgium
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The League set up its permanent secretariat in Geneva, Switzerland, a neutral location chosen to facilitate diplomatic dialogue among member states. Geneva’s central European position and its reputation for neutrality made it an ideal venue for the League’s administrative functions and for hosting international conferences during the interwar period.

Q3. Which South African statesman famously praised the League as a 'great miracle of history'?

A.Jan Smuts ✅
B.Nelson Mandela
C.Cyril Ramaphosa
D.Thabo Mbeki
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Jan Smuts, a prominent South African leader, expressed optimism about the League’s potential in the early 1930s, describing its achievements as one of history’s great miracles. His endorsement reflected the broader belief among many contemporaries that the League could sustain peace and promote cooperation among nations.

Q4. Which treaty came into operation on the same day the League was founded?

A.Treaty of Trianon
B.Treaty of Saint-Germain
C.Treaty of Versailles ✅
D.Treaty of Locarno
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The Treaty of Versailles, which formally ended World War I, entered into force on 10 January 1920—the same day the League of Nations was inaugurated. This synchronization ensured that the League’s Covenant was incorporated into the peace settlement, giving the organization immediate legal standing among the victorious Allied powers.

Q5. Who was the American president most closely associated with the concept of the League?

A.Woodrow Wilson ✅
B.Franklin D. Roosevelt
C.Theodore Roosevelt
D.Harry S. Truman
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: President Woodrow Wilson championed the idea of a permanent international organization for peace, embedding it as the final point in his famous Fourteen Points. Wilson’s advocacy and diplomatic efforts at the Paris Peace Conference were instrumental in shaping the League’s Covenant and securing its inclusion in the post‑war treaties.

Q6. What was the primary aim of the League of Nations?

A.Promoting colonial expansion
B.Collective security to prevent war ✅
C.Establishing a world currency
D.Facilitating global trade agreements
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The League’s core purpose was to maintain peace through collective security: if any member state was attacked, all other members would act together to restrain the aggressor. This principle was intended to deter aggression by making the cost of war prohibitive for any would‑be invader.

Q7. Why did the League fail to stop the Japanese invasion of Manchuria in 1931?

A.It lacked a standing army to enforce decisions ✅
B.Japan withdrew voluntarily after sanctions
C.The League imposed severe economic penalties
D.Member states unanimously supported Japan
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The League’s inability to prevent the Manchurian crisis stemmed from its lack of enforcement mechanisms, particularly a standing military force. Without a means to compel compliance, the League could only issue condemnations, which Japan ignored, exposing a fundamental weakness in the organization’s collective security framework.

Q8. Which of the following individuals was NOT involved in drafting the League’s Covenant?

A.Lord Robert Cecil
B.Jan Smuts
C.Leon Bourgeois
D.Benito Mussolini ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Benito Mussolini, the Italian fascist leader, never participated in the drafting of the League’s Covenant. The Covenant was prepared by an international committee that included Lord Robert Cecil of Britain, Jan Smuts of South Africa, and Léon Bourgeois of France, among others, reflecting a collaborative effort among Allied states.

Q9. If the League had 58 member states in 1920 and added 2 new members each year, how many members would it have by 1930?

A.68
B.78 ✅
C.88
D.98
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Starting with 58 members in 1920, the League would gain \(2 \times 10 = 20\) new members over ten years. Adding this to the original count yields \(58 + 20 = 78\) member states by 1930. This simple linear growth illustrates the organization’s expanding reach during the early interwar period.

Q10. Which scenario best illustrates the League’s humanitarian work?

A.Organizing a global trade summit
B.Providing food aid to famine‑stricken regions ✅
C.Negotiating a cease‑fire in a civil war
D.Establishing a new colonial territory
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The League’s humanitarian achievements were exemplified by its efforts to deliver food and medical assistance to populations suffering from famine and disease. By coordinating relief shipments and supporting refugee repatriation, the League demonstrated a commitment to social welfare that complemented its political aims of peacekeeping.

Q11. Why did including the League’s covenant in each peace treaty help ensure its existence?

A.It made the covenant a binding part of each nation’s law ✅
B.It allowed the League to collect taxes directly
C.It gave the League authority to deploy troops
D.It enabled the League to veto domestic legislation
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Embedding the covenant within each peace treaty turned the League’s principles into legally binding obligations for the signatory nations. This integration meant that the League could not be dismissed as a mere idea; instead, it became an operative part of international law, giving it a concrete foundation.

Q12. Which event in 1935 marked a major decline in the League’s authority?

A.The Japanese invasion of Manchuria
B.The Italian attack on Abyssinia ✅
C.The signing of the Locarno Treaties
D.The Munich Agreement
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The Italian invasion of Abyssinia (Ethiopia) in 1935 starkly exposed the League’s impotence. Despite issuing condemnations and imposing limited sanctions, the League could not coerce Italy to withdraw, revealing its inability to enforce collective security and significantly eroding its credibility among member states.

Q13. Which statement best describes the concept of collective security as envisioned by the League?

A.Only the most powerful nations enforce peace
B.All members act together to deter aggression ✅
C.Individual nations act independently to protect themselves
D.Security is ensured through secret alliances
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Collective security, the cornerstone of the League’s design, required that all member states coordinate their diplomatic and, if necessary, military responses to any act of aggression. By presenting a united front, the League aimed to make the cost of attacking any member prohibitively high, thereby deterring potential aggressors.

Q14. When did the League of Nations cease to meet for the last time?

A.Jan-39
B.Jun-39
C.Dec-39 ✅
D.Mar-40
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The League’s final session took place in December 1939, after which it never reconvened. By that time, the outbreak of World War II had rendered the organization ineffective, and its functions were eventually transferred to the newly created United Nations after the war.

Q15. Why did the League not intervene in Germany’s disputes with Czechoslovakia and Poland?

A.It lacked a mandate to address European conflicts
B.Member states were unwilling to confront Germany ✅
C.The League had dissolved before the disputes arose
D.It was preoccupied with Asian affairs
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Many member nations were reluctant to challenge Germany directly due to political appeasement and fear of escalating tensions. This hesitancy, combined with the League’s already weakened authority, meant that it could not assert influence over the German‑Czechoslovakian and German‑Polish crises that eventually led to World War II.

Q16. If the League required each member to allocate 0.5 % of its GDP to a common army, how might this have affected its ability to enforce decisions?

A.It would have created a powerful deterrent force ✅
B.It would have caused economic collapse in small states
C.It would have led to unanimous support for all actions
D.It would have eliminated the need for diplomacy
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Mandating a contribution of 0.5 % of GDP from each member would have generated a sizable multinational force, providing the League with real enforcement capabilities. Such a standing army could have acted as a credible deterrent, compelling aggressor nations to heed League resolutions and reducing the likelihood of unilateral aggression.

Q17. Which factor most decisively contributed to the League’s failure to prevent World War II?

A.Its early successes in humanitarian aid
B.The absence of a permanent military force ✅
C.Its comprehensive economic sanctions regime
D.The unanimous support of all major powers
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The lack of a standing army or any coercive power was the critical weakness that undermined the League’s ability to enforce collective security. Without tangible means to compel compliance, the League could only issue condemnations, which aggressor nations like Japan and Italy readily ignored, paving the way to global conflict.

Q18. If the League could impose sanctions reducing an aggressor’s trade by 30 %, would that likely have prevented Italy’s invasion of Abyssinia?

A.Yes, because economic pressure would force compliance
B.No, because Italy’s regime was willing to endure sanctions ✅
C.It would have had no effect on Italy’s decision
D.The sanctions would have caused Italy to withdraw but not end the war
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Historical evidence shows that Italy continued its campaign despite the League’s limited sanctions, indicating that a 30 % trade reduction would not have been sufficient to compel Mussolini’s government to abandon its imperial ambitions. The regime’s ideological motivations outweighed economic deterrence.

Q19. Was Jan Smuts’s optimism about the League justified in the early 1930s?

A.Yes, because the League resolved most disputes then
B.No, because subsequent aggressions exposed its weaknesses
C.Partially, as it succeeded in humanitarian work but failed politically ✅
D.It was entirely misplaced, given the League’s immediate collapse
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Smuts’s optimism reflected genuine achievements, such as the League’s refugee assistance and dispute resolution in the 1920s. However, the later inability to curb aggression by Japan and Italy demonstrated significant limitations, making his early confidence understandable but ultimately incomplete.

Q20. How many years did the League of Nations exist from its founding to its dissolution?

A.\(24\) years
B.\(25\) years
C.\(26\) years ✅
D.\(27\) years
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The League was founded in 1920 and dissolved in 1946, giving it a lifespan of \(1946 - 1920 = 26\) years. This period encompassed its early successes, the challenges of the 1930s, and its eventual replacement by the United Nations after World War II.

Q21. Why was embedding the League’s covenant in each peace treaty insufficient for guaranteeing its effectiveness?

A.It made the covenant unenforceable domestically
B.It allowed nations to ignore the covenant at will
C.It did not create a mechanism for collective enforcement ✅
D.It required unanimous approval for any action
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: While inclusion in peace treaties gave the covenant legal status, the League still lacked a concrete mechanism for collective enforcement, such as a standing army or binding economic sanctions. Without these tools, member states could disregard decisions without immediate repercussions, limiting the League’s practical influence.

Q22. If the League possessed a permanent military force of 100,000 troops, what impact might this have had on collective security?

A.It would guarantee deterrence against any aggressor ✅
B.It would cause member states to lose sovereignty
C.It would eliminate the need for diplomatic negotiations
D.It would lead to constant warfare among members
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: A dedicated force of 100,000 troops would have provided the League with credible deterrence, enabling it to back its resolutions with actual military capability. This presence could have discouraged potential aggressors, reinforcing the principle of collective security and possibly preventing some conflicts.

Q23. What key lesson from the League’s shortcomings influenced the formation of the United Nations?

A.The necessity of a permanent secretariat
B.The importance of economic development programs
C.The need for a binding security council with veto powers ✅
D.The requirement for universal membership of all nations
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The failure of the League highlighted the need for a stronger decision‑making body with enforceable authority, leading to the establishment of the United Nations Security Council, where permanent members possess veto power. This structure was designed to prevent the paralysis that hampered the League’s ability to act decisively.
