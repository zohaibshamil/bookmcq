What is UN and Czechoslovak crisis 1968?

The UN could not respond effectively to the 1968 Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia because the Soviet Union vetoed Security Council resolutions, illustrating how superpower dominance prevented collective action during Cold War crises.

📝 All UN and Czechoslovak crisis 1968 MCQs

Q1. When did the Prague Spring reforms, which sparked the crisis, officially begin?

A. 1965
B. 1968 ✅
C. 1970
D. 1972

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The reforms were introduced after Alexander Dubček became First Secretary in January 1968. His program of “socialism with a human face” sought greater political freedoms and economic decentralisation, alarming Soviet leaders and setting the stage for the later invasion.

Q2. Which Warsaw Pact country contributed the largest number of troops during the invasion?

A. Poland
B. East Germany
C. Hungary ✅
D. Bulgaria

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

While troops from several Warsaw Pact members entered Czechoslovakia, the Soviet Union supplied the bulk of the force, estimated at around 200,000 soldiers. The contributions of Poland, East Germany, Hungary, and Bulgaria were comparatively smaller, making the Soviet presence dominant.

Q3. What justification did the USSR give for its intervention in Czechoslovakia?

A. Protection of socialist ideals
B. Response to a formal request from the Czech government ✅
C. Defense against NATO aggression
D. Economic aid to the region

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Soviet Union claimed that the Czechoslovak leadership had invited the Warsaw Pact forces to restore order, a narrative later contested by Czech officials. This pretext was used to legitise the military action despite the lack of any credible evidence of a formal request.

Q4. Which United Nations body attempted to condemn the Soviet invasion, only to be vetoed by the USSR?

A. General Assembly
B. Security Council ✅
C. Economic and Social Council
D. International Court of Justice

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The UN Security Council, responsible for maintaining international peace, drafted a resolution condemning the invasion. However, the Soviet Union exercised its veto power, blocking the measure and illustrating the limitations of the UN when a permanent member opposes a proposal.

Q5. How did the Czechoslovak public react immediately after the invasion began?

A. Widespread celebrations
B. Mass protests and civil disobedience ✅
C. General indifference
D. Emigration in large numbers

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Citizens took to the streets, forming spontaneous “spontaneous demonstrations,” striking workers, and creating underground resistance networks. The visible opposition, despite the presence of foreign troops, demonstrated the depth of popular support for Dubček’s reforms and the rejection of Soviet domination.

Q6. Which of the following best describes the “Normalization” period that followed the crisis?

A. A return to liberal democratic reforms
B. A reinstatement of hard‑line communist policies ✅
C. A transition to a market economy
D. A period of neutral non‑alignment

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Normalization referred to the systematic reversal of the Prague Spring reforms, reinstating strict party control, censorship, and a centrally planned economy. It lasted until the late‑1980s, effectively suppressing the brief experiment with political liberalisation initiated in 1968.

Q7. If the United Nations had successfully passed a resolution condemning the invasion, which of the following would most likely have occurred?

A. Immediate withdrawal of Soviet troops
B. Economic sanctions imposed by the UN on the USSR
C. A diplomatic stalemate with limited practical effect ✅
D. Military intervention by NATO forces

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Given the Soviet Union’s veto power, a condemned resolution would have been largely symbolic. The most plausible outcome would be a diplomatic stalemate, with the resolution recorded but no enforcement mechanism, leaving the occupation largely unchanged.

Q8. What was the primary ideological conflict underlying the Soviet intervention?

A. Capitalist versus communist economic models
B. Nationalist independence versus Soviet hegemony ✅
C. Religious freedom versus state atheism
D. Ethnic tensions between Czech and Slovak populations

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The core dispute centered on the degree of autonomy Czechoslovakia could exercise within the socialist bloc. Moscow feared that Dubček’s reforms could inspire similar movements elsewhere, threatening Soviet hegemony and prompting a decisive military response to preserve ideological uniformity.

Q9. Which of the following leaders was most directly associated with the reforms that triggered the crisis?

A. Václav Havel
B. Alexander Dubček ✅
C. Milan Kundera
D. Gustáv Husák

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Alexander Dubček, elected First Secretary of the Communist Party in January 1968, launched the “Action Programme” that promised greater freedom of expression, economic decentralisation, and political pluralism, actions that alarmed Soviet leaders and ultimately led to the Warsaw Pact invasion.

Q10. How did the Soviet Union’s veto power in the UN Security Council affect the possibility of collective security actions?

A. It enhanced the UN’s ability to enforce resolutions
B. It rendered the Security Council ineffective against the USSR ✅
C. It allowed NATO to intervene unilaterally
D. It forced the UN to seek alternative dispute‑resolution mechanisms

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The permanent‑member veto enables any of the five major powers to block substantive resolutions, meaning that when the USSR vetoed the condemnation of its own actions, the Security Council could not authorise collective measures, highlighting a structural weakness in the UN system.

Q11. Which of these statements best captures the comparison made between the Czechoslovak crisis and the Hungarian uprising of 1956?

A. Both were successful revolutions that overthrew communist regimes
B. Both involved brief periods of liberalisation that were crushed by Soviet force ✅
C. Both were initiated by NATO support for local dissidents
D. Both resulted in immediate democratic elections

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The text explicitly likens the two events as “repeat performances,” noting that each featured a brief opening for reform or independence that was violently suppressed by Soviet‑led troops, illustrating a pattern of Soviet intolerance for deviation from the bloc’s line.

Q12. What role did the concept of “Sovereign Equality” play in the UN debate over the invasion?

A. It was used to justify the Soviet veto
B. It underpinned arguments that all nations, including Czechoslovakia, deserve equal protection ✅
C. It was cited to argue that the USSR had superior rights over smaller states
D. It was irrelevant to the discussion

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Proponents of the resolution invoked the UN Charter’s principle of sovereign equality, arguing that no member state should be subjected to external military intervention without its consent. The Soviet veto, however, demonstrated the tension between legal ideals and real‑political power.

Q13. Which international law principle was most directly violated by the Warsaw Pact invasion?

A. Non‑intervention in domestic affairs ✅
B. Self‑determination of peoples
C. Freedom of navigation on international waters
D. Prohibition of the use of chemical weapons

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The invasion breached the principle of non‑intervention, which obliges states to refrain from using force to influence the internal political affairs of another sovereign state. Despite the Soviet claim of invitation, the lack of legitimate consent made the action a clear violation of this norm.

Q14. If a hypothetical UN peacekeeping force had been deployed to Czechoslovakia before the invasion, which of the following outcomes would be most plausible?

A. The Soviet troops would have withdrawn immediately
B. The peacekeepers would have been expelled by Warsaw Pact forces ✅
C. The presence would have prevented any military action
D. The UN would have lost credibility without consent

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Given the Soviet Union’s willingness to use force to enforce its policies, any UN peacekeeping contingent lacking the backing of the Warsaw Pact would likely have been ordered out or forcibly removed, mirroring historical precedents where great‑power opposition undermines UN missions.

Q15. Which of the following best explains why the United Nations could not compel the USSR to withdraw its forces?

A. Lack of a clear legal definition of invasion
B. The USSR’s permanent‑member veto in the Security Council ✅
C. Absence of any member willing to propose a resolution
D. Insufficient funding for UN peace operations

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The structural design of the UN Security Council gives each of the five permanent members—China, France, Russia (formerly the USSR), the United Kingdom, and the United States—the right to veto substantive resolutions. When the USSR exercised this right, the Council could not adopt a binding measure demanding withdrawal.

Q16. Which of the following cultural figures openly opposed the invasion and later became a symbol of dissent?

A. Jaromír Jágr
B. Milan Kundera ✅
C. Antonín Dvořák
D. Karel Gott

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Milan Kundera, the renowned novelist, publicly condemned the Soviet crackdown and used his literary platform to critique the loss of freedom, making him an emblematic voice of resistance both within Czechoslovakia and internationally.

Q17. What was the immediate effect of the invasion on Czechoslovakia’s foreign policy orientation?

A. Alignment with NATO
B. Adoption of a non‑aligned stance
C. Strengthening ties with the Soviet bloc ✅
D. Withdrawal from all international organisations

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Following the invasion, Czechoslovakia’s foreign policy was forced back into strict alignment with the Soviet Union, abandoning the brief experiment of a more autonomous, potentially non‑aligned approach that Dubček had envisioned.

Q18. Which of the following best illustrates the concept of “soft power” in the context of the crisis?

A. Soviet economic aid to Czechoslovakia after the invasion
B. Use of propaganda to portray the invasion as a liberation ✅
C. Deployment of troops to enforce political conformity
D. Cultural exchanges promoting Czech literature abroad

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Soft power involves influencing others through attraction rather than coercion. Soviet propaganda framed the military intervention as a “brotherly assistance” to protect socialism, attempting to win domestic and international support without relying solely on hard military force.

Q19. How did the concept of “collective security” fail during the crisis?

A. The NATO alliance refused to intervene
B. The UN Security Council could not act due to a veto ✅
C. The Warsaw Pact withdrew its forces voluntarily
D. The League of Nations was dissolved

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Collective security depends on the willingness of major powers to act collectively against aggression. In this case, the Soviet Union’s veto of any condemning resolution prevented the UN from fulfilling its security mandate, exposing the fragility of the system when a major power opposes action.

Q20. Which of the following statements accurately reflects the outcome for the Czech leadership after the invasion?

A. All reformist leaders were executed
B. Dubček was replaced by a hard‑line successor ✅
C. The reformist leaders retained their positions
D. The leadership formed a coalition with Western democracies

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

After the invasion, Alexander Dubček was removed from his position and replaced by Gustáv Husák, a hard‑line communist who oversaw the period of “Normalization,” reversing many of the liberal reforms introduced during the Prague Spring.

Q21. In what way did the crisis influence later Eastern Bloc dissent movements?

A. It discouraged any further opposition
B. It inspired similar reformist attempts in Poland and Hungary ✅
C. It caused the Warsaw Pact to dissolve immediately
D. It led to the immediate collapse of the Soviet Union

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The suppression of the Prague Spring served as both a warning and a catalyst; while it demonstrated the limits of Soviet tolerance, it also highlighted the desire for change, influencing later movements such as Solidarity in Poland and the 1989 revolutions across the bloc.

Q22. Which of the following best describes the legal status of the Soviet‑led troops under international law at the time of the invasion?

A. They were peacekeepers under a UN mandate
B. They were occupying forces without consent ✅
C. They were invited by the Czechoslovak government
D. They were conducting humanitarian assistance

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The troops entered Czechoslovakia without a legitimate invitation or UN authorization, classifying them as occupying forces under international law, a status that contravenes the principle of non‑intervention and the prohibition of the use of force.

Q23. What was the reaction of the United States to the Soviet invasion?

A. Immediate military retaliation
B. Formal condemnation and diplomatic protests ✅
C. Support for the Soviet action
D. Neutrality and non‑involvement

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The United States issued strong statements condemning the invasion, lodged diplomatic protests, and supported UN efforts to denounce the action, but it refrained from direct military involvement, reflecting Cold War constraints and the risk of escalation.

Q24. Which of the following best explains why the Czechoslovak government denied having requested Soviet assistance?

A. Lack of communication channels
B. Desire to maintain domestic legitimacy ✅
C. Misperception of diplomatic protocols
D. Agreement with the Soviet narrative

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Acknowledging a request for intervention would have undermined the government’s credibility and suggested complicity, eroding public support for Dubček’s reforms. Denying the claim helped preserve a narrative of victimhood and resistance against external aggression.

Q25. If the Soviet Union had chosen not to intervene militarily, which of the following scenarios would most likely have occurred?

A. Czechoslovakia would have joined NATO
B. The reforms would have continued, potentially inspiring other satellite states ✅
C. Czechoslovakia would have collapsed economically
D. The Warsaw Pact would have dissolved immediately

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Without Soviet military pressure, Dubček’s reforms might have deepened, encouraging similar liberalisation in other Eastern Bloc nations. This could have led to a gradual erosion of Soviet control, though the exact trajectory would depend on complex internal and external dynamics.

Q26. Which of the following was a direct consequence of the invasion on Czechoslovak media?

A. Complete freedom of the press restored
B. Implementation of strict censorship and control ✅
C. Privatization of state television
D. Opening of foreign news bureaus

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

After the invasion, the regime reinstated rigorous censorship, shutting down independent publications, and placing the media under tight party supervision, thereby reversing the brief period of openness that had allowed critical discussion of political and cultural issues.

Q27. What percentage of the Warsaw Pact forces involved in the invasion were supplied by the Soviet Union?

A. Approximately 40 %
B. Approximately 60 %
C. Approximately 80 % ✅
D. Approximately 95 %

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Historical estimates indicate that roughly 80% of the total forces—about 200,000 troops—were Soviet, with the remaining contributions coming from Poland, East Germany, Hungary, and Bulgaria, underscoring the primacy of the USSR in the operation.

Q28. Which of the following best characterises the United Nations Security Council’s response to the crisis?

A. Decisive and unified condemnation
B. Inaction due to a Soviet veto ✅
C. Joint military planning with NATO
D. Passage of a peacekeeping resolution

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Council attempted to adopt a resolution condemning the invasion, but the Soviet Union exercised its veto, resulting in an effective stalemate and illustrating the Council’s inability to act when a permanent member opposes a measure.

Q29. How did the crisis affect the relationship between Czechoslovakia and its Slovak counterpart within the federation?

A. It strengthened their unity ✅
B. It caused a split and eventual separation
C. It had no impact on internal dynamics
D. It led to the creation of a joint defense force

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The invasion intensified the sense of shared victimhood between Czech and Slovak peoples, temporarily bolstering internal solidarity despite later political tensions that would culminate in the peaceful split of the federation in 1993.

Q30. Which of the following describes the “Action Programme” introduced by Dubček?

A. A plan to increase military spending
B. A set of reforms aimed at liberalising politics and economy ✅
C. A treaty to join NATO
D. A strategy to expand Soviet influence in Asia

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Dubček’s “Action Programme” outlined measures such as loosening censorship, allowing limited political pluralism, and decentralising economic decision‑making, all intended to create a more humane socialism, which alarmed Soviet leaders and precipitated the invasion.

Q31. In the context of the crisis, what does the term “Normalization” refer to?

A. A return to pre‑1970 democratic institutions
B. The process of reinstating strict communist orthodoxy ✅
C. The adoption of market‑economy reforms
D. The establishment of a neutral foreign policy

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Normalization denotes the systematic reversal of the Prague Spring reforms, reinstating hard‑line communist rule, re‑imposing censorship, and re‑aligning Czechoslovakia firmly with Soviet strategic interests, lasting until the late‑1980s.

Q32. Which of the following best explains why the UN could not enforce a cease‑fire despite condemnation?

A. Lack of any member country willing to provide troops
B. The Soviet Union’s veto prevented a binding resolution ✅
C. The Security Council lacked authority over Warsaw Pact nations
D. The cease‑fire required NATO approval

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A binding cease‑fire resolution requires Security Council approval. The USSR’s veto nullified any attempt to adopt such a measure, meaning the UN lacked the legal authority to compel the occupying forces to halt hostilities, illustrating the constraints of the veto system.

Q33. What role did the concept of “self‑determination” play in the diplomatic arguments surrounding the crisis?

A. It was used to justify Soviet intervention
B. It underpinned Western criticism of the invasion ✅
C. It was irrelevant to the UN debate
D. It supported the idea of a united Soviet bloc

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Western diplomats cited the principle of self‑determination to argue that Czechoslovakia had the right to choose its political path without external coercion, framing the Soviet invasion as a violation of this core UN Charter value.

Q34. Which of the following was a long‑term impact of the crisis on the Cold War dynamics?

A. Immediate end of the Cold War
B. Increased distrust between the USSR and its satellite states ✅
C. Strengthening of Soviet‑Western cooperation
D. Creation of a new NATO‑Warsaw Pact alliance

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The invasion deepened suspicion among Eastern Bloc nations regarding Soviet intentions, fostering a climate of dissent that eventually contributed to the weakening of Soviet influence and the eventual dissolution of the bloc, thereby reshaping Cold War relations.
