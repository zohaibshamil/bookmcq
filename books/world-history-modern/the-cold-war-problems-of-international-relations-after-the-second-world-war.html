📖 The Cold War: Problems of International Relations after the Second World War

Cold War Origins: From Wartime Alliance to Superpower Rivalry

The wartime alliance between the United States, Britain, and Soviet Union rapidly fractured after 1945 as ideological differences and competing security interests divided the international order. Disagreements at Yalta and Potsdam, Soviet control of Eastern Europe, and Western containment policies established rival political and military blocs. The Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Cominform, Berlin Blockade, and NATO formalised confrontation. After Stalin's death, diplomatic relations experienced a partial thaw, but nuclear competition continued, reaching a dangerous climax during the Cuban Missile Crisis and extending into the 1970s arms race.

Background: Russian Revolution, World War II and Ideological Division

Students should understand the Russian Revolution of 1917, the establishment of the Soviet communist state, and the ideological conflict between capitalism and communism. Knowledge of the Second World War, the wartime alliance against Nazi Germany, and the defeat of the Axis powers in 1945 is essential. Familiarity with the Allied occupation zones in Germany, the Yalta and Potsdam agreements, Soviet wartime losses, and disagreements over Poland and Eastern European governments provides the background for understanding the breakdown of cooperation. Previous knowledge of American isolationism and Soviet security concerns also helps explain their competing postwar objectives.

Learning Outcomes: Containment, Berlin and Nuclear Crises

Students should be able to explain the ideological, political, economic, and security causes of the Cold War and compare Soviet and Western interpretations of its origins. They should analyse the significance of Yalta, Potsdam, the Truman Doctrine, and Marshall Plan; explain the division of Germany and formation of NATO; and evaluate the consequences of the Berlin Blockade. They should also explain the limitations of the post-Stalin thaw, assess the Cuban Missile Crisis, and analyse the continuing nuclear arms race and international protests against nuclear weapons.

Topics: Yalta, Potsdam, Truman Doctrine, NATO and Cuba

  • Origins of the Cold War, 1945 - Ideological differences, wartime disagreements, Yalta, and Potsdam conferences.
  • Soviet Expansion in Eastern Europe, 1945–1948 - Communist governments, Soviet security policies, and Western suspicions.
  • Containment and Communist Organisation, 1947–1948 - Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Cominform, and Czechoslovak coup.
  • Berlin Crisis and Division of Germany, 1948–1949 - Blockade, airlift, separate German states, and NATO formation.
  • Nuclear Competition and Stalin's Death, 1949–1953 - Superpower weapons development and changing leadership in Moscow.
  • Cold War Thaw, 1953–1960 - Diplomatic reconciliation, peaceful coexistence, and continuing ideological disagreements.
  • Renewed Nuclear Tensions, 1962–1970s - Cuban Missile Crisis, strategic arms competition, détente, and anti-nuclear protests.

Study Sequence: From Postwar Settlements to the Cuban Missile Crisis

Begin with the ideological differences between capitalism and communism and examine the wartime relationship between the Western Allies and Soviet Union. Study the Yalta and Potsdam conferences before tracing Soviet consolidation of power in Eastern Europe. Next, examine the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Cominform, and Czechoslovak coup as developments that institutionalised division. Continue with the Berlin Blockade, airlift, establishment of two German states, and NATO. Then examine the nuclear arms race and Stalin's death, followed by Khrushchev-era reconciliation efforts and their limitations. Conclude with the Cuban Missile Crisis, 1970s arms competition, and nuclear disarmament protests.

How Soviet Expansion and Western Containment Deepened Division

Wartime disagreements at Yalta and Potsdam exposed incompatible American and Soviet objectives. Soviet control of Eastern Europe encouraged Western containment, while the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan prompted Moscow to strengthen communist coordination through Cominform. The Berlin crisis accelerated German division and NATO's formation. Stalin's death created opportunities for diplomatic thaw, but ideological rivalry and nuclear competition persisted. The Cuban Missile Crisis demonstrated the dangers of confrontation and encouraged subsequent arms-control initiatives.

Timeline: Berlin Blockade, NATO and the Cuban Missile Crisis

At Yalta, 4–11 February 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin discussed postwar arrangements; disagreements continued at Potsdam, 17 July–2 August. On 24 June 1948, Soviet authorities blocked land and water access to Western-controlled Berlin. Britain and the United States organised the Berlin Airlift, supplying the isolated population. The blockade ended on 12 May 1949. NATO was established on 4 April 1949, while West Germany was founded on 23 May and East Germany on 7 October, formalising Europe's division.

Why Cold War Rivalry Defined Postwar International Relations

The Cold War transformed the international system into a prolonged confrontation between two nuclear superpowers. Understanding its origins explains European division, military alliances, proxy conflicts, nuclear deterrence, and diplomatic competition. The developments also provide essential background for studying the Cuban Missile Crisis, détente, arms-control agreements, and international relations during the later twentieth century.

Common Misconceptions: Marshall Plan, Yalta and Stalin's Death

Students sometimes claim that 'Yalta and Potsdam established the Cold War'; both conferences addressed postwar arrangements, but worsening disagreements gradually produced confrontation. 'The Marshall Plan was a military alliance' confuses American economic assistance with NATO's collective defence commitments. Finally, 'Stalin's death ended the Cold War' overlooks continuing ideological rivalry, nuclear competition, and crises under Khrushchev and later Soviet leaders.

Exam Focus: Cold War Conferences, Alliances and Crises

Frequently tested areas include distinguishing the decisions of Yalta and Potsdam, explaining ideological causes, and matching the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, and Cominform with their purposes. Chronology questions examine the Berlin Blockade, NATO formation, and German division. Comparative questions address Soviet and American policies, while analytical questions evaluate the 1950s thaw, limitations of peaceful coexistence, causes and consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the continuing nuclear arms race.

Summary: Cold War Confrontation and Attempts at Détente

Postwar disagreements transformed the wartime alliance into an international confrontation between capitalist and communist powers. Soviet influence in Eastern Europe and Western containment policies produced divided alliances and separate German states. Although Stalin's death opened opportunities for improved diplomacy, nuclear rivalry remained dangerous. The Cuban Missile Crisis demonstrated the risk of direct superpower conflict, while subsequent arms-control efforts and anti-nuclear protests reflected growing international concern about nuclear destruction.

Key Terms, Leaders and Cold War Agreements

  • Yalta Conference - The February 1945 meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin concerning postwar Europe and international arrangements.
  • Potsdam Conference - The July–August 1945 meeting at which Allied leaders addressed Germany's occupation and unresolved European issues.
  • Containment - The American strategy of preventing further expansion of Soviet and communist influence internationally.
  • Truman Doctrine - The 1947 American policy of supporting countries threatened by communist expansion, initially announced in relation to Greece and Turkey.
  • Marshall Plan - The American European Recovery Program announced in 1947 to assist economic reconstruction and encourage political stability.
  • Cominform - The Communist Information Bureau established in 1947 to coordinate communist parties under Soviet leadership.
  • NATO - The North Atlantic military alliance established in April 1949 on the principle of collective defence.
  • Cuban Missile Crisis - The October 1962 confrontation between the United States and Soviet Union over Soviet nuclear missiles stationed in Cuba.

Primary Documents on Yalta, Potsdam and Cold War Policy

  • Yalta and Potsdam Conference Records - Examine original agreements, diplomatic minutes, and correspondence involving Roosevelt, Churchill, Truman, and Stalin through the Avalon Project at Yale Law School.
  • Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan Documents - Search President Truman's 12 March 1947 address, George C. Marshall's Harvard speech of 5 June 1947, and American government records concerning European recovery.
  • National Archives Berlin Airlift Collection - Investigate original photographs, military transportation records, diplomatic correspondence, and documentation concerning the 1948–1949 blockade.
  • NATO Historical Archives - Explore the original North Atlantic Treaty, founding negotiations, collective defence arrangements, and historical documents concerning the division of Europe.
  • Wilson Center Digital Archive - Search declassified Soviet, American, and Eastern European documents concerning Cominform, Soviet foreign policy, Khrushchev's leadership, and diplomatic relations during the Cold War.
  • John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and National Archives - Examine the Cuban Missile Crisis recordings, intelligence photographs, correspondence between Kennedy and Khrushchev, and decisions concerning the removal of nuclear missiles.
  • Arms Control Association and Nuclear History Collections - Research SALT I, the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty of 1972, the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963, nuclear weapons demonstrations, and international disarmament campaigns.
