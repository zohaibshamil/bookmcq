📖 International Relations 1933-1939

Chapter Overview:

From 1933 to 1939, international relations deteriorated as Japan, Fascist Italy, and Nazi Germany pursued territorial expansion and challenged the postwar settlement. Japanese conquest in Manchuria and China, Mussolini's invasion of Ethiopia, and Hitler's rearmament and territorial ambitions undermined collective security. Britain and France adopted appeasement amid military, economic, and imperial constraints, while the Soviet Union pursued changing diplomatic relationships. The Munich Agreement, destruction of Czechoslovakia, and Nazi-Soviet Pact preceded Germany's invasion of Poland and the outbreak of war in Europe.

Background Knowledge Needed:

Students should understand the Treaty of Versailles, the League of Nations, and the limitations of collective security established after the First World War. Knowledge of Japanese occupation of Manchuria in 1931, the establishment of Manchukuo, the Great Depression, the rise of Italian Fascism, and Hitler's appointment as German Chancellor in 1933 is essential. Familiarity with German rearmament restrictions, the Stresa Front of 1935, French security concerns, British imperial interests, and Soviet diplomatic isolation provides the necessary background for understanding the international confrontations of the 1930s.

Learning Outcomes:

Students should be able to explain Japanese and Italian expansionism, analyse Hitler's foreign policy objectives, and identify the major diplomatic victories achieved by Nazi Germany between 1933 and 1938. They should compare British and French approaches to appeasement, explain the changing relationships among Germany, Italy, Japan, and the Soviet Union, and assess the significance of Munich. They should also evaluate Hitler's responsibility, the limitations of appeasement, and the Soviet role in the outbreak of the Second World War.

Periods and Themes Covered:

  • Japanese Expansion in Asia, 1931–1937 - Manchuria's occupation, Manchukuo, and the invasion of China.
  • Mussolini's Expansionist Foreign Policy - Mediterranean ambitions, Ethiopian invasion, and weakening relations with Western powers.
  • Hitler's Foreign Policy, 1933–1936 - Rearmament, treaty violations, Rhineland remilitarisation, and diplomatic opportunities.
  • Germany's Diplomatic Successes, 1936–1938 - Rome-Berlin alignment, Anschluss, and growing European influence.
  • Appeasement and the Munich Agreement, 1938 - British-French concessions, Sudeten demands, and Czechoslovak territorial disintegration.
  • Collapse of European Security, 1939 - German occupation of Prague, Polish crisis, and failed diplomatic negotiations.
  • Soviet Diplomacy and Responsibility for War - German-Soviet agreement, territorial ambitions, and competing historical interpretations.

Suggested Study Sequence:

Begin with Japanese expansion in Manchuria and China to understand the weakening of international security in Asia. Examine Mussolini's foreign policy, the invasion of Ethiopia, and the resulting deterioration of Italy's relations with Britain and France. Next, trace Hitler's withdrawal from international restrictions, rearmament, Rhineland remilitarisation, and diplomatic victories. Study the development of the Rome-Berlin relationship and Axis cooperation before examining appeasement, the Anschluss, and the Munich Agreement. Conclude with the occupation of Czechoslovakia, the Polish crisis, Soviet diplomacy, the German-Soviet Pact, and the outbreak of war.

How the Developments Connect:

Japanese and Italian aggression exposed the League's inability to enforce collective security. Italy's conflict with Britain and France weakened the Stresa Front, creating opportunities for closer German-Italian cooperation. Hitler exploited international divisions through rearmament, territorial expansion, and successive diplomatic victories. Appeasement failed to prevent the destruction of Czechoslovakia. Meanwhile, unsuccessful Anglo-French-Soviet negotiations and mutual distrust preceded the German-Soviet Pact, allowing Hitler to attack Poland without immediate Soviet opposition.

Key Events Timeline:

On 3 October 1935, Mussolini invaded Ethiopia, weakening relations with Britain and France. On 7 March 1936, Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland. Germany annexed Austria in March 1938, followed by the Munich Agreement on 29–30 September, which transferred the Sudetenland to Germany. On 15 March 1939, German forces occupied Bohemia and Moravia. Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact on 23 August. German troops invaded Poland on 1 September; Britain and France declared war on 3 September 1939.

Why This Chapter Matters:

The collapse of international cooperation during the 1930s explains how regional aggression developed into a European war. Understanding appeasement, German expansion, Italian and Japanese ambitions, and Soviet diplomacy is essential for analysing the causes of the Second World War, the failure of the Versailles settlement, and the changing international balance of power.

Common Confusions in History:

Students sometimes claim that 'the Munich Agreement gave Germany all of Czechoslovakia'; it transferred the Sudetenland in 1938, while Germany occupied the Czech territories in March 1939. 'Appeasement began only with Chamberlain' overlooks earlier British and French concessions during the decade. Finally, 'the Soviet Union joined Germany as a permanent ideological ally' ignores their ideological hostility; the 1939 pact was a temporary non-aggression arrangement supported by separate territorial understandings.

Exam Focus Areas:

Common examination questions require arranging Japanese, Italian, and German acts of aggression chronologically and matching diplomatic agreements with their consequences. Students should explain the causes of appeasement, compare Chamberlain's and French policies, and assess the importance of Munich. Analytical questions frequently examine Hitler's responsibility, the Soviet-German Pact, the failure of collective security, and whether appeasement encouraged further German expansion or reflected the military and economic limitations facing Britain and France.

Chapter Summary:

International stability deteriorated as aggressive powers challenged existing borders and diplomatic agreements. British and French concessions failed to restrain Hitler, while Germany, Italy, and Japan increasingly cooperated against their international opponents. The destruction of Czechoslovakia demonstrated the failure of appeasement, and the Nazi-Soviet Pact removed a major obstacle to German expansion eastward. Germany's invasion of Poland finally transformed years of diplomatic confrontation into a European war.

Key Terms and Figures:

  • Manchukuo - The Japanese-controlled state established in Manchuria in 1932 following Japan's invasion of the region.
  • Abyssinian Crisis - The international confrontation caused by Italy's invasion of Ethiopia in October 1935.
  • Appeasement - The diplomatic policy of making concessions to aggressive powers in an attempt to avoid war.
  • Rome-Berlin Axis - The political alignment between Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany announced in November 1936.
  • Anschluss - Germany's annexation of Austria in March 1938.
  • Munich Agreement - The September 1938 agreement permitting Germany to annex Czechoslovakia's Sudetenland.
  • Adolf Hitler - German dictator whose rearmament, territorial expansion, and invasion of Poland led to war in Europe.
  • Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact - The German-Soviet non-aggression agreement of August 1939, accompanied by a secret protocol dividing spheres of influence in Eastern Europe.

Further Research:

  • League of Nations Archives, Geneva - Examine original diplomatic reports, sanctions records, and investigations concerning Japanese aggression in Manchuria and Italian aggression in Ethiopia.
  • Munich Agreement, 1938 - Read the original agreement, diplomatic correspondence, and British Cabinet records to investigate the negotiations involving Hitler, Chamberlain, Daladier, and Mussolini.
  • German Foreign Policy Documents, 1933–1939 - Search German diplomatic records concerning rearmament, the Rhineland, Austria, Czechoslovakia, and preparations for the invasion of Poland.
  • British National Archives - Explore Cabinet conclusions, Foreign Office correspondence, and Neville Chamberlain's diplomatic papers concerning appeasement and European security.
  • German-Soviet Pact, 1939 - Compare the published non-aggression agreement with its secret protocol concerning Poland and Eastern European territories through historical archival collections and the Avalon Project at Yale Law School.
  • Japanese Diplomatic and Military Records - Investigate the Mukden Incident, establishment of Manchukuo, Marco Polo Bridge Incident, and Japanese expansion in China through archival documents and contemporary reports.
  • Appeasement Historiography - Explore traditionalist, revisionist, and post-revisionist interpretations through academic research on British and French diplomatic decisions during the 1930s.
