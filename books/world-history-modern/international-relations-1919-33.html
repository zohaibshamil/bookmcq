📖 International relations 1919-33

International Relations, 1919–1933: Reparations and Reconciliation

International relations between 1919 and 1933 were shaped by the unsettled Versailles order, German reparations, French security concerns, Soviet diplomatic isolation, and the emergence of successor states in Central and Eastern Europe. Early confrontation gradually gave way to partial reconciliation through economic agreements, international conferences, and diplomatic cooperation during the 1920s. However, the Great Depression, renewed nationalism, and failed disarmament negotiations undermined international stability. These developments reveal the changing balance between reconciliation, national interests, economic recovery, and collective security during the interwar period.

Background: Versailles, Soviet Russia and the Successor States

Students should understand the First World War peace settlements, particularly the Treaty of Versailles and the treaties of Saint-Germain and Trianon. Knowledge of German reparations, the occupation of German territories, the Russian Revolution of 1917, the establishment of the Soviet government, and the formation of Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary, and Yugoslavia is essential. Familiarity with the League of Nations, Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, French concerns about German recovery, and American financial involvement in European reconstruction provides the necessary context.

Learning Outcomes: Interwar Conferences, Plans and Alliances

Students should be able to compare international relations during 1919–1923, 1924–1929, and 1930–1933; explain the significance of the Washington and Genoa Conferences; and assess the consequences of the Dawes and Young Plans. They should analyse French security policies, Franco-German reconciliation, Soviet diplomatic relations, and American isolationism. They should also explain how the Great Depression weakened international cooperation and evaluate the failure of the World Disarmament Conference.

Topics: Ruhr Occupation, Dawes Plan, Locarno and Disarmament

  • Postwar International Relations, 1919–1923 - Reparations, territorial disputes, French security, and continuing diplomatic confrontations.
  • Successor States and European Security - Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary, Poland, Yugoslavia, and regional alliances.
  • International Conferences, 1921–1922 - Washington naval agreements, Genoa negotiations, and the Treaty of Rapallo.
  • French, German, and Soviet Diplomacy - Security alliances, occupation policies, diplomatic recognition, and changing political relationships.
  • International Cooperation and Recovery, 1924–1929 - Dawes Plan, Locarno Treaties, Kellogg-Briand Pact, and Young Plan.
  • American Foreign Policy and Economic Relations - Isolationism, financial involvement, European reconstruction, and international commitments.
  • Depression and Diplomatic Breakdown, 1929–1933 - Economic crisis, protectionism, disarmament disputes, and weakening international cooperation.

Study Sequence: From Postwar Tensions to the Great Depression

Begin with the post-1919 settlement and examine why German reparations, French security demands, and new European borders created continuing disputes. Study the political and economic difficulties of the successor states before examining the Washington Naval Conference, Genoa Conference, and Treaty of Rapallo. Next, follow French-German relations from Ruhr occupation and confrontation to reconciliation through the Dawes Plan and Locarno Treaties. Examine Soviet relations with Britain and France alongside American foreign policy. Continue with the Kellogg-Briand Pact and Young Plan, then conclude with the Great Depression, disarmament negotiations, and the international breakdown of 1930–1933.

How Reparations, US Finance and European Security Interacted

The Versailles settlement created reparations disputes and encouraged France to construct security arrangements against Germany. The Ruhr occupation intensified confrontation, while the Dawes Plan provided financial relief and made reconciliation more practical. Locarno and the Kellogg-Briand Pact reflected growing confidence in diplomacy. Meanwhile, Soviet relations developed separately through recognition and cooperation with Germany. The Great Depression disrupted financial recovery, increased national rivalries, and undermined the willingness of governments to accept international limitations.

Timeline: Ruhr Crisis, Locarno and the Kellogg–Briand Pact

On 11 January 1923, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr after Germany was declared in default on timber deliveries. Berlin encouraged passive resistance, worsening economic disruption. Gustav Stresemann ended resistance on 26 September 1923. A committee chaired by Charles G. Dawes presented a reparations proposal in April 1924, which the London Conference adopted in August. The settlement eased the crisis and supported European recovery. In October 1925, the Locarno Treaties established new diplomatic guarantees for Germany's western borders, marking a movement towards reconciliation.

Why Interwar Diplomacy Failed to Secure Lasting Peace

Interwar diplomacy explains why the peace established in 1919 remained unstable despite several successful agreements. French security policies, Soviet isolation, successor-state difficulties, and American financial involvement shaped European politics. The collapse of economic cooperation after 1929 helps explain the failure of collective security and the international tensions that preceded the Second World War.

Common Misconceptions: US Isolationism and Locarno Guarantees

Students sometimes claim that 'the United States followed complete isolationism'; although it rejected League membership, it actively participated in naval diplomacy and European financial reconstruction. 'The Locarno Treaties guaranteed all German borders' is incorrect because their principal guarantees covered western frontiers, not Germany's eastern borders. Finally, 'the Dawes Plan cancelled German reparations'; it reorganised payments and provided loans, while the Young Plan subsequently reduced and rescheduled the total obligation.

Exam Focus: Conferences, Reparations Plans and Treaties

Important examination questions include arranging the Washington, Genoa, Locarno, and Kellogg-Briand agreements chronologically and matching diplomatic plans with their purposes. Students should compare the Dawes and Young Plans, explain the causes of the Ruhr occupation, and identify the objectives of French alliances. Other recurring questions examine Soviet-British relations, successor states, American isolationism, and the connection between the Great Depression and the failure of disarmament.

Summary: Fragile Reconciliation in the 1920s

Postwar international relations initially reflected unresolved territorial disputes, reparations, and competing security interests. Economic reconstruction and diplomatic negotiations encouraged a period of reconciliation during the middle 1920s, although political disagreements remained unresolved. The Great Depression weakened the financial foundations of cooperation, while national rivalries obstructed disarmament. By 1933, the international system established after the First World War had become increasingly vulnerable to economic instability, political extremism, and renewed aggression.

Key Diplomatic Plans, Treaties and Interwar Leaders

  • Dawes Plan - The 1924 arrangement that reorganised German reparations payments and introduced American financial assistance.
  • Young Plan - The 1929 settlement that reduced and rescheduled Germany's reparations obligations over a longer period.
  • Locarno Treaties - The 1925 agreements establishing guarantees for Germany's western frontiers and encouraging European reconciliation.
  • Treaty of Rapallo - The 1922 German-Soviet agreement restoring diplomatic relations and renouncing certain mutual financial and territorial claims.
  • Little Entente - The alliance system involving Czechoslovakia, Romania, and Yugoslavia, established to oppose Hungarian revisionism and Habsburg restoration.
  • Kellogg-Briand Pact - The 1928 international agreement in which signatory states renounced war as an instrument of national policy.
  • Gustav Stresemann - German foreign minister associated with the Dawes Plan, Locarno diplomacy, and improved relations with Western powers.
  • Aristide Briand - French statesman who promoted Franco-German reconciliation and international cooperation during the 1920s.

Primary Documents on Interwar Diplomacy and Reparations

  • Foreign Relations of the United States Historical Documents - Examine American diplomatic correspondence concerning the Washington Conference, European reparations, Soviet relations, and the Kellogg-Briand Pact.
  • League of Nations Archives, Geneva - Explore diplomatic correspondence, Assembly proceedings, economic reports, disarmament negotiations, and records concerning European successor states.
  • German History in Documents and Images - Search original documents relating to the Ruhr occupation, Treaty of Rapallo, Dawes Plan, Locarno agreements, and Weimar foreign policy.
  • British National Archives and Parliamentary Papers - Investigate British diplomatic relations with France, Germany, and the Soviet Union, including the 1924 recognition of the Soviet government.
  • Avalon Project at Yale Law School - Read original texts of the Versailles settlement, Treaty of Rapallo, Locarno agreements, and Kellogg-Briand Pact.
  • European Successor States and Interwar Diplomacy - Research original constitutional documents, minority treaties, diplomatic agreements, and archival materials concerning Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary, Poland, and Yugoslavia.
