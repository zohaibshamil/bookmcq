What is Rwandan Genocide 1994?

The Rwandan Genocide of 1994 was the systematic slaughter of approximately 800,000 Tutsis and moderate Hutus over 100 days, orchestrated by Hutu extremist leaders using state media and militias, occurring amid civil war and international inaction that allowed the atrocities to unfold unchecked.

📝 All Rwandan Genocide 1994 MCQs

Q1. In which month and year was President Juvénal Habyarimana assassinated?

A.Jan-94
B.Apr-94 ✅
C.Jul-94
D.Dec-94
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is B because the aircraft carrying President Juvénal Habyarimana was shot down in April \(1994\), killing him and the Burundian president. Option A (January \(1994\)) and option C (July \(1994\)) are incorrect dates, and option D (December \(1994\)) occurs after the genocide had ended. Knowing the exact timing helps students link the assassination to the rapid escalation of violence that followed, illustrating how a single event can trigger large‑scale conflict.

Q2. What is the name of the Tutsi‑dominated rebel group that began fighting the Rwandan government in 1990?

A.Interahamwe
B.Rwandese Patriotic Front (FPR) ✅
C.National Liberation Front
D.Rwanda Liberation Army
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is B because the Rwandese Patriotic Front (FPR) was formed by Tutsi exiles and launched an armed campaign in 1990. Interahamwe (A) was a Hutu militia, National Liberation Front (C) and Rwanda Liberation Army (D) are not historically accurate. Recognizing the FPR’s identity is essential for understanding the two‑sided nature of the conflict and why the war evolved from a political rebellion into a full‑scale genocide.

Q3. Which combination of factors most directly contributed to the 1994 genocide in Rwanda?

A.Economic downturn
B.Colonial legacy of ethnic classification
C.Foreign intervention
D.All of the above ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is D because the genocide resulted from a mix of an economic crisis, the colonial practice of classifying Hutu and Tutsi as distinct groups, and external influences that fueled extremist ideology. Option A isolates a single cause, option B ignores the political and foreign dimensions, and option C overlooks the deep‑rooted ethnic tensions. Recognizing the multi‑causal nature helps students appreciate how complex historical, economic, and external pressures can combine to produce mass violence.

Option A suggests they desired power sharing, which is opposite to their goal; option C implies a desire for foreign aid, which was not a primary motive; option D references elections, which were not the central issue. Understanding this rejection clarifies how political fear can motivate extremist actions leading to genocide.
