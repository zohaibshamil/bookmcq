📝 Political problems in African states (7 MCQs)

Postcolonial State Formation and Political Instability

Political problems in African states emerged from the difficult transition from colonial administration to independent government, particularly during the independence movements of the 1950s and 1960s. Newly established governments had to build national institutions across territories containing diverse communities and unevenly developed regions. Many inherited centralised administrations, limited democratic experience and security institutions designed to maintain colonial authority rather than encourage representative political participation.

Colonial Legacies, Institutional Weakness and Leadership Rivalries

Colonial systems often concentrated authority in governors and appointed officials, leaving limited experience of accountable national government. After independence, competition over state resources, regional representation and political leadership intensified. Cold War rivalries sometimes encouraged foreign governments to support particular leaders or military groups. Economic pressures, corruption, inequality and unresolved regional grievances further weakened institutions and created opportunities for authoritarian rule and military intervention.

Coups, One-Party Rule and the Struggle for Democratic Government

During the 1960s and 1970s, military coups removed elected governments in several countries, including Ghana in 1966 and Nigeria in 1966. Some civilian leaders established one-party systems, arguing that political unity was essential for development. During the 1980s and 1990s, economic crises and public pressure encouraged multiparty reforms. These transitions produced different outcomes, ranging from competitive elections to prolonged authoritarian governments.

Ghana's 1966 Coup and the Removal of Kwame Nkrumah

On 24 February 1966, military and police officers overthrew Ghanaian President Kwame Nkrumah while he was visiting China. The coup established the National Liberation Council and ended his one-party government. Economic difficulties, political repression and dissatisfaction within the armed forces contributed to opposition. Ghana's experience demonstrates how weak institutional safeguards and military involvement in politics could interrupt constitutional government after independence.

Consequences for Governance, Rights and Regional Cooperation

Military rule and authoritarian government frequently weakened accountability, restricted political opposition and encouraged corruption. Repeated changes of government disrupted development policies and reduced public confidence in political institutions. Some regimes also intensified regional divisions and human rights abuses. Since the 1990s, stronger electoral systems and regional democratic standards have contributed to political reform, although election-related violence, repression and unconstitutional transfers of power remain challenges.

Misconceptions About African Political Instability

'Every African country experienced military rule' - political systems have differed considerably. 'Colonialism alone explains every coup' - domestic rivalries, military interests and economic crises also contributed. 'Multiparty elections guarantee democracy' - competitive voting must be supported by independent courts, civil liberties and accountable institutions. These distinctions prevent students from treating Africa's political history as a single uniform experience.

Examining Coups, Authoritarianism and Democratic Transitions

Focus on the difference between military coups, one-party government, personal dictatorship and constitutional democracy. Recall Ghana's 1966 coup, Nigeria's 1966 military takeover and the multiparty transitions of the 1990s. Questions may connect political instability with colonial institutions, Cold War intervention, economic crises and human rights. Compare individual national experiences rather than assuming that all African governments followed the same political path.

Political Vocabulary of Postcolonial African States

  • Military coup - Forced seizure of government.
  • One-party state - System dominated by one legal party.
  • Authoritarianism - Concentration of political power.
  • Accountability - Responsibility to public institutions.
  • Multiparty system - Political competition among several parties.
  • Constitutional government - Rule under established legal principles.

📝 All Political problems in African states MCQs

Q1. If a newly independent African nation chooses a one‑party system to ensure stability, which outcome is most likely based on historical patterns?

A.Immediate economic growth
B.Reduced risk of military coups
C.Long‑term political repression ✅
D.Increased foreign investment
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Historical cases show that one‑party rule often brings short‑term order but tends to limit political freedoms, leading to repression over time. While stability may appear, the lack of legal opposition allows the ruling party to suppress dissent, creating an environment where citizens cannot voice grievances. This repression fuels resentment and can later erupt into unrest or coups, confirming that long‑term political repression is the most probable outcome.

Q2. A government fearing future coups must decide on a preventive strategy. Which approach most directly reduces the risk of a military takeover?

A.Introduce multiparty elections
B.Suppress dissent violently
C.Adopt Marxist ideology
D.Strengthen military loyalty ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Strengthening loyalty within the armed forces addresses the immediate source of coup risk: the military’s willingness to intervene. By building trust, offering incentives, and ensuring alignment with civilian leadership, the army is less likely to view a takeover as necessary. Other options either increase political tension or change ideological direction without affecting the core loyalty that determines whether officers will act against the government.

Q3. A one‑party state wishes to move toward democracy without provoking a coup. Which transition path is most likely to succeed?

A.Gradual political liberalization ✅
B.Immediate free elections
C.Military takeover
D.External intervention
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Gradual liberalization lets existing power structures adapt slowly, giving institutions time to develop checks and balances while reducing perceived threats among the military and elite. By incrementally expanding political space, opposition groups can organize peacefully, and the regime can retain control over the pace of change. Sudden reforms or external pressure often trigger alarm in the armed forces, increasing coup risk, whereas a measured approach minimizes instability.

Q4. Why did many African leaders after independence adopt Marxist ideas?

A.To attract Soviet aid ✅
B.To emulate European colonial powers
C.To maintain traditional tribal structures
D.To promote free‑market capitalism
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: During the Cold War, the Soviet Union offered financial, military, and technical assistance to countries that embraced Marxist ideology. African leaders seeking resources for nation‑building found this support attractive, especially when Western aid was limited or conditional. The promise of socialist development also resonated with anti‑colonial sentiments, making Soviet assistance a practical motive for adopting Marxist ideas.

Q5. How does inexperience with parliamentary democracy contribute to governmental corruption?

A.Leads to concentration of power
B.Encourages transparency
C.Increases foreign aid ✅
D.Reduces bureaucracy
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Leaders unfamiliar with parliamentary checks often centralize authority, bypassing mechanisms that distribute power and hold officials accountable. This concentration creates opportunities for personal gain, as oversight is weak and decision‑making is opaque. When corruption flourishes, public trust erodes, and resources meant for development are misused, reinforcing a cycle where inexperienced governance directly fuels corrupt practices.

Q6. In what year was President Nkrumah of Ghana removed by the army?

A.1960
B.1966 ✅
C.1970
D.1980
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: President Kwame Nkrumah was overthrown in a military coup in 1966. The coup followed two failed assassination attempts and reflected growing discontent with his one‑party rule. This date is a key historical marker for post‑colonial African politics, illustrating how military interventions became a common method for removing unpopular leaders during that era.

Q7. Which critique best challenges the claim that one‑party states in Kenya and Tanzania were successful because they provided stable and effective government?

A.Economic growth was unprecedented
B.Stability was achieved through suppression of dissent ✅
C.Military coups were absent
D.Foreign investors favored one‑party regimes
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: While these regimes delivered a degree of order, that stability often depended on limiting political freedoms and silencing opposition. The suppression of dissent prevented legitimate channels for grievance expression, meaning that stability was not a product of effective governance but of coercive control. This undermines the argument that the one‑party system’s success was solely due to its ability to deliver stable and effective administration.
