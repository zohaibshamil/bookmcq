What is Why was Kwame Nkrumah overthrown 1966?

Kwame Nkrumah was overthrown in 1966 due to growing discontent over his increasingly authoritarian rule, economic mismanagement that led to debt and shortages, suppression of political opposition, and resentment within the military and civil service toward his centralized power and socialist policies.

📝 All Why was Kwame Nkrumah overthrown 1966 MCQs

Q1. In what year did the military coup that removed Nkrumah occur?

A. 1964
B. 1965
C. 1966 ✅
D. 1967

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The coup that ended Nkrumah’s rule happened in 1966 while he was abroad. This date is widely recorded in Ghanaian history and marks the point when the army seized power. Options 1964 and 1965 refer to earlier events that did not involve a coup, and 1967 was after the new government had already been installed. Knowing the correct year helps place the overthrow in the broader Cold‑War timeline.

Q2. What was the primary economic factor that triggered the crisis leading to Nkrumah’s downfall?

A. Overreliance on cocoa export price decline ✅
B. High industrial output
C. Excess foreign aid
D. Low population growth

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Ghana’s economy depended heavily on cocoa earnings, so when world cocoa prices fell sharply, export revenues collapsed, creating a balance‑of‑payments deficit. This single factor strained the budget and forced the government to borrow abroad. High industrial output, excess aid, and low population growth were not the immediate triggers, making option A the clear cause of the economic shock.

Q3. How did Nkrumah’s shift toward a one‑party state undermine political stability?

A. It increased foreign investment
B. It suppressed opposition leading to unrest ✅
C. It improved governmental efficiency
D. It reduced corruption

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

By banning rival parties and imprisoning critics, Nkrumah removed peaceful channels for dissent, causing frustration among elites and the public. The lack of legal opposition created an atmosphere where grievances could not be aired, eventually provoking the military to intervene. Increased investment, efficiency, or reduced corruption would strengthen stability, not weaken it, so option B correctly describes the destabilising effect.

Q4. Why did the American corporation operating Ghana’s smelting plant prefer importing bauxite rather than using local ore?

A. Better quality abroad
B. Contractual obligations
C. Profit motive to import ✅
D. Lack of local expertise

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The company owned the plant and sought maximum profit, so it arranged to buy bauxite from its own supply chains abroad where costs were lower for the firm. This decision ignored Ghana’s own mineral wealth, undermining the intended industrial benefit. Contractual obligations or quality issues might be cited, but the dominant driver was the profit motive, making option C the most accurate explanation.

Q5. How did Nkrumah justify the cult of personality surrounding his leadership?

A. It distracted from economic issues
B. It created loyalty to the party
C. It promoted foreign aid
D. It unified people under a single leader ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Nkrumah argued that a single, charismatic figure could embody national aspirations better than abstract state institutions, helping citizens identify with a tangible symbol of progress. This rationale aimed to foster unity and motivate collective effort. The other options describe effects not claimed by Nkrumah, so the justification centered on unifying the population under one leader, which is option D.

Q6. What role did Cold‑War dynamics play in the United States’ support for the coup that removed Nkrumah?

A. Cold‑War anti‑communist stance ✅
B. Desire for oil resources
C. Trade rivalry with Britain
D. Personal animosity toward Nkrumah

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

During the 1960s, the United States sought to prevent the spread of Soviet influence in Africa. Nkrumah’s overtures to communist states alarmed Washington, prompting the CIA to back a coup that could install a more pro‑Western regime. While oil, trade, or personal factors might matter in other contexts, the primary driver here was the anti‑communist Cold‑War policy, making option A correct.

Q7. How did the 1969 constitution aim to prevent a return to dictatorship?

A. Strong executive powers
B. Separation of powers ✅
C. Single‑party rule
D. Indefinite terms for leaders

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The new constitution introduced checks and balances by dividing authority among the legislature, executive, and judiciary, ensuring no single branch could dominate. This structural safeguard directly addressed the previous abuse of power. A strong executive or single‑party system would concentrate power, while indefinite terms would remove regular accountability, so the separation of powers is the correct mechanism.

Q8. If Ghana’s cocoa price fell by 40% in 1965 and the budget deficit was one‑third of total revenue, which policy would most directly ease the balance‑of‑payments crisis?

A. Increase import tariffs
B. Devalue the currency
C. Diversify exports ✅
D. Cut public‑sector wages

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

A sharp drop in cocoa earnings reduces foreign exchange, so expanding the range of export commodities lessens reliance on a single product and improves trade balance. Raising tariffs or devaluing the currency might affect imports but do not generate new earnings, and wage cuts could worsen domestic demand. Diversifying exports directly tackles the shortage of foreign currency, making option C the best choice.

Q9. Which combination of policies would help a modern government avoid the pitfalls of Nkrumah’s rapid industrialisation strategy?

A. Rapid large‑scale projects funded by foreign loans
B. Gradual investment with domestic resource use ✅
C. Rely solely on commodity exports
D. Privatise all infrastructure

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A cautious approach that matches investment pace with available domestic capital reduces debt risk and ensures projects are economically viable. This contrasts with Nkrumah’s quick, heavily borrowed ventures that later proved unsustainable. Funding large projects without local capacity, depending only on raw commodity sales, or fully privatizing infrastructure each carry distinct drawbacks, so option B offers the balanced path.

Q10. A low‑GDP‑per‑capita country with high debt is planning a ten‑mile highway similar to the Accra‑Tema road. Which economic principle suggests the project may be unsustainable?

A. Opportunity cost ✅
B. Comparative advantage
C. Law of diminishing returns
D. Purchasing power parity

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Opportunity cost highlights that scarce resources devoted to the highway could be used for more urgent needs such as health or education. When a nation already struggles financially, allocating funds to a costly road may forego essential investments, making the project economically questionable. Comparative advantage, diminishing returns, and PPP relate to other aspects of trade and price levels, not directly to the budgeting dilemma.

Q11. Which institutional feature best embodies the “checks and balances” promise in the new constitution after the 1966 coup?

A. Unicameral legislature
B. Independent judiciary ✅
C. Single‑party rule
D. Centralized executive

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

An independent judiciary can review and, if necessary, invalidate actions by the executive or legislature, preventing any single branch from becoming authoritarian. This separation is the core of checks and balances. A single‑party system or a centralized executive concentrates power, while a unicameral legislature alone does not provide external oversight, so option B correctly captures the safeguard.

Q12. When a leader bans all opposition parties and arrests critics without trial, which democratic principle is most violated?

A. Majority rule
B. Freedom of speech ✅
C. Separation of church and state
D. Federalism

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Freedom of speech guarantees the right to express dissenting opinions and to organize politically. Banning parties and imprisoning opponents eliminates that right, undermining democratic discourse. Majority rule, church‑state separation, and federalism address other aspects of governance, but the core violation here is the suppression of free expression, making option B the accurate answer.

Q13. Foreign investors demand that a national mining company purchase raw material from abroad. What economic risk does this represent for the host country?

A. Currency risk
B. Resource nationalism
C. Trade dependency ✅
D. Inflation

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Requiring the domestic firm to import its own raw material creates dependence on external markets, exposing the country to price fluctuations and supply disruptions beyond its control. This trade dependency can erode the intended benefits of nationalizing resources. Currency risk, nationalism, and inflation are distinct concerns, but the scenario directly illustrates a dependency risk, so option C is correct.

Q14. A country faces a sharp decline in its main export price, similar to Ghana’s cocoa drop. Which macroeconomic tool could stabilize the economy without increasing external debt?

A. Expansionary fiscal policy financed by loans
B. Tight monetary policy
C. Development of value‑added processing industries ✅
D. Fixed exchange rate

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Investing in value‑added processing turns raw exports into higher‑priced finished goods, diversifying income sources and reducing vulnerability to commodity price swings without borrowing more money. Expansionary fiscal measures financed by loans would raise debt, tight monetary policy could stifle growth, and a fixed exchange rate might worsen the balance‑of‑payments problem, so option C offers the most sustainable solution.
