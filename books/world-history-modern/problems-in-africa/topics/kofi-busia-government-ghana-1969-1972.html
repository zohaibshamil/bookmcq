📝 Kofi Busia government Ghana 1969-1972 (8 MCQs)

Kofi Busia and Ghana's Return to Civilian Government

Kofi Abrefa Busia became prime minister of Ghana in September 1969 after elections ended the National Liberation Council's military administration. His Progress Party won a large parliamentary majority, and his government represented Ghana's return to civilian constitutional rule after the overthrow of Kwame Nkrumah in 1966. Busia's political programme emphasised liberal economic policies, administrative reform and a parliamentary system.

The Political Transition and Economic Conditions of 1969

The National Liberation Council supervised the constitutional transition and transferred authority to an elected government. Ghana nevertheless faced serious economic difficulties, including foreign debt, balance-of-payments pressures, inflation and dependence on cocoa exports. Busia inherited a weakened economy and attempted to restore confidence through spending controls and market-oriented reform. His policies were controversial because their costs affected households and businesses.

Government Policies, Foreign Residents and Economic Reform

Busia's administration introduced the Aliens Compliance Order in 1969, requiring undocumented foreign residents to regularise their status or leave Ghana. In 1971, the government devalued the currency as part of its effort to address economic imbalances. It also pursued austerity measures and administrative changes. These decisions aimed to improve economic conditions but generated domestic criticism and social hardship.

The 1971 Devaluation and Growing Political Opposition

Ghana's devaluation in December 1971 increased the domestic cost of imported goods and contributed to public dissatisfaction. Trade unions, workers and political opponents criticised the government's economic direction. The administration also faced difficulties implementing reforms amid declining confidence. On 13 January 1972, Lieutenant Colonel Ignatius Kutu Acheampong led a military coup that removed Busia while he was outside Ghana.

The Fall of Busia's Government and Its Historical Significance

Busia's removal ended Ghana's short-lived Second Republic and returned the country to military rule under the National Redemption Council. His experience exposed the vulnerability of civilian governments facing economic crisis, unpopular austerity and a politically powerful military. Although his administration had restored parliamentary government, it could not establish durable constitutional stability or prevent another military intervention.

Misconceptions About Busia's Administration

'Busia took power immediately after independence' - he became prime minister in 1969, twelve years after independence. 'The Aliens Compliance Order was an economic development programme' - it concerned the residence and documentation of foreign nationals. 'Busia left office through an election' - Acheampong removed him in a military coup in January 1972.

Examining Ghana's Second Republic, 1969–1972

Recall Busia's Progress Party, the September 1969 transition, the Aliens Compliance Order and the December 1971 devaluation. Connect economic austerity and public dissatisfaction with the military's political influence. Questions may compare Busia's civilian government with Nkrumah's earlier administration and Acheampong's subsequent military rule. The coup date, 13 January 1972, is especially important.

Political and Economic Terms of Busia's Government

  • Kofi Busia - Ghanaian prime minister from 1969 to 1972.
  • Progress Party - Busia's governing political party.
  • Aliens Compliance Order - 1969 foreign-resident regulation.
  • Devaluation - Reduction in a currency's official value.
  • Austerity - Policies restricting public expenditure.
  • Second Republic - Ghana's 1969–1972 constitutional government.

Q1. When was Kofi Busia overthrown by the army?

A.Dec-71 ✅
B.Jan-72
C.Mar-73
D.Jun-74
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is January 1972 because historical records show that the coup occurred while Busia was in London. Option A is wrong because the overthrow did not happen in 1971, and Option C and D are incorrect dates that do not match the event. Knowing the exact timing helps understand the rapid political turnover in Ghana and why the military acted swiftly to replace the civilian government.

Q2. Which factor most directly undermined Kofi Busia's ability to keep political stability?

A.Strong tribal unity
B.High foreign aid
C.Low unemployment
D.Dependence on army support combined with severe economic crises ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Option D is correct because Busia’s legitimacy relied on the army’s consent while he faced soaring unemployment, rising prices, low cocoa revenues, and large debt burdens, creating an unstable environment. Option A is opposite; tribal unity would aid stability. Option B is inaccurate as foreign aid was limited and did not offset fiscal problems. Option C misstates the situation because unemployment was actually rising, not low. The combination of military dependence and economic strain explains why his government collapsed.

Q3. How did the near‑50 % devaluation of Ghana's currency in 1971 affect the government's fiscal capacity?

A.Reduced the real value of export earnings
B.Increased foreign investment ✅
C.Lowered debt repayments
D.Stabilized domestic prices
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is that devaluation reduced the real value of export earnings because the currency lost almost half its value, making foreign‑denominated revenues worth less in local terms. Option B is wrong; devaluation typically discourages investment due to uncertainty. Option C misinterprets the effect; debt obligations remain the same, so repayments become harder, not easier. Option D suggests price stability, but devaluation often fuels inflation. Understanding this fiscal impact shows why Busia’s budget was squeezed.

Q4. What was a major consequence of allocating roughly \(40\%\) of export profits to debt repayment?

A.Boosted infrastructure development
B.Created a fiscal surplus
C.Limited funds for domestic development projects ✅
D.Reduced unemployment rates
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Option C is correct because directing four‑tenths of export earnings to service debt left only a small share for public spending, curbing investment in schools, roads, and health care. Option A is false; infrastructure suffered due to the lack of resources. Option B is incorrect because the budget was strained, not surplus. Option D misstates the effect; unemployment rose as economic growth stalled. This constraint illustrates why Busia’s administration faced public dissatisfaction.

Q5. If Ghana's export profits total \(100\) million dollars and 40 % must be used for debt repayment, how much money is allocated to debt?

A.\(40\) million
B.\(20\) million ✅
C.\(60\) million
D.\(80\) million
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The correct answer is \(40\) million because 40 % of \(100\) million equals \(0.40 \times 100 = 40\) million dollars. Option B calculates 20 % instead of 40 %. Option C uses 60 % and Option D uses 80 %, both overstating the amount. This straightforward calculation shows how a large share of export earnings can be consumed by debt service, a key factor in Busia’s fiscal challenges.

Q6. A government wants to cut debt payments to 25 % of export profits while keeping import restrictions at 10 %. Which policy would most likely achieve this goal?

A.Increase cocoa price through subsidies ✅
B.Reduce military spending
C.Raise taxes on agriculture
D.Renegotiate debt terms with creditors
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Renegotiating debt terms is the only option that directly lowers the proportion of export profits needed for repayment, allowing the share to fall to 25 %. Raising cocoa prices (A) may raise revenue but does not change the debt proportion. Cutting military spending (B) frees budget space but does not affect debt ratios. Raising taxes on agriculture (C) could reduce growth and export capacity. Therefore, D is the most effective strategy to adjust the debt burden.

Q7. If tribal conflicts cause a 15 % drop in tax revenue, how would this impact the ability to meet a debt obligation that requires 40 % of export profits?

A.No impact because debt is fixed
B.Likely make it harder to meet payments ✅
C.Improves ability due to lower spending
D.Forces increase in export volume
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A 15 % reduction in tax revenue shrinks the overall fiscal resources, meaning the government has less flexibility to allocate funds toward debt service, which still demands 40 % of export earnings. Therefore, meeting the obligation becomes more difficult, making option B correct. Option A is wrong because debt payments depend on available funds, not a fixed amount. Option C incorrectly assumes lower spending helps debt payment, and option D misinterprets the need for higher exports without addressing revenue shortfalls.

Q8. If export composition shifts from 80 % cocoa to 50 % cocoa, what effect does this have on vulnerability to world cocoa price fluctuations?

A.Increases vulnerability ✅
B.No change in vulnerability
C.Decreases vulnerability
D.Turns Ghana into a net importer of cocoa
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Reducing cocoa’s share from 80 % to 50 % spreads export earnings across more products, so a fall in cocoa prices will affect a smaller portion of total revenue, thereby decreasing vulnerability. Option A is opposite, suggesting higher risk. Option B ignores the diversification benefit. Option D misstates the outcome; Ghana would still export cocoa, not become a net importer. Thus, C correctly captures how diversification mitigates price‑shock exposure.
