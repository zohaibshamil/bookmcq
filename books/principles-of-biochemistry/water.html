📖 Water

How Water Supports Biological Chemistry

Water supplies the continuous medium in which biomolecules dissolve, interact, and react, but its role extends beyond simple solvent action. Its polarity and hydrogen-bonding capacity produce weak interactions that shape molecular associations and the hydrophobic effect. Reversible ionization establishes the basis of pH; weak acids and bases determine proton balance, while buffers limit abrupt changes. Water also participates directly in reactions such as hydrolysis. These properties explain why aqueous conditions support cellular chemistry.

How Ionization Determines Buffer Response

Water ionization establishes the relationship between hydronium and hydroxide concentrations, providing the chemical basis for pH. Weak-acid dissociation introduces Ka and pKa, which describe proton release and acid strength. These equilibria explain how conjugate acid-base pairs operate in buffers. Hydrogen bonding and other weak interactions influence molecular behavior, while water's participation in hydrolysis extends its role from reaction medium to chemical reactant.

Carbon Dioxide and the Bicarbonate Buffer

In blood, carbon dioxide from respiring tissues enters red blood cells and reacts with water: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3. Carbonic acid ionizes to H+ and HCO3−, so the bicarbonate pair can absorb or release protons. At blood pH near 7.4, the bicarbonate-to-dissolved-CO2 ratio is approximately 20:1, using an apparent pKa near 6.1. Removing CO2 in the lungs shifts the equilibrium and helps restore pH. This connects ionization, weak-acid equilibrium, buffering, and water as a reactant.

Why Aqueous Chemistry Controls Cell Function

Water's interactions govern protein folding, molecular recognition, and the behavior of membranes and dissolved metabolites. pH control is essential because changes in proton concentration alter biomolecular charge and reaction rates. These principles underpin later study of enzyme mechanisms, protein structure, metabolic pathways, and cellular homeostasis.

Distinguishing Neutrality, Buffering, and Hydrolysis

'Neutral pH is always 7' is incorrect: neutrality means equal hydronium and hydroxide concentrations, and their values depend on temperature. 'A buffer keeps pH constant' overstates its role; buffers resist change only within their capacity. 'Hydrolysis is just dissolving in water' is wrong because hydrolysis uses water to break a covalent bond, unlike simple dissolution.

pH Calculations and Buffer Equilibrium Questions

Expect pH calculations from hydronium concentration, comparisons of Ka and pKa, and predictions about weak-acid ionization. Buffer questions may ask how added H+ or OH− changes conjugate-pair amounts. Other tasks test recognition of hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic effects, and hydrolysis equations, as well as explanations of why water's properties favor cellular reactions.

Water's Role in Molecular Life

Water's polarity and hydrogen bonding create a medium for selective molecular interactions. Self-ionization and weak-acid equilibria establish proton concentrations, while conjugate pairs stabilize pH against limited disturbances. Because water can also be a reactant, it participates in chemical transformations rather than merely surrounding them. Together, these properties make aqueous conditions central to living systems.

Essential Terms for Aqueous Biochemistry

  • Hydrogen bond - A weak attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to an electronegative atom and another electronegative atom.
  • Hydrophobic effect - The tendency of nonpolar substances to associate in water, reducing their exposure to the aqueous environment.
  • Hydronium ion - The H3O+ ion formed when a water molecule accepts a proton.
  • Ion product of water (Kw) - The equilibrium product of hydronium and hydroxide concentrations in water.
  • pKa - The negative logarithm of an acid's dissociation constant, indicating its tendency to release protons.
  • Conjugate acid-base pair - Two chemical species that differ by one proton.
  • Buffer capacity - The amount of added acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH changes substantially.
  • Hydrolysis - A chemical reaction in which water participates in breaking a covalent bond.
