📝 Understanding Graphs of Linear Inequalities in Two Variables (40 MCQs)

Linear Inequalities as Regions of the Coordinate Plane

A linear inequality in two variables describes a collection of ordered pairs rather than a single straight line. Expressions such as y>mx+by>mx+by>mx+b and Ax+By≤CAx+By\le CAx+By≤C specify conditions that coordinate values must satisfy. Their graphical representations consist of a linear boundary and a region on one side of that boundary. This provides a geometric interpretation of algebraic comparisons and distinguishes inequalities from ordinary linear equations.

Why an Inequality Produces a Half-Plane

A linear equation identifies coordinates at which two expressions are equal, producing a straight-line graph. An inequality permits coordinates for which one expression is strictly greater, greater than or equal to, or satisfies another comparison. These coordinates generally occupy an entire half-plane. Plotting only the boundary would therefore omit most solutions and fail to represent the complete mathematical relationship.

Interpreting Boundary Inclusion and the Shading Condition

Replace the inequality symbol with equality to obtain the boundary equation. Use a solid line for ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥, because equality is included, and a dashed line for <<< or >>>. To determine the correct side, substitute a test point not on the boundary, commonly (0,0)(0,0)(0,0), into the original inequality. Shade the side for which the comparison is true.

Worked Interpretation of the Inequality y>2x−1y>2x-1y>2x−1

Consider y>2x−1y>2x-1y>2x−1. Draw the boundary y=2x−1y=2x-1y=2x−1 as a dashed line because equality is excluded. Substitute (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) into the inequality to obtain 0>−10>-10>−1, which is true. Therefore, shade the side containing the origin. The resulting half-plane contains every solution but excludes all points on its boundary.

Using Inequality Regions to Represent Constraints

Linear inequalities represent practical restrictions involving budgets, available materials, production capacities, and minimum requirements. Their graphs display all permissible combinations of two quantities. Several inequalities can be combined to identify an overlapping feasible region. The mathematical model must accurately reflect the stated restrictions, and additional conditions such as nonnegative or whole-number quantities may reduce the solutions that are practically acceptable.

Interpreting Solid Lines, Dashed Lines, and Shading

'Every boundary is solid' ignores strict inequalities, which exclude equality and require dashed boundaries. 'Shading above always means greater than' is unreliable without considering the equation's arrangement; testing a coordinate establishes the correct side. 'Every point on the boundary is a solution' is false for strict comparisons because points satisfying equality do not satisfy <<< or >>>.

Reading and Constructing Graphical Inequality Representations

Problems may require selecting the correct graph, identifying a region, or determining whether a coordinate satisfies a linear constraint. Establish the boundary first, interpret whether equality is included, and test an appropriate point. Desmos can display shaded regions and compare candidate graphs. Algebraic substitution remains necessary for distinguishing the precise solution set from a visually approximate representation.

Terminology of Linear Inequality Graphs

  • Linear inequality - comparison of linear expressions.
  • Boundary line - line separating two regions.
  • Half-plane - one side of a straight line.
  • Strict inequality - comparison excluding equality.
  • Inclusive inequality - comparison allowing equality.
  • Test point - coordinate used to determine shading.
