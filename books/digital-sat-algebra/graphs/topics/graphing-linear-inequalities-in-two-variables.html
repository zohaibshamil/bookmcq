📝 Graphing Linear Inequalities in Two Variables (33 MCQs)

Representing Algebraic Inequalities on the Coordinate Plane

Graphing a linear inequality in two variables converts an algebraic comparison into a geometric region containing all satisfying ordered pairs. A typical inequality, such as y>mx+by>mx+by>mx+b, produces a straight boundary and a shaded half-plane. The inequality symbol determines whether points on the boundary are included, while the comparison determines which side contains the solutions. This process extends the graphing of linear equations to a complete set of permissible coordinates.

Why a Complete Graph Requires Both a Line and Shading

An equation identifies coordinates where two expressions have equal values, but an inequality describes a larger collection of coordinates satisfying a comparison. Drawing only the boundary would omit the points lying on the valid side. Shading identifies these additional solutions, while solid and dashed line styles distinguish inclusive and strict comparisons. Both graphical features are therefore mathematically necessary.

Rearranging the Inequality and Following Graphing Conditions

Rearrange the inequality into a convenient form, such as y>mx+by>mx+by>mx+b, then graph the corresponding equality as its boundary. Use a solid line for ≤\le≤ or ≥\ge≥, and a dashed line for <<< or >>>. Select a test point outside the boundary and evaluate the original inequality. Reverse the comparison sign whenever multiplying or dividing by a negative number.

Worked Graph of 2x+y>42x+y>42x+y>4

Rearrange 2x+y>42x+y>42x+y>4 into y>−2x+4y>-2x+4y>−2x+4. Draw y=−2x+4y=-2x+4y=−2x+4 as a dashed boundary because equality is excluded. Testing (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) gives 0>40>40>4, which is false. Therefore, shade the side opposite the origin. Every point within that shaded half-plane satisfies the original inequality, but boundary points do not.

Applications of Graphing Constraints and Permissible Values

Linear inequality graphs represent restrictions involving budgets, available resources, production levels, and minimum or maximum requirements. When several restrictions apply simultaneously, their overlapping shaded areas identify combinations satisfying every condition. These continuous regions may require additional restrictions for real applications, such as nonnegative or integer quantities. The graph therefore provides a visual foundation for analyzing constrained mathematical models.

Preventing Sign Reversal and Boundary Errors

'Dividing by a negative leaves the inequality unchanged' is incorrect because negative multiplication or division reverses the comparison sign. 'A strict inequality has a solid boundary' contradicts its definition; strict comparisons require dashed boundaries. 'Shading can be chosen without testing a point' risks selecting the wrong half-plane; substitution into the original inequality establishes the correct region reliably.

Constructing Accurate Graphs from Algebraic Conditions

Problems may provide an inequality in standard form, require its graph, or ask which region satisfies several restrictions. Isolate a variable when convenient, identify the boundary, determine whether equality is included, and test an appropriate coordinate. A graphing calculator or Desmos can display the region and verify the result, while careful algebra ensures correct signs and boundary conditions.

Terminology of Linear Inequality Graphing

  • Linear inequality - comparison involving linear expressions.
  • Boundary line - line separating solution regions.
  • Half-plane - one side of a boundary line.
  • Strict inequality - comparison excluding equality.
  • Inclusive inequality - comparison allowing equality.
  • Feasible region - area satisfying specified restrictions.
