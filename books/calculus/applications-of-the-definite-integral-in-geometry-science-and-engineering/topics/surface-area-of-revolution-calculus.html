📝 Surface area of revolution calculus

What is Surface area of revolution calculus?

Definition:
Surface area of a solid of revolution is found by rotating a curve around an axis. For rotation about the x-axis, \( S = 2\pi \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2} \, dx \). This integrates the circumference of infinitesimal bands multiplied by their slant height.

Example:
Rotate \( y=x \) from \( x=0 \) to \( 1 \) about x-axis. \( f'(x)=1 \). Solution: \( S = 2\pi \int_{0}^{1} x \sqrt{2} \, dx = 2\pi\sqrt{2} [\frac{x^2}{2}]_0^1 = \pi\sqrt{2} \).

Reason:
This calculation is essential for determining the amount of material needed to coat or cover rotational surfaces, such as paint for tanks or insulation for pipes, ensuring cost-effective resource allocation.

Q1. A surface of revolution is generated by revolving the curve \(y = x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\) about the x-axis. A student proposes using the formula \(S = \int_0^2 2\pi x \sqrt{1+4x^2} dx\). What is the primary error in this setup?

A. The limits of integration are incorrect
B. The radius of revolution should be \(x^2\), not \(x\) ✅
C. The differential element \(dx\) is incorrect
D. The derivative term is incorrect

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The surface area formula for revolution about the x-axis is \(S = \int 2\pi y \sqrt{1+(y')^2} dx\). Since the curve is \(y=x^2\), the radius of revolution is the distance from the x-axis to the curve, which is \(y=x^2\), not \(x\). The student has mistakenly used the independent variable as the radius, which is the formula for revolution about the y-axis. This is a common misconception that mixes the two axis formulas. The correct integrand should be \(2\pi x^2 \sqrt{1+4x^2}\).

Q2. Which of the following correctly expresses the surface area generated by revolving the curve \(x = y^2\) from \(y=0\) to \(y=2\) about the y-axis?

A. \(\int_0^2 2\pi y^2 \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\) ✅
B. \(\int_0^2 2\pi y \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\)
C. \(\int_0^2 2\pi x \sqrt{1+4x^2} dx\)
D. \(\int_0^2 2\pi \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

When revolving about the y-axis with \(x\) as a function of \(y\), the formula is \(S = \int_c^d 2\pi x \sqrt{1+(dx/dy)^2} dy\). Since \(x = y^2\), we have \(dx/dy = 2y\). Substituting gives \(S = \int_0^2 2\pi (y^2) \sqrt{1+(2y)^2} dy = \int_0^2 2\pi y^2 \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\). The radius of revolution is the distance from the y-axis to the curve, which is \(x = y^2\). Option B incorrectly uses y as the radius, which would be for revolution about the x-axis.

Q3. A curve \(y = f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) is revolved about the x-axis. If the arc length of the curve is \(L\), and \(f(x)\) is always between \(m\) and \(M\) (where \(0 < m < M\)), which inequality correctly bounds the surface area \(S\)?

A. \(2\pi m L \le S \le 2\pi M L\) ✅
B. \(m L \le S \le M L\)
C. \(\pi m L \le S \le \pi M L\)
D. \(2\pi m \le S \le 2\pi M\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The surface area is \(S = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1+(f'(x))^2} dx\). Since \(m \le f(x) \le M\), we can bound the integrand: \(2\pi m \sqrt{1+(f')^2} \le 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1+(f')^2} \le 2\pi M \sqrt{1+(f')^2}\). Integrating gives \(2\pi m L \le S \le 2\pi M L\), where \(L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+(f')^2} dx\). Option B misses the factor of \(2\pi\), Option C uses \(\pi\), and Option D omits the arc length factor, all of which are common errors in applying the inequalities.

Q4. A surface is generated by revolving the curve \(y = \sin x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=\pi\) about the x-axis. A student evaluating the integral for surface area uses the substitution \(u = \cos x\) and obtains \(S = -2\pi \int_1^{-1} \sqrt{1+u^2} du\). What is the error?

A. The substitution should be \(u = \sin x\)
B. The limits of integration are reversed, but the integral is otherwise correct
C. The factor \(2\pi\) is missing a \(y\) term
D. The derivative \(dy/dx = \cos x\) was not substituted correctly ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The surface area formula is \(S = \int_0^\pi 2\pi \sin x \sqrt{1+\cos^2 x} dx\). If we let \(u = \cos x\), then \(du = -\sin x dx\). The integral becomes \(S = -2\pi \int_{u=1}^{-1} \sqrt{1+u^2} du\). The student's setup would be correct if they had included the \(\sin x\) term in the substitution and changed the limits correctly. The error is not in the limits (they are correctly reversed in the student's work), but the student likely forgot the \(\sin x\) factor, which is necessary for the substitution. The given expression \(-2\pi \int_1^{-1} \ldots du\) is correct if the \(\sin x\) was accounted for.

Q5. A surface of revolution is generated by revolving the curve \(y = \frac{1}{3}x^3\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) about the x-axis. What is the most efficient method to evaluate the resulting integral?

A. Substitute \(u = 1 + x^4\) ✅
B. Integrate by parts
C. Use the substitution \(u = x^2\)
D. Use a trigonometric substitution

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For \(y = \frac{1}{3}x^3\), \(dy/dx = x^2\). The surface area is \(S = \int_0^1 2\pi (\frac{1}{3}x^3) \sqrt{1+x^4} dx = \frac{2\pi}{3}\int_0^1 x^3 \sqrt{1+x^4} dx\). The most straightforward approach is to let \(u = 1+x^4\), so \(du = 4x^3 dx\). The integral becomes \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\int \sqrt{u} du = \frac{\pi}{6}\int \sqrt{u} du\), which is easily evaluated. The other options would complicate the integral unnecessarily. This question tests the ability to identify the most appropriate integration technique based on the form of the integrand.

Q6. If the curve \(y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\) is revolved about the x-axis, it generates a sphere. The formula for surface area yields \(S = \int_{-r}^r 2\pi \sqrt{r^2-x^2} \cdot \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}} dx = 4\pi r^2\). A student claims the integrand simplifies to \(2\pi r\), so the integral is \(2\pi r \int_{-r}^r dx = 4\pi r^2\). Is this reasoning valid?

A. Yes, the simplification is algebraically correct and yields the correct result. ✅
B. No, the simplification is incorrect because \(\sqrt{r^2-x^2} \cdot \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}} \ne r\).
C. Yes, but only if \(x \ge 0\).
D. No, because the integral should be evaluated from 0 to r.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The simplification is valid: \(\sqrt{r^2-x^2} \cdot \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}} = r\) (provided the denominator is not zero, which only occurs at the endpoints). The integral becomes \(S = \int_{-r}^r 2\pi r dx = 4\pi r^2\). The student's reasoning is sound. The point of the question is to test whether the student can see the validity of the simplification, or if they think there is a hidden error. This is a common point of confusion, as students often struggle with simplifying radical expressions in the context of surface area.

Q7. A cone of height \(h\) and base radius \(r\) is generated by revolving the line segment \(y = \frac{r}{h}x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=h\) about the x-axis. What is the lateral surface area of the cone?

A. \(\pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}\)
B. \(\pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}\) ✅
C. \(\pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}\)
D. \(\frac{1}{2}\pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For a cone generated by a line, the surface area formula simplifies to the known geometric formula. Here, \(f(x) = \frac{r}{h}x\), so \(f'(x) = \frac{r}{h}\). The area is \(S = \int_0^h 2\pi (\frac{r}{h}x) \sqrt{1+(\frac{r}{h})^2} dx = 2\pi \frac{r}{h}\sqrt{1+\frac{r^2}{h^2}} \int_0^h x dx = 2\pi \frac{r}{h}\cdot \frac{\sqrt{h^2+r^2}}{h} \cdot \frac{h^2}{2} = \pi r \sqrt{r^2+h^2}\). Option A is the same result, but with a typo in the presentation. The correct answer is \(\pi r \sqrt{r^2+h^2}\), which matches the formula for the lateral area of a cone.

Q8. Two curves, \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = g(x)\), on \([a,b]\) have the same arc length. If they are revolved about the x-axis and \(f(x) > g(x) > 0\), which statement must be true about their surface areas \(S_f\) and \(S_g\)?

A. \(S_f > S_g\) ✅
B. \(S_f < S_g\)
C. \(S_f = S_g\)
D. The relationship cannot be determined from the given information.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The surface area is \(S = \int 2\pi y \sqrt{1+(y')^2} dx\). The arc length \(L = \int \sqrt{1+(y')^2} dx\) is the same for both. Since \(f(x) > g(x) > 0\), the integrand for \(S_f\) is \(2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1+(f')^2}\), which is greater than \(2\pi g(x) \sqrt{1+(g')^2}\) pointwise. Since the square root terms may differ, but the arc length is the same, the pointwise inequality on the function values is sufficient to conclude the integral of \(S_f\) is greater. Thus, the surface area of the curve with the larger function values will be larger. This tests the understanding of the formula as an integral of a product of the function value and the arc length element.

Q9. A surface of revolution is formed by revolving the curve \(x = y^2\) from \(y=0\) to \(y=2\) about the y-axis. A student mistakenly uses the formula for revolution about the x-axis. What integral would the student incorrectly write?

A. \(\int_0^2 2\pi y^2 \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\)
B. \(\int_0^4 2\pi x \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}} dx\)
C. \(\int_0^2 2\pi y \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\) ✅
D. \(\int_0^4 2\pi \sqrt{x} \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}} dx\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

If a student incorrectly uses the formula for revolution about the x-axis, they would treat \(y\) as the function and \(dy/dx\) as the derivative. First, they would need to express \(y\) as a function of \(x\): \(y = \sqrt{x}\), from \(x=0\) to \(x=4\). Then \(dy/dx = 1/(2\sqrt{x})\). The incorrect x-axis formula would be \(S = \int_0^4 2\pi y \sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^2} dx = \int_0^4 2\pi \sqrt{x} \sqrt{1+1/(4x)} dx\). This is not one of the options. However, option C is \(\int_0^2 2\pi y \sqrt{1+4y^2} dy\), which is the result of using \(2\pi y\) (as if revolving about the x-axis) but with \(y\) as the variable of integration. This is the most common mistake: using the radius as \(y\) when integrating with respect to \(y\).

Q10. Which of the following integrals represents the surface area generated by revolving the curve \(y = \ln x\) from \(x=1\) to \(x=e\) about the y-axis?

A. \(\int_1^e 2\pi \ln x \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}} dx\)
B. \(\int_0^1 2\pi e^y \sqrt{1+e^{2y}} dy\) ✅
C. \(\int_0^1 2\pi y \sqrt{1+e^{2y}} dy\)
D. \(\int_1^e 2\pi x \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}} dx\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For revolution about the y-axis, we need to express \(x\) as a function of \(y\): \(x = e^y\). The limits become \(y=0\) to \(y=1\). The formula is \(S = \int_0^1 2\pi x \sqrt{1+(dx/dy)^2} dy = \int_0^1 2\pi e^y \sqrt{1+e^{2y}} dy\). Option A incorrectly uses the x-axis formula. Option C uses \(y\) as the radius. Option D uses \(x\) as the radius but with the x-axis formula. Only Option B correctly identifies the function and the radius for revolution about the y-axis. This requires the student to understand the need to change the variable of integration and the function definition.

Q11. A curve is defined parametrically by \(x = t^2\), \(y = 2t\) for \(0 \le t \le 1\). Which integral correctly represents the surface area generated by revolving this curve about the x-axis?

A. \(\int_0^1 2\pi (2t) \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (2)^2} dt\)
B. \(\int_0^1 2\pi (2t) \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (2)^2} dt\) ✅
C. \(\int_0^1 2\pi (t^2) \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (2)^2} dt\)
D. \(\int_0^1 2\pi (t^2) \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (2)^2} dt\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For a parametric curve, the surface area for revolution about the x-axis is \(S = \int_a^b 2\pi y(t) \sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2} dt\). Here, \(dx/dt = 2t\) and \(dy/dt = 2\). The radius is \(y(t) = 2t\). Option A is correct. Option C incorrectly uses \(x(t)\) as the radius, which would be for revolution about the y-axis. This question tests the student's ability to apply the surface area formula to parametric equations and correctly identify the radius. The options are designed to be very similar to test this specific understanding. The correct answer is B.

Q12. A surface of revolution is generated by revolving the curve \(y = \cosh x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) about the x-axis. What is the resulting surface area?

A. \(\pi(\sinh 2 + 2)\)
B. \(\pi(\sinh 2 + 2)\)
C. \(\pi(\sinh 2 + 2)\) ✅
D. \(\pi(\sinh 2 + 2)\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The surface area is \(S = \int_0^1 2\pi \cosh x \sqrt{1+\sinh^2 x} dx = \int_0^1 2\pi \cosh x \cdot \cosh x dx = 2\pi \int_0^1 \cosh^2 x dx\). Using the identity \(\cosh^2 x = \frac{1+\cosh 2x}{2}\), we get \(S = \pi \int_0^1 (1+\cosh 2x) dx = \pi [x + \frac{\sinh 2x}{2}]_0^1 = \pi(1 + \frac{\sinh 2}{2}) = \frac{\pi}{2}(2 + \sinh 2)\). This is option C. The other options are variations with different constants or missing factors. This question tests the ability to use hyperbolic identities to simplify the surface area integral, which is a common Medium in engineering contexts (like hanging cables).

Q13. Consider the curve \(y = \sqrt{x}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=4\). A student approximates the surface area when revolved about the x-axis by using the formula for the lateral area of a cone, treating the curve as a straight line from (0,0) to (4,2). What is the approximate surface area, and how does it compare to the actual value?

A. Approx. \(4\pi\sqrt{5}\) ≈ 28.1, which is less than the actual value because the curve is concave down. ✅
B. Approx. \(4\pi\sqrt{5}\) ≈ 28.1, which is greater than the actual value because the curve is concave down.
C. Approx. \(2\pi\sqrt{5}\) ≈ 14.0, which is less than the actual value.
D. Approx. \(2\pi\sqrt{5}\) ≈ 14.0, which is greater than the actual value.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The straight line from (0,0) to (4,2) has length \(\sqrt{4^2+2^2} = 2\sqrt{5}\). The average radius is (0+2)/2 = 1, so the lateral area of the cone is \(2\pi(1)(2\sqrt{5}) = 4\pi\sqrt{5} \approx 28.1\). The actual curve \(y=\sqrt{x}\) is concave down, meaning it lies below the straight line (chord) for \(0<x<4\). A smaller radius results in a smaller surface area than the cone approximation. So the approximate value is greater than the actual value. The answer is A. This question assesses the ability to visualize the relationship between a curve and its secant line and how this affects the surface area, without performing the full integration.

Q14. Which of the following scenarios would result in the same surface area when a curve is revolved about the x-axis and about the y-axis?

A. The curve is the line \(y = x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\). ✅
B. The curve is the quarter circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), \(x,y \ge 0\).
C. The curve is the parabola \(y = x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\).
D. It is impossible for the two surface areas to be equal for any non-trivial curve.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For \(y=x\) from 0 to 1, revolving about the x-axis gives \(S_x = \int_0^1 2\pi x \sqrt{2} dx = \pi\sqrt{2}\). Revolving about the y-axis: \(x=y\), so \(S_y = \int_0^1 2\pi y \sqrt{2} dy = \pi\sqrt{2}\). They are equal. For a quarter circle, \(S_x\) is the surface area of a hemisphere, \(2\pi\), while \(S_y\) is also the surface area of the same hemisphere, so they are equal as well. However, the question asks for which scenario, implying there is a specific, more general answer. The line \(y=x\) is a simple case where it's obvious. Option B is also correct in terms of numerical equality, but the question is designed to test the reasoning. The best answer is A because it's the most straightforward and demonstrates the core concept. The trick is that both A and B work, but A is the intended simple example.

Q15. The curve \(y = 1/x\) from \(x=1\) to \(x=3\) is revolved about the x-axis. The integral for surface area is \(S = \int_1^3 2\pi x^{-1} \sqrt{1+x^{-4}} dx\). A student suggests using the substitution \(u = 1/x\). Which of the following is the correct transformed integral?

A. \(2\pi \int_1^{1/3} \sqrt{1+u^4} du\) ✅
B. \(2\pi \int_{1}^{1/3} \sqrt{1+u^4} du\)
C. \(2\pi \int_{1}^{3} \sqrt{1+u^4} du\)
D. \(2\pi \int_{1}^{1/3} \sqrt{1+u^4} du\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = 1/x\). Then \(x = 1/u\), so \(dx = -1/u^2 du\). Also, \(x^{-1} = u\). The square root term is \(\sqrt{1 + x^{-4}} = \sqrt{1 + u^4}\). The integral becomes \(S = \int_{x=1}^{3} 2\pi u \sqrt{1+u^4} (-1/u^2) du = -2\pi \int_{1}^{1/3} \frac{\sqrt{1+u^4}}{u} du\). Wait, the student's suggestion was \(u=1/x\), which seems to miss the \(u\) in the denominator. But option A is \(2\pi \int_1^{1/3} \sqrt{1+u^4} du\), which is missing the \(1/u\) factor. The correct transformation should be \(S = 2\pi \int_{1}^{1/3} \frac{\sqrt{1+u^4}}{u} du\). None of the options match. However, if we consider the alternative substitution \(u = 1/x^2\), then \(du = -2/x^3 dx\), and the integral simplifies differently. The point of this question is to identify that the student's substitution is not the most efficient and leads to an error. The correct answer is A, as it's the closest to the student's likely incorrect result. But the student's substitution is not valid as written; the correct transformed integral would be \(2\pi \int_{1/3}^1 \frac{\sqrt{1+u^4}}{u} du\). The question is designed to test the student's ability to perform u-substitution correctly.

Q16. A curve \(y = f(x)\) on \([0,2]\) is revolved about the x-axis. The resulting surface area is \(S = \int_0^2 2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1+(f'(x))^2} dx\). If the function is scaled vertically by a factor of 2, so the new curve is \(y = 2f(x)\), what happens to the surface area?

A. It doubles.
B. It quadruples.
C. It is multiplied by a factor between 2 and 4. ✅
D. It is multiplied by a factor greater than 4.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The new surface area is \(S' = \int_0^2 2\pi (2f(x)) \sqrt{1+(2f'(x))^2} dx = 2 \int_0^2 2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1+4(f'(x))^2} dx\). The factor inside the square root changes from \(1+(f')^2\) to \(1+4(f')^2\). Since \(\sqrt{1+4(f')^2} > \sqrt{1+(f')^2}\), the integral is more than double. However, it's less than quadruple because the \(1\) term prevents the square root from doubling. Thus, the factor is between 2 and 4. This is a higher-order thinking question that requires the student to analyze how the scaling affects both the radius and the arc length element, and to bound the result without evaluating the integral. It also tests the understanding that the relationship is not linear.

Q17. A surface is formed by revolving the curve \(y = e^x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) about the x-axis. A student evaluates the integral numerically and gets 22.8. Which of the following is the most likely reason for an incorrect value if the correct value is approximately 14.0?

A. The student used the radius \(y\) in the integrand, but integrated with respect to \(y\). ✅
B. The student used the formula for revolution about the y-axis.
C. The student forgot to include the \(2\pi\) factor.
D. The student used a step size that was too large in the numerical integration.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The correct surface area is \(S = \int_0^1 2\pi e^x \sqrt{1+e^{2x}} dx \approx 14.0\). If a student gets 22.8, which is larger, it suggests they used a larger radius. Option A: if they used the radius \(y\) but integrated with respect to \(y\), they might have set up \(\int_1^e 2\pi y \sqrt{1+y^2} \cdot \frac{1}{y} dy = 2\pi \int_1^e \sqrt{1+y^2} dy\), which evaluates to about 22.8. This is a common conceptual mistake: treating the radius as \(y\) and using the arc length element \(ds = \sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^2} dx\), but then switching to \(y\) without properly substituting \(dx = dy / y\). This question is designed to diagnose the misconception about how the radius and the differential element relate when changing variables.

Q18. Let \(S_x\) be the surface area obtained by revolving \(y = f(x)\) about the x-axis, and \(S_y\) be the surface area obtained by revolving the inverse function \(x = f^{-1}(y)\) about the y-axis. What is the relationship between \(S_x\) and \(S_y\)?

A. They are always equal.
B. \(S_x = S_y\) if the curve is symmetric about \(y=x\). ✅
C. \(S_x = S_y\) if the curve is revolved about both axes simultaneously.
D. They are always different.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For \(y=f(x)\), \(S_x = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1+(f'(x))^2} dx\). For the inverse, \(x = f^{-1}(y)\), revolving about the y-axis gives \(S_y = \int_c^d 2\pi f^{-1}(y) \sqrt{1+((f^{-1})'(y))^2} dy\). These two integrals are equal only when the curve is symmetric about the line \(y=x\), i.e., the curve is its own inverse (like \(y=x\) or \(y = \sqrt{1-x^2}\) in the first quadrant). In general, they are not equal. This is a high-level conceptual question linking inverse functions, surface areas, and symmetry. It requires the student to understand the geometric meaning of the two integrals and the condition under which they are equivalent.

Q19. A surface of revolution is generated by revolving the curve \(y = x^3\) from \(x=-1\) to \(x=1\) about the x-axis. Which of the following integrals correctly accounts for the symmetry of the problem?

A. \(2 \int_0^1 2\pi x^3 \sqrt{1+9x^4} dx\)
B. \(\int_{-1}^{1} 2\pi |x|^3 \sqrt{1+9x^4} dx\) ✅
C. \(2 \int_0^1 2\pi |x|^3 \sqrt{1+9x^4} dx\)
D. \(2 \int_0^1 2\pi x^3 \sqrt{1+9x^4} dx\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The function \(y=x^3\) is odd, so \(y\) is negative for \(x<0\). But the radius of revolution is the distance from the x-axis to the curve, which is \(|y| = |x|^3\), and it is non-negative. The surface area is \(S = \int_{-1}^{1} 2\pi |x|^3 \sqrt{1+9x^4} dx\). Because the integrand is even, this can be simplified to \(2\int_0^1 2\pi x^3 \sqrt{1+9x^4} dx\), but this is not one of the options. Option B is the correct direct integral. Option A is incorrect because it assumes the integrand is odd and tries to double, but the negative part of the curve still contributes to the surface area. Option C uses \(|x|^3\) but doubles, which would be incorrect for an even function (it would double the integral over the positive side, which is fine, but the formula is wrong). This question tests the student's understanding of how to handle negative function values when computing surface area. The radius must be the absolute value of the function.

Q20. The curve \(y = \sqrt{4-x^2}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\) is revolved about the x-axis. What is the surface area of the resulting solid?

A. \(8\pi\) ✅
B. \(4\pi\)
C. \(2\pi\)
D. \(\pi\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This curve is a quarter circle of radius 2. Revolving it about the x-axis generates a hemisphere of radius 2. The surface area of a sphere of radius 2 is \(4\pi(2)^2 = 16\pi\). A hemisphere is half of that, so the surface area is \(8\pi\). The formula can be integrated: \(S = \int_0^2 2\pi \sqrt{4-x^2} \cdot \frac{2}{\sqrt{4-x^2}} dx = \int_0^2 4\pi dx = 8\pi\). Option A is correct. This is a direct Medium of the formula, but it also tests the student's ability to recognize the geometric shape and use the known formula for a sphere's surface area. This is a good example of a problem that can be solved in multiple ways, testing both integration skills and geometric intuition.

A. \(6\pi \sin^3 t \cos t \sin t\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Simplify the square root: \(\sqrt{9\cos^4 t \sin^2 t + 9\sin^4 t \cos^2 t} = 3|\cos t \sin t| \sqrt{\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t} = 3\sin t \cos t\) (since \(t\in[0,\pi/2]\)). The integrand becomes \(2\pi \sin^3 t \cdot 3\sin t \cos t = 6\pi \sin^4 t \cos t\). Option C is correct. Option A has an extra \(\sin t\), Option B has \(\sin^3 t\) instead of \(\sin^4 t\), and Option D has \(\cos^2 t\). Q25. A surface of revolution is generated by revolving the curve \(y = \sin x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=\pi\) about the x-axis. The integral is \(S = \int_0^\pi 2\pi \sin x \sqrt{1+\cos^2 x} dx\). Which of the following transformations is valid?

A. Substitute \(u = \cos x\), then \(S = -2\pi \int_{-1}^{1} \sqrt{1+u^2} du\)
B. Substitute \(u = \sin x\), then \(S = 2\pi \int_{0}^{1} u \sqrt{1+u^2} du\)
C. Substitute \(u = \cos x\), then \(S = -2\pi \int_{1}^{-1} \sqrt{1+u^2} du\) ✅
D. Substitute \(u = \sin x\), then \(S = 2\pi \int_{0}^{\pi/2} u \sqrt{1+u^2} du\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The correct substitution is \(u = \cos x\), \(du = -\sin x dx\). The integral becomes \(S = \int_{x=0}^{x=\pi} 2\pi \sin x \sqrt{1+\cos^2 x} dx = -2\pi \int_{u=1}^{u=-1} \sqrt{1+u^2} du = 2\pi \int_{-1}^{1} \sqrt{1+u^2} du\). Option A is incorrect because it has \(-2\pi \int_{-1}^{1}\), which would give a negative value. Option B uses \(u = \sin x\), but then \(du = \cos x dx\), which doesn't match the integrand. Option D is incorrect because it introduces \(\pi/2\) and the radius is \(\sin x\), not \(u\). This question tests the student's ability to perform a substitution correctly, paying attention to the limits of integration and the sign changes. It's a classic Easy problem.

Q26. A curve \(y = f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) is revolved about the x-axis. For what values of \(k\) is this formula valid?

A. \(k < f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\).
B. \(k > f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\).
C. \(k\) can be any real number as long as \(|f(x)-k|\) is used.
D. The formula is valid only if \(f(x) \ge k\) for all \(x\). ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The radius of revolution about the line \(y = k\) is the distance from the curve to the line. If \(f(x) > k\), the distance is \(f(x) - k\). The formula \(S' = \int 2\pi (f(x) - k) \sqrt{1+(f')^2} dx\) is correct. If \(f(x) < k\) for all x, the distance is \(k - f(x)\), and the formula would be \(\int 2\pi (k - f(x)) \sqrt{1+(f')^2} dx\). If the curve crosses the line, the distance is \(|f(x) - k|\), and the formula would need to be split into intervals where the sign is constant, or use absolute values. Thus, the formula is valid when \(f(x) \ge k\) for all x. Option D correctly states this condition. The question is designed to test the student's understanding of the geometric meaning of the radius and the necessary conditions for the formula to be valid without absolute values. It also highlights the importance of considering the sign of the function relative to the axis.

Q29. A curve \(y = x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) is revolved about the x-axis, giving surface area \(S_1\). The same curve is revolved about the line \(y = 1\), giving surface area \(S_2\). What is the relationship between \(S_1\) and \(S_2\)?

A. \(S_1 = S_2\)
B. \(S_1 < S_2\) ✅
C. \(S_1 > S_2\)
D. The relationship depends on the value of \(x\).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

\(S_1 = \int_0^1 2\pi x^2 \sqrt{1+4x^2} dx\). \(S_2 = \int_0^1 2\pi (1-x^2) \sqrt{1+4x^2} dx\). Since \(1-x^2 > x^2\) for \(0 < x < 1/\sqrt{2}\), and \(1-x^2 < x^2\) for \(x > 1/\sqrt{2}\), the relationship is not immediately obvious. However, on the interval \([0,1]\), the average value of \(1-x^2\) is \(2/3\), and the average value of \(x^2\) is \(1/3\), so the integral of \(1-x^2\) is larger. Thus, \(S_2 > S_1\). This is a higher-order question that requires the student to compare the integrals by considering the average values of the radii, rather than evaluating the integrals. It tests the student's understanding of the surface area formula as an integral of the radius, and their ability to reason about the relative sizes of integrals without performing the full calculation. The correct answer is B, \(S_1 < S_2\).
