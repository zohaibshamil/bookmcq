What is Arc length of a curve calculus?

The arc length of a smooth curve \( y=f(x) \) from \( x=a \) to \( x=b \) is determined by integrating the square root of one plus the squared derivative, given by \( L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx \), which sums infinitesimal linear distances along the curved path.

Q1. A student states that the arc length of \(y=x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) can be found by evaluating \(\int_0^1 (1+2x)\,dx\). Is this correct?

A. Yes, because the integrand should be \(1+f'(x)\).
B. Yes, because the arc length formula is \(\int (1+f'(x))dx\).
C. No, because the formula requires \(1+[f'(x)]^2\) inside the square root. ✅
D. No, because the derivative of \(x^2\) is \(2x\), not \(2\).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The student mistakenly simplified \(\sqrt{1+(2x)^2}\) as \(1+2x\). The correct integrand is \(\sqrt{1+4x^2}\). Squaring a sum is not the same as the sum of squares. This is a common algebraic error when applying the arc length formula.

Q2. Which of the following is the correct integral for the arc length of \(y=\sin x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=\pi\)?

A. \(\int_0^\pi \sqrt{1+\cos^2 x}\,dx\) ✅
B. \(\int_0^\pi \sqrt{1+\sin^2 x}\,dx\)
C. \(\int_0^\pi \sqrt{1-\cos^2 x}\,dx\)
D. \(\int_0^\pi \sqrt{1+\cos x}\,dx\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The arc length formula for \(y=f(x)\) is \(\int_a^b \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx\). For \(y=\sin x\), \(f'(x)=\cos x\). Squaring it gives \(\cos^2 x\), so the integrand is \(\sqrt{1+\cos^2 x}\). Easy of the standard formula is required.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

When a curve is expressed as \(x=g(y)\), the formula for arc length from \(y=c\) to \(y=d\) is \(\int_c^d \sqrt{1+[g'(y)]^2}\,dy\). The derivative must be with respect to \(y\), and the limits must be in terms of \(y\). Easy is needed here.

Q4. Why is the Mean Value Theorem used in the derivation of the arc length formula?

D. To find the exact value of the arc length integral.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The derivation of the arc length formula uses the Mean Value Theorem to express the change in \(y\) over a subinterval as \(f'(x_k^*)\Delta x_k\). This is crucial for transforming the sum of segment lengths into a Riemann sum that can be evaluated as an integral. It's not about continuity or existence of the limit in a general sense.

The arc length formula is derived assuming \(y=f(x)\) is a smooth curve, which requires \(f'\) to be continuous on \([a,b]\). This ensures the Mean Value Theorem can be applied and the resulting integral exists. Continuity of \(f\) alone is not sufficient for the derivation to work.

Q6. For the curve \(y=x^{3/2}\) from \((1,1)\) to \((2,2\sqrt{2})\), which setup for the arc length is correct when integrating with respect to \(x\)?

D. \(\int_1^2 \sqrt{1+\frac{9}{4}x^{1/2}}\,dx\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The derivative of \(y=x^{3/2}\) is \(\frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}\). Squaring this gives \(\frac{9}{4}x\). The correct integrand is \(\sqrt{1+\frac{9}{4}x}\). Options B, C, and D have errors in the exponent or the derivative. Medium of the formula requires careful differentiation and algebra.

Q7. A cable hangs in the shape of a catenary \(y=\cosh x\). To find its length from \(x=0\) to \(x=\ln 2\), you set up the integral. What is a key property of \(\cosh x\) that simplifies this integral?

B. \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1\) ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For \(y=\cosh x\), \(y'=\sinh x\), so the arc length integrand is \(\sqrt{1+\sinh^2 x}\). Using the identity \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x=1\), this simplifies to \(\sqrt{\cosh^2 x}=\cosh x\). This is the core conceptual step that makes the integral easy to evaluate. The derivative property is part of it, but the identity is key to simplification.

Q8. A student computes the arc length of \(y=x^2\) from \(x=1\) to \(x=2\) and gets \(\int_1^2 \sqrt{1+4x^2}\,dx\). Another student computes the arc length of the inverse curve \(x=\sqrt{y}\) from \(y=1\) to \(y=4\) and gets \(\int_1^4 \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4y}}\,dy\). Are these two integrals equal?

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The curve \(y=x^2\) from \(x=1\) to \(2\) is the same as the curve \(x=\sqrt{y}\) from \(y=1\) to \(4\). The arc length is a geometric property of the curve and is independent of the parameterization. Both integrals, after appropriate substitution (e.g., \(u=x^2\)), should evaluate to the same numerical value, demonstrating the equivalence of the two formulations.

Q10. When approximating arc length with line segments, why is the length of the \(k\)-th segment \( \sqrt{(\Delta x_k)^2 + (\Delta y_k)^2} \) and not just \(|\Delta y_k|\)?

A. Because the curve must be vertical.
B. Because the curve is not a straight line.
C. Because the curve has both horizontal and vertical components. ✅
D. Because \(\Delta x_k\) is negligible.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Arc length considers the total distance traveled along the curve. The line segment connecting two points has both a horizontal change (\(\Delta x_k\)) and a vertical change (\(\Delta y_k\)). Ignoring \(\Delta x_k\) would only measure the vertical component, which would be incorrect unless the curve is vertical. The formula uses the Pythagorean theorem to find the true length of the segment.

A. \(2L\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For \(y=2f(x)\), the derivative is \(2f'(x)\). The arc length is \(\int_a^b \sqrt{1+[2f'(x)]^2}\,dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+4[f'(x)]^2}\,dx\). It is not simply \(2L\) because the vertical scaling also affects the horizontal component of the curve's slope, changing the length in a non-linear way.

This problem requires multiple conceptual steps. First, find \(y'=\tan x\). Then, the integrand becomes \(\sqrt{1+\tan^2 x}\). Since \(\sec x\) is positive on the interval, \(\sqrt{\sec^2 x}=\sec x\). Each step (differentiation, identity, and considering the sign) is crucial and interdependent, making this a Hard problem that tests comprehensive understanding.

Q13. If the arc length of \(y=f(x)\) from \(a\) to \(b\) is \(L\), what is the arc length of the curve \(y=f(x)+C\) from \(a\) to \(b\), where \(C\) is a constant?

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Adding a constant to a function translates it vertically. The derivative, \(f'(x)\), remains unchanged. Since the arc length formula depends only on the derivative, the arc length remains the same. A vertical shift does not change the shape or length of the curve. This tests a deep understanding of the formula's dependence on the derivative.

Q14. Why is the formula \(\int_a^b \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx\) not directly applicable to find the arc length of \(y=x^{2/3}\) from \(x=-1\) to \(x=8\)?

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For \(y=x^{2/3}\), the derivative is \(y'=\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}\), which is undefined at \(x=0\). The arc length formula requires \(f'\) to be continuous. The cusp at \(x=0\) makes the curve not smooth, and the integral would be improper. A common error is to apply the formula without checking the smoothness condition.

Q15. A curve is defined parametrically by \(x(t)=t^2\), \(y(t)=t^3\) for \(0\le t\le 1\). What is the correct integral for its arc length?

A. \(\int_0^1 \sqrt{4t^2+9t^4}\,dt\) ✅
B. \(\int_0^1 \sqrt{2t+3t^2}\,dt\)
C. \(\int_0^1 \sqrt{4t^2+9t^4}\,dt\)
D. \(\int_0^1 (2t+3t^2)\,dt\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For parametric curves, the arc length formula is \(\int_a^b \sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^2+(\frac{dy}{dt})^2}\,dt\). Here, \(\frac{dx}{dt}=2t\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}=3t^2\). Squaring and adding gives \(4t^2+9t^4\). This is a direct Medium of the parametric arc length formula, requiring careful differentiation and algebra.

This is a misconception about the effect of translations on the curve's length, which only changes if the shift is in the direction of the independent variable.

This is not simply a factor of \(L\), as the scaling changes the slope and the horizontal component of length. This requires a deep understanding of the arc length formula and transformations.

Q23. To find the arc length of \(y=\sqrt{9-x^2}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=3\), which method is most efficient?

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

\(y=\sqrt{9-x^2}\) is the upper half of a circle of radius 3 centered at the origin. The arc from \(x=0\) to \(x=3\) is a quarter of the circle. Its length is \(\frac{1}{4}(2\pi(3)) = \frac{3\pi}{2}\). This is much more efficient than setting up and evaluating the integral. This tests the ability to recognize geometric shapes from their equations.

Q24. A curve is represented by the parametric equations \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\) for \(0\le t \le \pi/2\). The arc length is \(\pi/2\). What is the arc length of the curve \(x=\cos(2t)\), \(y=\sin(2t)\) for \(0\le t \le \pi/4\)?

The second curve is also a quarter circle, but the parameter \(t\) runs from 0 to \(\pi/4\), while the angle \(\theta=2t\) runs from 0 to \(\pi/2\). The arc length of a quarter circle of radius 1 is always \(\pi/2\), regardless of the parametrization's speed, as long as the trace is exactly the quarter circle once. This tests the understanding that arc length is a geometric property independent of parametrization.

Q25. An integral for the arc length of \(y=e^x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) is \(\int_0^1 \sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\,dx\). A student suggests using the substitution \(u=e^x\). After substitution, what is the correct new integral?

A. \(\int_1^e \frac{\sqrt{1+u^2}}{u}\,du\) ✅
B. \(\int_0^1 \sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

With \(u=e^x\), \(du=e^x dx \implies dx = du/u\). The limits become \(u=1\) to \(u=e\). The integrand becomes \(\sqrt{1+u^2}\cdot \frac{du}{u}\). This is a correct Medium of substitution, requiring careful handling of the \(dx\) term and the limits. It tests a multi-step Medium of calculus techniques.

Q27. Which of the following is the best reason for using numerical methods to approximate arc length integrals?

This is a high-level conceptual question linking arc length to the geometric idea of a curve, independent of its parameterization.

Q29. The arc length of a curve \(y=f(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) is given by \(L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx\). If the curve is instead parameterized by its arc length \(s\), what is \(\frac{dy}{ds}\)?

A. \(\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\)
B. \(\frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}}\) ✅
C. \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}}\)
D. \(f'(x)\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \(ds = \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2} dx\), we have \(\frac{dx}{ds} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}}\). Then \(\frac{dy}{ds} = \frac{dy}{dx}\frac{dx}{ds} = \frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}}\). This is a sophisticated problem that requires understanding the relationship between the differential of arc length and the standard derivative, going beyond basic formula Medium.

Q30. A curve has a vertical tangent at a point. What issue does this cause for the arc length formula \(L=\int_a^b \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx\)?

📖 Explanation:

A vertical tangent means the slope \(dy/dx\) is infinite. The formula \(y=f(x)\) requires a finite derivative. The curve can often be re-parameterized, for example, as \(x=g(y)\), to handle this. A student might incorrectly assume the length is infinite or that the function is discontinuous. The problem is with the representation, not the curve itself.
