📝 Polar area symmetry

📖 From Calculus • 11. Parametric and Polar curves: Conic Sections • 24 questions available

What is Polar area symmetry?

Definition: If a polar curve is symmetric about the polar axis (x-axis), then \( r(-\theta)=r(\theta) \) or \( r(\theta)=r(-\theta) \). About the line \( \theta=\pi/2 \) (y-axis): \( r(\pi-\theta)=r(\theta) \). About the origin: \( r(\theta+\pi)=r(\theta) \). Use symmetry to halve integration limits and multiply area. Example: For \( r=\cos\theta \) (circle), symmetric about polar axis. Area \( = 2 \cdot \frac12 \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta \, d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta \, d\theta = \pi/4 \), but full circle area \( \pi/4 \)? Actually circle radius 0.5 area π/4, correct. Reason: Symmetry reduces computation time and avoids missing regions when finding total area or intersections.

Q1. A polar curve is defined by \( r = 2 + \cos(3\theta) \). A student calculates the total enclosed area by integrating \( \frac{1}{2}r^2 \) from \( 0 \) to \( 2\pi \). Another student argues that due to symmetry, integrating from \( 0 \) to \( \pi/3 \) and multiplying by 6 yields the same result more efficiently. Which statement best evaluates these approaches?

A. Both methods are valid, but the second exploits rotational symmetry of order 6 correctly. ✅
B. The first method is invalid because the curve retraces itself; only the second works.
C. Both are valid, but the second is incorrect because the period of \( \cos(3\theta) \) requires multiplication by 3, not 6.
D. The first method double-counts area; the second undercounts because symmetry axes are misidentified.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The curve \( r = 2 + \cos(3\theta) \) has three-fold rotational symmetry since \( \cos(3\theta) \) repeats every \( 2\pi/3 \), but because \( r > 0 \) always, each petal-like lobe is traced once per \( 2\pi/3 \), yielding three identical regions. However, within each \( 2\pi/3 \) sector, there is reflection symmetry about \( \theta = \pi/3 \), allowing integration over \( [0, \pi/3] \) and multiplying by 6. The full \( 0 \) to \( 2\pi \) integral is also correct but less efficient. Option C misidentifies the symmetry order, while D incorrectly claims double-counting.

Q2. Consider the parametric curve \( x(t) = t^3 - 3t \), \( y(t) = t^2 - 1 \) for \( t \in [-2, 2] \). To find the area enclosed by the loop, which symmetry property should be leveraged, and what is the correct integral setup?

A. Symmetry about the x-axis; integrate \( y(t)x'(t) \) from \( -1 \) to \( 1 \) and multiply by 2. ✅
B. Symmetry about the y-axis; integrate \( x(t)y'(t) \) from \( 0 \) to \( \sqrt{3} \) and multiply by 2.
C. No symmetry exists; must integrate \( y(t)x'(t) \) from \( -\sqrt{3} \) to \( \sqrt{3} \).
D. Symmetry about the origin; integrate \( \frac{1}{2}(x y' - y x') dt \) from \( 0 \) to \( \sqrt{3} \) and multiply by 4.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The parametric equations satisfy \( x(-t) = -x(t) \) and \( y(-t) = y(t) \), indicating symmetry about the x-axis. The loop occurs between the two t-values where \( x(t) = 0 \) and \( y(t) \geq 0 \), specifically \( t = \pm1 \). Because y is even and x’ is odd, \( y(t)x'(t) \) is odd, but area uses absolute orientation; leveraging x-axis symmetry allows computing upper half (\( t \in [-1,1] \)) and doubling. Option B confuses axis symmetry, C ignores existing symmetry, and D misapplies origin symmetry which doesn’t hold for area via this formula.

Q3. A student computes the area inside \( r = \sin(2\theta) \) by integrating \( \frac{1}{2}\sin^2(2\theta) \) from \( 0 \) to \( \pi/2 \) and multiplying by 4. Their answer matches the known result. However, when applying the same logic to \( r = \sin(3\theta) \), they integrate from \( 0 \) to \( \pi/3 \) and multiply by 6, obtaining an incorrect area. What is the fundamental error in their reasoning?

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

For \( r = \sin(n\theta) \), if n is odd, the curve has n petals traced exactly once over \( [0, \pi] \); each petal spans \( \pi/n \), so integrating over \( [0, \pi/3] \) and multiplying by 3 (not 6) gives total area. For even n like 2, there are 2n petals over \( [0, 2\pi] \), each spanning \( \pi/n \), justifying multiplication by 4 for \( n=2 \). The student erroneously applied the even-n rule to an odd-n case. Negative r-values don’t cancel area in polar integration since \( r^2 \) is always positive, refuting option B.

Q4. Given the conic section in polar form \( r = \frac{4}{1 + 0.5\cos\theta} \), which represents an ellipse with focus at the pole, how can symmetry reduce computational effort when finding the total area?

A. Integrate from \( 0 \) to \( \pi \) and double, exploiting symmetry about the polar axis. ✅
B. Integrate from \( 0 \) to \( \pi/2 \) and quadruple, using both axial symmetries.

D. Use Cartesian conversion and exploit y-axis symmetry of the resulting ellipse equation.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This ellipse is symmetric about the polar axis (x-axis) because replacing \( \theta \) with \( -\theta \) leaves r unchanged. It is not symmetric about \( \theta = \pi/2 \) since \( \cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta \neq \cos\theta \). Thus, only reflection across the polar axis holds, allowing integration over \( [0, \pi] \) and doubling. Option B assumes bilateral symmetry which doesn’t exist here. Option C is false—conics with focus at pole retain polar-axis symmetry. Option D adds unnecessary complexity when polar symmetry suffices.

Q5. A graph shows a closed parametric curve symmetric about both coordinate axes. A student sets up the area integral as \( 4\int_{0}^{a} y(x)\,dx \). Under what condition would this approach fail despite apparent symmetry?

A. When the curve is not a function of x in the first quadrant, requiring parametric or polar formulation.
B. When the curve crosses itself in the first quadrant, violating simple region assumptions.
C. When the parameterization traverses the first quadrant non-monotonically in x.
D. All of the above could invalidate the Cartesian symmetry approach. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Even with geometric symmetry, using \( \int y\,dx \) assumes y is a single-valued function of x and the boundary is traversed monotonically. If the curve loops or backtracks in the first quadrant (common in parametric curves), \( y(x) \) may be multi-valued or dx may change sign, making the Cartesian integral invalid. Parametric area formula \( \int y x'\,dt \) handles such cases correctly. Thus, all listed issues represent realistic pitfalls where naive symmetry-based Cartesian integration fails, necessitating careful analysis of parameterization behavior.

The cardioid \( r = 1 + \cos\theta \) satisfies \( r(\theta) = r(-\theta) \), so it's symmetric about the polar axis, and the full curve is traced over \( [0, 2\pi] \), but the lower half mirrors the upper, allowing \( 2\int_0^\pi \). The rose \( r = \cos(2\theta) \) also has polar-axis symmetry, but over \( [0, \pi] \) it traces all four petals (since period is \( \pi \)), so integrating \( 0 \) to \( \pi \) already gives total area—doubling would double-count. Thus, the key difference lies in how many times the curve is traced over symmetric intervals, not negativity of r (since \( r^2 \) eliminates sign).

Q9. An incorrect solution claims the area inside \( r = \sin\theta + \cos\theta \) is \( \pi/2 \) by asserting symmetry about \( \theta = \pi/4 \) and integrating from \( 0 \) to \( \pi/4 \) then multiplying by 4. What is the flaw in this symmetry argument?

But among options, \( r = \sin(2\theta) \) actually fails the symmetry condition, making it the correct choice for “does NOT guarantee” because the premise isn’t met. B. The student forgot that sinθ is negative in [π, 2π], but r² makes it positive, so no issue.
C. The curve is only defined for θ in [0, π]; beyond that, r < 0 is invalid.
D. Symmetry about θ = π/2 was ignored, leading to incorrect bounds.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The polar curve \( r = 2\sin\theta \) represents a circle of radius 1 centered at (0,1). As θ ranges from 0 to π, r ≥ 0 and the circle is traced exactly once. From π to 2π, sinθ < 0, so r < 0, which in polar coordinates plots the same points as positive r in opposite direction, retracing the same circle. Thus, integrating over [0, 2π] counts the area twice. Leveraging symmetry, one should integrate only over [0, π] (or [0, π/2] and double) to get correct area π. Option B misunderstands that r² positivity doesn’t prevent double-tracing. Option C is false—negative r is allowed but causes retracing. Q24. A student claims that for any polar curve with \( r(\theta) = r(\theta + \pi) \), the area from 0 to π equals half the total area. For which curve is this claim FALSE?
