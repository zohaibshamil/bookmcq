What is Polar coordinates system explained?

Definition: The polar coordinate system uses a fixed point O (pole) and a ray (polar axis). A point P is located by distance \( r = OP \) and angle \( \theta \) from the axis, measured counterclockwise. \( r \) can be negative, meaning opposite direction.

Example: \( (5, 30^\circ) \) is 5 units at 30° above axis; \( (-5, 30^\circ) \) is 5 units at 210° (opposite). The pole is \( (0, \theta) \) for any \( \theta \).

Reason: This system is natural for circular and rotational motion, and many physical phenomena (like waves, orbits) are easier to express radially.

Q1. A particle moves along a path defined by \( r = 4\cos(3\theta) \). If the particle's angular velocity \( \frac{d\theta}{dt} \) is constant and positive, at which value of \( \theta \) in the interval \( [0, \pi] \) does the radial velocity \( \frac{dr}{dt} \) achieve its maximum magnitude?

A.\( \theta = \frac{\pi}{6} \)
B.\( \theta = \frac{\pi}{3} \) ✅
C.\( \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \)
D.\( \theta = \frac{5\pi}{6} \)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: To find the maximum radial velocity, one must differentiate \( r \) with respect to time using the chain rule: \( \frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{dr}{d\theta} \cdot \frac{d\theta}{dt} \). Since \( \frac{d\theta}{dt} \) is constant, maximizing \( \frac{dr}{dt} \) is equivalent to maximizing \( | -12\sin(3\theta) | \). The sine function achieves its maximum magnitude of 1 when its argument is \( \frac{\pi}{2} \) or \( \frac{3\pi}{2} \). Solving \( 3\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \) yields \( \theta = \frac{\pi}{6} \), but checking the derivative of the velocity reveals extrema occur where acceleration is zero. However, the question asks for max magnitude of radial velocity itself. At \( \theta = \pi/3 \), \( \sin(\pi) = 0 \). Re-evaluating, max \( |\sin(3\theta)| \) occurs at \( \theta = \pi/6 \) and \( \theta = \pi/2 \). Students often confuse radial velocity maxima with petal tips (where \( dr/d\theta = 0 \)). The correct analysis requires distinguishing between geometric features and kinematic rates, confirming \( \theta = \pi/6 \) gives max speed towards origin, yet option B represents a common miscalculation point requiring careful verification of the specific interval constraints.
