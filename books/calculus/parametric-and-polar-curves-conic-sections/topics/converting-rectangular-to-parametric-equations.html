What is Converting rectangular to parametric equations?

Definition: To convert a rectangular equation \( y = f(x) \) or \( F(x,y)=0 \) to parametric form, choose a parameter (often \( t \)) and set \( x = t \) (or \( x = g(t) \)), then solve for \( y \) in terms of \( t \).

Example: For \( y = x^2 \), let \( x = t \), then \( y = t^2 \), so parametric equations are \( x=t, y=t^2 \). For ellipse \( x^2/4 + y^2/9 = 1 \), use \( x=2\cos t, y=3\sin t \).

Reason: Conversion gives flexibility for graphing, calculus, and modeling motion, especially when the rectangular form is difficult to integrate or differentiate.

Q1. A particle moves along the curve \( y = x^3 - 3x \). If the horizontal velocity is given by \( \frac{dx}{dt} = t^2 + 1 \), which parametric representation correctly models the vertical velocity \( \frac{dy}{dt} \) as a function of \( t \), assuming \( x(0) = 0 \)?

A. \( \frac{dy}{dt} = (t^2+1)(3(t^2+1)^2 - 3) \)
B. \( \frac{dy}{dt} = 3x^2 - 3 \)
C. \( \frac{dy}{dt} = (3x^2 - 3)(t^2 + 1) \) where \( x = \int_0^t (u^2+1)\,du \) ✅
D. \( \frac{dy}{dt} = 3(t^2+1)^3 - 3(t^2+1) \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This question requires multi-step reasoning combining calculus and parametric definitions. Students must recognize that \( \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx} \cdot \frac{dx}{dt} \) via the chain rule. Since \( \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 - 3 \) depends on \( x \), and \( x \) itself is a function of \( t \) defined by integration of \( \frac{dx}{dt} \), one cannot substitute \( t \) directly into \( x \) without first expressing \( x(t) \). Option A incorrectly treats \( x \) as \( t^2+1 \). Option B omits the chain rule factor. Option D substitutes \( t^2+1 \) for \( x \) erroneously. Only C correctly maintains functional dependence through integration, testing deep conceptual understanding of parametric derivatives.

The student’s claim arises from mistakenly taking \( \sqrt{t^4} = t^2 \) without considering absolute value or misapplying root rules. Option A reinforces the misconception. Option C discusses injectivity but doesn’t address the sign error. Option D contradicts the definition of principal square root. Only B correctly identifies the confusion between parameter behavior and function range, emphasizing that parametrizations must respect original constraints.

Q4. A model for population growth follows \( P = k e^{rt} \). To analyze sensitivity to time scaling, researchers reparametrize using \( \tau = t^3 \). How does \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} \) compare to \( \frac{dP}{dt} \) at \( t = 2 \)?

A. \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} = \frac{1}{12} \frac{dP}{dt} \) ✅
B. \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} = 12 \frac{dP}{dt} \)
C. \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} = \frac{1}{8} \frac{dP}{dt} \)
D. \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} = \frac{dP}{dt} \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This application question integrates parametric reparameterization with real-world modeling. Given \( \tau = t^3 \), then \( \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{1}{3t^2} \). By chain rule, \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} = \frac{dP}{dt} \cdot \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{dP}{dt} \cdot \frac{1}{3t^2} \). At \( t = 2 \), \( 3t^2 = 12 \), so \( \frac{dP}{d\tau} = \frac{1}{12} \frac{dP}{dt} \). Distractors arise from forgetting the derivative of inverse function (choosing B), miscalculating \( t^3 \) derivative as \( 3t \) (C), or ignoring chain rule (D). This emphasizes that rate interpretation changes under nonlinear time rescaling, crucial in dynamical systems analysis.
