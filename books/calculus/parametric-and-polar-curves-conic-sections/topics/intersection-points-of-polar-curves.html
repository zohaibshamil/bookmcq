What is Intersection points of polar curves?

Definition: To find intersection points of \( r=f(\theta) \) and \( r=g(\theta) \), solve \( f(\theta)=g(\theta) \) for \( \theta \). Also check pole (r=0) separately because it may occur at different angles. Convert to rectangular to verify if needed. Example: Intersect \( r=1 \) (circle) and \( r=2\cos\theta \) (circle). Solve \( 1=2\cos\theta \) → \( \cos\theta=1/2 \) → \( \theta=\pm\pi/3 \). Points: \( (1, \pm\pi/3) \). Pole: \( r=0 \) not on \( r=1 \), so only these. Reason: Intersections are important for area between curves and for understanding curve relationships; polar solving is often simpler than rectangular.

Q1. A student solves \( r = 2\cos\theta \) and \( r = 1 \) algebraically by setting \( 2\cos\theta = 1 \), finding \( \theta = \pm\pi/3 \). They conclude there are exactly two intersection points. Which critical error analysis best describes the flaw in this reasoning?

A. The student failed to account for negative \( r \) values producing geometrically identical points at different angles.
B. The student ignored that the pole may be an intersection point even if not satisfied simultaneously by both equations. ✅
C. The student should have converted to Cartesian coordinates first to avoid polar ambiguity entirely.
D. The cosine function has four solutions in \( [0, 2\pi) \), so the student missed two valid intersections.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When finding intersections of polar curves, solving \( f(\theta) = g(\theta) \) only finds points where both curves pass through the same location at the same angle. However, curves can intersect at the pole independently: one curve may pass through the origin at \( \theta = \alpha \) while the other passes through at \( \theta = \beta \neq \alpha \). Since the pole has no unique angular coordinate, it must always be checked separately by testing whether each equation has any solution with \( r = 0 \). This is a common oversight in purely algebraic approaches.

B. 5
C. 6 ✅
D. 7

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Setting \( 3\sin(2\theta) = 3\sin\theta \) gives \( \sin\theta(2\cos\theta - 1) = 0 \), yielding \( \theta = 0, \pi, \pi/3, 5\pi/3 \). The solutions \( \theta = 0 \) and \( \theta = \pi \) both correspond to the pole. Additionally, we must check if negative \( r \) values create coincident points: for instance, \( r = -a \) at angle \( \theta \) equals \( r = a \) at \( \theta + \pi \). Careful enumeration shows six distinct geometric points including the pole counted once, demonstrating that algebraic solutions alone undercount due to polar representation non-uniqueness.

Q11. Conceptual understanding: Why can two polar curves intersect at a point where \( f(\theta_1) = g(\theta_2) \) with \( \theta_1 \neq \theta_2 \), yet this intersection never appears in the solution set of \( f(\theta) = g(\theta) \)?

A. Because polar functions are not injective; multiple parameter values map to the same geometric point.
B. Because the equality \( f(\theta) = g(\theta) \) enforces identical radial distance and angle simultaneously. ✅
C. Because such intersections only occur at the pole, which is excluded from standard domains.
D. Because trigonometric identities fail when angles differ by multiples of \( \pi \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The equation \( f(\theta) = g(\theta) \) seeks parameter values where both the radial coordinate and angular coordinate match exactly. Geometric intersection, however, only requires the Cartesian coordinates to coincide, which can happen when \( (f(\theta_1), \theta_1) \) and \( (g(\theta_2), \theta_2) \) represent the same point via polar non-uniqueness (e.g., \( r_1 = -r_2 \) and \( \theta_1 = \theta_2 + \pi \)). Thus, \( f(\theta) = g(\theta) \) is sufficient but not necessary for geometric intersection. Understanding this distinction is central to mastering polar intersections and explains why supplementary checks are mandatory.

Q22. Direct recall: Which of the following is NOT a valid reason to convert polar intersection problems to Cartesian coordinates?

The advantage of Cartesian is avoiding polar representation ambiguities (options A, C, D are valid benefits), but pole checking remains necessary.
