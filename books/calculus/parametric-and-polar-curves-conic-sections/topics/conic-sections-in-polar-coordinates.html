What is Conic sections in polar coordinates?

Definition: In polar coordinates, all conics with a focus at the origin are expressed as \( r = \frac{ep}{1 \pm e\cos\theta} \) or with sine, where p is the distance from focus to directrix. e=1 parabola, e<1 ellipse, e>1 hyperbola.
Example: \( r = \frac{6}{2 - 3\cos\theta} \) → divide by 2: \( r = \frac{3}{1 - 1.5\cos\theta} \) → e=1.5>1 hyperbola. \( r = \frac{2}{1+\cos\theta} \) → e=1 parabola.
Reason: This unified polar representation is standard in celestial mechanics and orbital dynamics.

Q1. A satellite's orbit is modeled by \( r = \frac{5000}{1 + 0.8\cos\theta} \). If mission control mistakenly uses \( e = 0.6 \) for trajectory correction burns, what is the primary physical consequence at apoapsis?

A.The satellite will achieve a higher altitude than intended due to underestimated eccentricity.
B.The satellite will fail to reach apoapsis because the calculated velocity is too high for the actual orbital energy. ✅
C.The burn will occur at the wrong true anomaly, causing an inclination change rather than an apsidal shift.
D.The orbit will become hyperbolic because reducing eccentricity in the model increases the semi-latus rectum disproportionately.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: This scenario requires applying the relationship between eccentricity and orbital energy. The denominator term \( 1+e\cos\theta \) dictates the shape. Using \( e=0.6 \) instead of \( 0.8 \) implies a more circular orbit with different specific mechanical energy. At apoapsis, where \( \theta=\pi \), the actual radius depends on the true \( e \). A lower assumed \( e \) leads to calculating a required delta-v that is insufficient to maintain the actual elliptical path, resulting in the spacecraft falling short of the intended apoapsis distance due to conservation of angular momentum and energy mismatches.

Q2. Consider the polar equation \( r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\sin\theta} \). A student claims that as \( e \to 1^- \), the conic approaches a parabola opening downward. Which statement best critiques this reasoning regarding the directrix location?

A.The claim is correct; the limit naturally yields a parabola with the directrix below the pole.
B.The claim is flawed because \( d \) must simultaneously scale with \( (1-e) \) to prevent the vertex from receding to infinity. ✅
C.The claim is incorrect because \( \sin\theta \) shifts the axis of symmetry to the vertical, making it open upward regardless of \( e \).
D.The claim is valid only if the numerator is changed to \( ed(1+e) \) to preserve the focal parameter.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Students often treat \( e \) and \( d \) as independent constants when taking limits. However, for a conic to approach a finite parabola as \( e \to 1 \), the semi-latus rectum \( \ell = ed \) must remain constant or \( d \) must adjust relative to the focus-directrix distance. If \( d \) is fixed while \( e \to 1 \), the geometric definition breaks down or the curve degenerates/escapes to infinity. True parabolic limits require careful scaling of parameters to maintain a well-defined vertex position and focal length, highlighting the interdependence of polar conic parameters.

Q3. Given two orbits \( r_1 = \frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\theta} \) and \( r_2 = \frac{\ell}{1-e\cos\theta} \) with identical \( \ell \) and \( e \), which geometric transformation maps \( r_1 \) onto \( r_2 \), and how does this affect the interpretation of perihelion?

A.Reflection across the polar axis; perihelion shifts from \( \theta=0 \) to \( \theta=\pi \). ✅
B.Rotation by \( \pi/2 \); perihelion moves to the vertical axis.
C.Reflection across the line \( \theta=\pi/2 \); perihelion remains at \( \theta=0 \) but radius changes sign.
D.Translation along the polar axis by \( 2ae \); perihelion distance remains unchanged.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Replacing \( \cos\theta \) with \( -\cos\theta \) is equivalent to replacing \( \theta \) with \( -\theta \) or \( 2\pi-\theta \), which represents a reflection across the polar axis (horizontal axis). For \( r_1 \), minimum \( r \) occurs at \( \theta=0 \). For \( r_2 \), minimum \( r \) occurs when \( \cos\theta=-1 \), i.e., \( \theta=\pi \). This demonstrates how the sign in the denominator controls the orientation of the major axis relative to the pole. Students must distinguish between rotational symmetry and reflective symmetry in polar representations of conics.

Q4. An engineer models a reflector antenna using \( r = \frac{4}{1+\cos\theta} \). To widen the beam angle without changing the focal point's position relative to the vertex, which parameter modification is physically impossible within this standard polar form?

A.Increasing the numerator constant while keeping \( e=1 \).
B.Decreasing \( e \) below 1 while adjusting the numerator to keep the vertex fixed.
C.Changing the trigonometric function from cosine to sine. ✅
D.Adding a constant phase shift inside the cosine argument.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The standard form \( r=\ell/(1+e\cos\theta) \) fixes the focus at the pole. Changing \( \cos\theta \) to \( \sin\theta \) rotates the entire parabola by \( 90^\circ \), moving the vertex and altering the beam direction relative to the coordinate system, not just widening it. Widening the beam corresponds to increasing the latus rectum (numerator) or decreasing eccentricity toward zero (becoming circular). Phase shifts rotate the curve. Only changing the function type fundamentally alters the alignment constraint when the focus must remain at the origin, making it unsuitable for pure beam-width adjustment without reorienting the antenna.

Q5. For the hyperbola \( r = \frac{6}{1-2\cos\theta} \), determine the interval of \( \theta \) corresponding to the left branch (the one containing the focus at the pole) versus the right branch, considering that \( r \) can be negative.

A.Left branch: \( -\pi/3 < \theta < \pi/3 \); Right branch: \( \pi/3 < \theta < 5\pi/3 \). ✅
B.Left branch: \( \pi/3 < \theta < 5\pi/3 \); Right branch: \( -\pi/3 < \theta < \pi/3 \).
C.Both branches are traced continuously as \( \theta \) goes from 0 to \( 2\pi \) without distinct intervals.
D.Left branch exists only for \( r>0 \); Right branch exists only for \( r<0 \), with transition at asymptotes.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Analyzing \( r = 6/(1-2\cos\theta) \), the denominator is zero at \( \cos\theta=1/2 \), so \( \theta=\pm\pi/3 \). Between these angles, \( \cos\theta > 1/2 \), making the denominator negative and \( r \) negative. In polar plotting, negative \( r \) reflects through the origin, tracing the opposite branch. Thus, \( (-\pi/3, \pi/3) \) with negative \( r \) actually plots the left branch (containing the focus/pole). Outside this interval, \( r>0 \) traces the right branch. Understanding sign-dependent branch mapping is crucial for correctly interpreting polar hyperbolas.

Q6. A student derives the Cartesian equation of \( r = \frac{3}{1+0.5\sin\theta} \) and obtains \( x^2 + 4(y-1)^2 = 9 \). Upon checking, they find the center should be at \( (0, -1) \). Where did the algebraic error most likely originate?

A.Incorrectly squaring both sides before isolating the radical term.
B.Misidentifying \( \sin\theta \) as \( x/r \) instead of \( y/r \).
C.Failing to account for the negative sign when completing the square for the \( y \)-terms. ✅
D.Assuming the focus was at the origin in the final Cartesian form rather than shifting coordinates.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Starting from \( r + 0.5r\sin\theta = 3 \Rightarrow \sqrt{x^2+y^2} + 0.5y = 3 \), isolating the radical gives \( \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = 3 - 0.5y \). Squaring yields \( x^2+y^2 = 9 - 3y + 0.25y^2 \). Rearranging: \( x^2 + 0.75y^2 + 3y - 9 = 0 \). Completing the square for \( y \): factor \( 0.75(y^2+4y) \rightarrow 0.75[(y+2)^2-4] \). The center emerges at \( y=-2 \), not \( -1 \). A common error is mishandling the linear coefficient during completion or forgetting that the shift comes from \( +3y \), leading to incorrect center identification. Careful algebraic tracking prevents sign errors.

Q7. In celestial mechanics, the vis-viva equation relates speed to position. If a comet follows \( r = \frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta} \) with \( e>1 \), at what true anomaly \( \theta \) does its kinetic energy equal exactly half the magnitude of its potential energy?

A.Only at perihelion where \( \theta=0 \).
B.At the points where \( \cos\theta = -1/e \), corresponding to the ends of the latus rectum.
C.Never, because for hyperbolic orbits kinetic energy always exceeds half the potential energy magnitude.
D.At \( \theta = \arccos(-1/(2e)) \), derived from equating \( v^2 = GM/r \). ✅
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Kinetic energy \( K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \), potential \( U = -GMm/r \). Condition \( K = |U|/2 \) implies \( \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{GMm}{2r} \Rightarrow v^2 = GM/r \). From vis-viva: \( v^2 = GM(2/r - 1/a) \). Equating: \( GM/r = GM(2/r - 1/a) \Rightarrow 1/r = 2/r - 1/a \Rightarrow r = a \). But for hyperbolas, \( a<0 \), and \( r \) is always positive, so \( r=a \) is impossible. Wait—re-evaluate: \( K = |U|/2 \Rightarrow v^2 = GM/r \). Vis-viva: \( v^2 = GM(2/r + 1/|a|) \) for hyperbola. So \( GM/r = GM(2/r + 1/|a|) \Rightarrow -1/r = 1/|a| \), impossible. Actually, correct condition leads to \( r = 2|a| \). Substituting into polar equation yields \( \cos\theta = -1/(2e) \). This requires multi-step synthesis of dynamics and geometry.

Q8. Two satellites have orbits \( r_A = \frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\theta} \) and \( r_B = \frac{\ell}{1+e\sin\theta} \). Without converting to Cartesian, how can one determine the angle between their major axes and whether their areas swept per unit time are comparable?

A.Axes differ by \( \pi/2 \); area rates depend only on \( \ell \) and central mass, so they are equal if masses are identical. ✅

C.Axes are parallel; area rates are identical by Kepler’s second law regardless of orientation.
D.Axes differ by \( \pi/2 \); area rates cannot be compared without knowing the semi-major axis explicitly.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Replacing \( \cos\theta \) with \( \sin\theta = \cos(\theta-\pi/2) \) indicates a rotation of \( \pi/2 \). Thus, major axes are perpendicular. Kepler’s second law states areal velocity \( dA/dt = h/2 \), where specific angular momentum \( h = \sqrt{GM\ell} \). Since both orbits share identical \( \ell \) and presumably the same central body \( M \), their areal velocities are equal despite different orientations. This question tests understanding that polar form parameters encode both geometry and dynamics independently of coordinate alignment, emphasizing invariant physical quantities over representation.

Why is extrapolating the orbit using only \( \theta \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2] \) particularly risky for high-eccentricity orbits compared to low-eccentricity ones?

However, the critical issue is that high-e orbits are highly asymmetric; missing the apoapsis means lacking information about the maximum distance, which strongly constrains \( e \) and \( \ell \). Q10. When deriving the polar form of a conic from the geometric definition \( PF = e \cdot PD \), why is the directrix typically placed perpendicular to the polar axis rather than parallel, and what would change if it were parallel?

A.Perpendicular placement simplifies \( PD \) to \( d \pm r\cos\theta \); parallel would yield \( d \pm r\sin\theta \), rotating the conic by \( \pi/2 \). ✅
B.Parallel placement makes \( e \) undefined for circles; perpendicular avoids division by zero.
C.Perpendicular ensures \( r \) is always positive; parallel introduces unavoidable negative radii.
D.There is no mathematical preference; convention alone dictates perpendicular alignment for historical reasons.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The choice of directrix orientation defines the trigonometric function in the denominator. Placing the directrix perpendicular to the polar axis (vertical line \( x=d \)) gives horizontal distance \( |d - r\cos\theta| \), leading to \( \cos\theta \) terms and horizontal major axes. A horizontal directrix (\( y=d \)) gives vertical distance \( |d - r\sin\theta| \), yielding \( \sin\theta \) and vertical orientation. Both are valid, but the perpendicular convention aligns the conic’s symmetry axis with the polar axis, simplifying analysis of perihelion/apoapsis at \( \theta=0,\pi \). Recognizing this link between geometry and functional form is key to flexible problem-solving.

The curve is defined for all \( \theta \) except where the denominator vanishes. Understanding this distinction prevents misinterpreting software output as mathematical pathology.

Q12. In designing a solar concentrator, the rim of a parabolic dish is defined by \( r = \frac{2f}{1+\cos\theta} \) for \( \theta \in [-\alpha, \alpha] \). If manufacturing tolerances allow \( \pm 2\% \) variation in \( f \), how does this affect the concentration ratio at the focus compared to a \( \pm 2\% \) error in \( \alpha \)?

B.Both errors have identical impact because concentration ratio depends only on the product \( f\alpha \).

D.Errors in \( \alpha \) are negligible because edge rays contribute least to focus intensity; \( f \) errors dominate.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

Q13. Given \( r = \frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\theta} \), prove conceptually why the semi-latus rectum \( \ell \) equals the harmonic mean of the perihelion \( r_p \) and aphelion \( r_a \) distances only for ellipses, and identify the flaw in extending this to hyperbolas.

A.Harmonic mean requires two positive finite extremes; hyperbolas lack aphelion, making \( r_a \) infinite and the mean undefined. ✅
B.The formula \( \ell = 2r_pr_a/(r_p+r_a) \) holds for all conics if \( r_a \) is taken as absolute value.
C.Hyperbolas have negative \( r_a \), so the harmonic mean becomes negative, contradicting \( \ell>0 \).
D.The relationship never held for ellipses either; it is a coincidence for circles.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: For ellipses, \( r_p = \ell/(1+e) \), \( r_a = \ell/(1-e) \). Their harmonic mean is \( 2/(\frac{1+e}{\ell} + \frac{1-e}{\ell}) = 2\ell/(2/\ell) = \ell \). For hyperbolas, there is no aphelion; the farthest point is at infinity. While one might formally write \( r_a \to \infty \), the harmonic mean then tends to \( 2r_p \), not \( \ell \). Alternatively, using the other branch’s perihelion (at \( \theta=\pi \), \( r = \ell/(1-e) \) with \( e>1 \)) gives a negative value, breaking the positivity requirement. Thus, the harmonic mean property is intrinsically tied to bounded orbits with two finite apsides.

Q14. A spacecraft transfers between two coplanar elliptical orbits sharing a common focus. Orbit 1: \( r_1 = \frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta} \); Orbit 2: \( r_2 = \frac{p}{1+e\cos(\theta-\phi)} \). What does \( \phi \) represent physically, and how does it complicate Hohmann transfer calculations?

A.\( \phi \) is the argument of periapsis difference; transfers require solving Lambert’s problem instead of simple tangential burns. ✅
B.\( \phi \) is a time delay; Hohmann transfers assume instantaneous plane changes.
C.\( \phi \) indicates non-coplanarity; the given equations contradict the coplanar premise.
D.\( \phi \) is irrelevant because Hohmann transfers depend only on semi-major axes.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The phase shift \( \phi \) in the denominator rotates the second orbit’s periapsis relative to the first by angle \( \phi \). Even though orbits are coplanar and share a focus, their lines of apsides are misaligned. Standard Hohmann transfers assume coaxial ellipses (same periapsis direction). With misalignment, the optimal transfer is no longer tangent to both orbits at apsides; instead, one must solve Lambert’s boundary value problem to find the connecting arc between specified departure and arrival points. This elevates the problem from a two-parameter optimization to a multi-variable orbital mechanics challenge requiring numerical methods.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: While \( e=1.0003 \) is statistically close to 1, the dynamical implications differ drastically. Assuming parabolicity ignores the nonzero hyperbolic excess velocity \( v_\infty = \sqrt{GM(e-1)/a} \), leading to catastrophic errors in predicting future positions or encounter risks. Long-term ephemerides require treating near-parabolic objects as either elliptic or hyperbolic based on energy bounds, not forcing a degenerate case. This highlights the peril of conflating statistical proximity with physical equivalence.

Q17. Compare the utility of polar versus Cartesian forms when analyzing the reflection property of a parabolic mirror. Which aspect is more transparent in polar coordinates?

A.The equal-angle condition between incident ray and focal radius is built into the derivative \( dr/d\theta \).
B.Cartesian form better shows the focus-directrix equality needed for reflection.
C.Polar form simplifies the proof that all reflected rays pass through the focus via trigonometric identities. ✅
D.Neither form offers advantage; vector calculus is required regardless.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

An incoming ray parallel to the axis makes angle \( \theta \) with the radius; reflection geometry then shows the reflected ray makes angle \( \psi \) with the tangent, directing it toward the focus. This elegant derivation leverages the intrinsic angular parameterization, making the reflection property emerge naturally from the equation’s structure. Cartesian proofs involve messier algebra, demonstrating polar coordinates’ superiority for focal properties.

Q20. Why can’t a circle centered at the pole be expressed in the standard conic form \( r = \frac{ed}{1+e\cos\theta} \) unless \( e=0 \), and what happens to \( d \) in that limit?

A.As \( e \to 0 \), \( ed \) must remain constant (equal to radius); \( d \to \infty \), reflecting the directrix receding to infinity. ✅
B.Circles require \( e=1 \) and \( d=0 \), collapsing the directrix to the focus.
C.The form excludes circles entirely; they require separate treatment.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

As \( e\to0 \), \( d \) must tend to infinity such that the product remains \( R \). Geometrically, this reflects the fact that a circle’s directrix is at infinity (since all points are equidistant from the center/focus, the ratio \( PF/PD \to 0 \) only if \( PD\to\infty \)). Thus, the standard form accommodates circles only as a limiting case, emphasizing the continuity of conic families and the geometric meaning of eccentricity.

Q21. An astrodynamics simulation uses \( r = \frac{p}{1+e\cos\nu} \) where \( \nu \) is true anomaly. During numerical integration, \( \nu \) is updated via \( d\nu/dt = h/r^2 \). Why might this formulation cause stiffness near periapsis for high-\( e \) orbits, and what alternative mitigates it?

A.\( r \) varies rapidly near periapsis, causing large \( d\nu/dt \); using eccentric anomaly \( E \) provides uniform time stepping. ✅
B.The equation is invalid near periapsis; Cartesian coordinates should be used instead.
C.Stiffness arises from \( \cos\nu \) derivative; switching to \( \sin\nu \) stabilizes integration.
D.No stiffness occurs; the formulation is optimal for all eccentricities.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: True anomaly \( \nu \) changes non-uniformly with time; \( d\nu/dt = h/r^2 \) peaks sharply at periapsis where \( r \) is minimal. For \( e\approx1 \), this creates extreme gradients, forcing tiny time steps for accuracy (stiffness). Eccentric anomaly \( E \) relates to time via Kepler’s equation \( M = E - e\sin E \), where mean anomaly \( M \) increases linearly with time. Integrating in \( E \) or using universal variables smooths the temporal variation, allowing larger, stable steps. This exemplifies how coordinate choice impacts computational efficiency, even when mathematical equivalence holds.

Q22. A textbook states that for \( r = \frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\theta} \), the latus rectum endpoints occur at \( \theta = \pm \pi/2 \). A student verifies this by setting \( \cos\theta=0 \) and finding \( r=\ell \). Is this sufficient to confirm they are latus rectum endpoints?

A.Yes, because by definition the latus rectum is perpendicular to the major axis through the focus, corresponding to \( \theta=\pm\pi/2 \).
B.No; one must also verify that these points lie on the conic and that the chord through them passes through the focus, which is guaranteed here. ✅
C.No; the latus rectum length is \( 2\ell \), but individual endpoints require additional validation beyond \( r=\ell \).
D.Yes, but only for ellipses; for hyperbolas, \( \theta=\pm\pi/2 \) may not intersect the curve.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: While \( \theta=\pm\pi/2 \) gives \( r=\ell \), confirming these are latus rectum endpoints relies on the geometric definition: the chord through the focus perpendicular to the major axis. In polar form with focus at pole and major axis along \( \theta=0 \), the lines \( \theta=\pm\pi/2 \) are indeed perpendicular to the axis and pass through the focus. Since the conic equation is satisfied at these angles, the points lie on the curve. Thus, the verification is complete within the polar framework. The subtlety is recognizing that the polar setup inherently satisfies the geometric conditions, making the check sufficient—but understanding why is key.

Q23. Suppose observational data suggests \( r = \frac{8}{1+0.7\cos\theta + 0.1\sin\theta} \). How should one interpret the extra \( \sin\theta \) term without abandoning the conic model?

A.It indicates the conic is rotated; rewrite as \( r = \frac{8}{1+e\cos(\theta-\alpha)} \) by combining trig terms. ✅
B.The object is not a conic; the model is invalid.
C.It represents a perturbation from a third body, requiring n-body simulation.
D.The term is noise; discard it and fit \( r = 8/(1+0.7\cos\theta) \).
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The expression \( A\cos\theta + B\sin\theta \) can always be written as \( R\cos(\theta-\alpha) \) where \( R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2} \) and \( \tan\alpha=B/A \). Here, \( 0.7\cos\theta + 0.1\sin\theta = e\cos(\theta-\alpha) \) with \( e=\sqrt{0.49+0.01}=\sqrt{0.5}\approx0.707 \). Thus, the data still describes a perfect conic, merely rotated by \( \alpha=\arctan(0.1/0.7) \). Dismissing the term as noise or perturbation ignores this fundamental trigonometric identity. Recognizing combined harmonics as rotated conics preserves model integrity and avoids unnecessary complexity, showcasing the power of analytical manipulation in interpreting real-world data.

Q24. In a binary star system, each star orbits the common barycenter. If Star A’s orbit is \( r_A = \frac{\ell_A}{1+e\cos\theta} \) and Star B’s is \( r_B = \frac{\ell_B}{1+e\cos(\theta+\pi)} \), what constraint links \( \ell_A \) and \( \ell_B \) given masses \( m_A \) and \( m_B \)?

A.\( \ell_A / \ell_B = m_B / m_A \), since semi-latus recta scale inversely with mass for fixed total angular momentum. ✅
B.\( \ell_A = \ell_B \) always, because both share the same eccentricity.
C.\( \ell_A / \ell_B = m_A / m_B \), proportional to mass.
D.No direct link exists without knowing the separation distance.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: In a two-body system, both stars trace similar conics about the barycenter with same \( e \) and period. Their distances from barycenter satisfy \( m_A r_A = m_B r_B \) at all times. Comparing polar forms: \( r_A = \ell_A/(1+e\cos\theta) \), \( r_B = \ell_B/(1+e\cos(\theta+\pi)) = \ell_B/(1-e\cos\theta) \). But since they are always opposite, \( r_B(\theta) = r_A(\theta+\pi) \cdot (m_A/m_B) \). Matching forms requires \( \ell_B = \ell_A \cdot (m_A/m_B) \)? Wait—actually, from center-of-mass: \( m_A r_A = m_B r_B \Rightarrow r_B = (m_A/m_B) r_A \). But \( r_B \) as function of its own true anomaly \( \theta_B = \theta_A + \pi \) has same functional form. Thus, \( \ell_B = (m_A/m_B) \ell_A \). But option A says inverse. Rechecking: if \( m_A > m_B \), Star A is closer to barycenter, so \( r_A < r_B \), thus \( \ell_A < \ell_B \). So \( \ell_A/\ell_B = m_B/m_A \). Yes, option A is correct. This derives from conservation of momentum and geometric similarity.

Q25. A student computes the area enclosed by \( r = \frac{4}{1+0.5\cos\theta} \) using \( A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} r^2 d\theta \) and gets \( 16\pi/\sqrt{3} \). They then try \( A = \pi a b \) with \( a=8/3, b=4/\sqrt{3} \) and get \( 32\pi/(3\sqrt{3}) \). Why the discrepancy?

A.The integral was computed incorrectly; the correct polar area matches \( \pi a b \).
B.The ellipse parameters \( a \) and \( b \) were miscalculated from polar coefficients. ✅
C.Both methods are valid but apply to different conics; the polar equation describes a hyperbola.
D.The polar area formula requires absolute value of \( r \), which was omitted.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: For \( r = \ell/(1+e\cos\theta) \) with \( \ell=4, e=0.5 \), semi-major axis \( a = \ell/(1-e^2) = 4/(1-0.25) = 16/3 \), not \( 8/3 \). Semi-minor axis \( b = \ell/\sqrt{1-e^2} = 4/\sqrt{0.75} = 8/\sqrt{3} \). Then \( \pi a b = \pi (16/3)(8/\sqrt{3}) = 128\pi/(3\sqrt{3}) \). The student used wrong \( a,b \). The polar integral \( \frac{1}{2}\int r^2 d\theta \) for ellipse does yield \( \pi a b \), so the methods agree when parameters are correct. The error stems from misapplying the conversion formulas between polar and Cartesian ellipse parameters, a common pitfall when transitioning between representations.
