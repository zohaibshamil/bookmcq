What is Polar Coordinates?

Definition: Polar coordinates represent a point by \( (r, \theta) \) where \( r \) is the distance from the origin (pole) and \( \theta \) is the angle from the positive x-axis (polar axis). Conversion: \( x = r\cos\theta, y = r\sin\theta \), and \( r^2 = x^2+y^2, \tan\theta = y/x \).

Example: The point \( (3, \pi/3) \) has rectangular coordinates \( x=3\cos(\pi/3)=1.5, y=3\sin(\pi/3)=2.598 \). Conversely, \( (1,1) \) has \( r=\sqrt{2}, \theta=\pi/4 \).

Reason: Polar coordinates simplify equations for circles, spirals, and curves with radial symmetry, making graphing and calculus easier in many physics problems.

Q1. A particle moves along a path defined by \( r = 4\cos(2\theta) \). At which values of \( \theta \) in \( [0, 2\pi) \) does the particle pass through the pole, and what is the geometric significance of these angles regarding the curve's symmetry?

A. \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{3\pi}{4}, \frac{5\pi}{4}, \frac{7\pi}{4}\); these define the axes of symmetry for the four petals ✅
B. \(\theta = 0, \frac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}\); these represent the maximum radial extent of each petal
C. \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}, \frac{7\pi}{6}, \frac{11\pi}{6}\); these indicate inflection points where curvature changes sign
D. \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{8}, \frac{3\pi}{8}, \frac{5\pi}{8}, \frac{7\pi}{8}\); these mark the midpoints between consecutive petals

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The particle passes through the pole when \( r = 0 \), so solving \( 4\cos(2\theta) = 0 \) yields \( 2\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi \), giving \( \theta = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{n\pi}{2} \). These four angles correspond to the lines \( y = x \), \( y = -x \), etc., which are precisely the symmetry axes of the rose curve. This connects algebraic solution to geometric structure, requiring understanding that zeros of \( r \) in even-petal roses align with symmetry lines, not petal tips.

Q3. Two curves are given: \( C_1: r = 3\cos\theta \) and \( C_2: r = 3\sin\theta \). Without converting to Cartesian coordinates, determine the angle between their tangent lines at their non-origin intersection point.

A. \( \frac{\pi}{4} \)
B. \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) ✅
C. \(\frac{\pi}{3}\)
D. \(\frac{\pi}{6}\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Both are circles of diameter 3 centered on the x- and y-axes respectively. They intersect when \( 3\cos\theta = 3\sin\theta \Rightarrow \tan\theta = 1 \Rightarrow \theta = \pi/4 \). In polar coordinates, the angle \( \psi \) between radius vector and tangent satisfies \( \tan\psi = r/(dr/d\theta) \). For \( C_1 \), \( \tan\psi_1 = 3\cos\theta / (-3\sin\theta) = -\cot\theta \); at \( \theta=\pi/4 \), \( \psi_1 = 3\pi/4 \). For \( C_2 \), \( \tan\psi_2 = 3\sin\theta / (3\cos\theta) = \tan\theta \); at \( \theta=\pi/4 \), \( \psi_2 = \pi/4 \). The angle between tangents is \( |\psi_1 - \psi_2| = \pi/2 \). This uses intrinsic polar geometry without coordinate conversion.

Q4. A region is bounded by \( r = 2 + \cos\theta \) and \( r = 2 - \cos\theta \). Which integral correctly computes the area of their overlapping region?

D. \(\2\int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1}{2}(2 + \cos\theta)^2 d\theta\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The curves are symmetric about both axes. They intersect when \( 2 + \cos\theta = 2 - \cos\theta \Rightarrow \cos\theta = 0 \Rightarrow \theta = \pi/2, 3\pi/2 \). In \( [0, \pi/2] \), \( 2 - \cos\theta \leq 2 + \cos\theta \), so the inner boundary is \( r = 2 - \cos\theta \). By four-fold symmetry, total area is \( 4 \times \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi/2} (2 - \cos\theta)^2 d\theta \). Option C matches this. Distractors misuse symmetry bounds or subtract areas incorrectly. This requires identifying the correct bounding curve in each sector and applying symmetry properly.

Q5. Given the polar graph of \( r = f(\theta) \) has a cusp at \( \theta = \alpha \), which condition must hold?

A. \( f(\alpha) = 0 \) and \( f'(\alpha) = 0 \) ✅
B. \(\f(\alpha) \neq 0 \) and \( f'(\alpha) = 0 \)
C. \(\f(\alpha) = 0 \) and \( f'(\alpha) \neq 0 \)
D. \(\f'(\alpha) \) is undefined

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

A cusp in polar coordinates occurs when the curve reaches the pole (\( r=0 \)) and the derivative also vanishes, causing the tangent direction to reverse abruptly. If \( f(\alpha)=0 \) but \( f'(\alpha)\neq0 \), the curve passes smoothly through the pole with a well-defined tangent. If \( f(\alpha)\neq0 \) and \( f'=0 \), it’s a local max/min of \( r \), not a cusp. Undefined derivative may indicate vertical tangent but not necessarily a cusp. Thus, both \( r=0 \) and \( dr/d\theta=0 \) are necessary for a true cusp, linking analytic behavior to geometric singularity.

This tests understanding that polar equations can represent complete geometric objects even if intermediate expressions have domain restrictions.

Q7. For the spiral \( r = e^{\theta} \), the angle \( \phi \) between the radius vector and the tangent line is constant. What is this angle, and why does this property make the spiral useful in modeling natural growth patterns?

A. \(\phi = \pi/4\); constant angle ensures proportional growth rate relative to distance from origin ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For \( r = e^\theta \), \( dr/d\theta = e^\theta = r \), so \( \tan\phi = r/(dr/d\theta) = 1 \Rightarrow \phi = \pi/4 \). This constant angle defines a logarithmic (equiangular) spiral. In nature, such spirals appear in shells and galaxies because growth occurs proportionally to current size, maintaining self-similarity. The constant angle encodes this scale-invariant property. Option D is tempting but imprecise; \( \arctan(1) = \pi/4 \), but the key is the constancy, not just the value. This links calculus, geometry, and real-world modeling.

Q10. Compare the arc length computation for \( r = 2\cos\theta \) over \( [0, \pi] \) versus its Cartesian equivalent \( x^2 + y^2 = 2x \). Which approach is more efficient and why?

Q11. If \( r = f(\theta) \) is periodic with period \( 2\pi/n \) where \( n \) is an odd integer, how many distinct petals does the rose curve \( r = \cos(n\theta) \) have, and why does parity matter?

A. \( n \) petals; odd \( n \) causes each petal to be traced twice over \( [0, 2\pi) \)
B. \(\2n \) petals; odd \( n \) still generates double coverage
C. \(\n \) petals; odd \( n \) results in single tracing per petal over \( [0, \pi) \) ✅
D. \(\n/2 \) petals; symmetry reduces count for odd \( n \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

For \( r = \cos(n\theta) \) with odd \( n \), the curve completes in \( [0, \pi) \) because \( \cos(n(\theta + \pi)) = \cos(n\theta + n\pi) = -\cos(n\theta) \), and since \( r \) can be negative, this retraces existing petals rather than creating new ones. Thus, only \( n \) distinct petals exist, each traced once in \( [0, \pi) \). For even \( n \), \( \cos(n(\theta + \pi)) = \cos(n\theta) \), so \( [0, \pi) \) only covers half the petals, requiring \( [0, 2\pi) \) for \( 2n \) petals. Parity determines whether negative \( r \) produces new geometry or redundancy.

Meanwhile \( y = r\sin\theta = \tan\theta\sin\theta = \sin^2\theta/\cos\theta \to \infty \). Thus, curve approaches vertical line \( x=1 \) asymptotically. Not a closed loop. This requires combining limits with coordinate definitions to infer asymptotic behavior, going beyond simple divergence. Demonstrates sophisticated interpretation of polar singularities.

Q23. When deriving the polar area formula \( A = \frac{1}{2}\int r^2 d\theta \), which assumption is implicitly made about the curve’s behavior?

A. The curve does not intersect itself within the interval of integration
B. \(\r(\theta) \) is continuously differentiable
C. \The region is star-shaped with respect to the origin ✅
D. \(\r(\theta) \geq 0 \) throughout the interval

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The derivation approximates area as sum of circular sectors, which assumes every ray from origin intersects the boundary exactly once—i.e., star-shaped w.r.t. origin. Self-intersections violate this, requiring subdivision. Non-negativity isn’t required since \( r^2 \) handles sign. Differentiability ensures smoothness but isn’t fundamental to area definition. Star-shaped condition is core geometric assumption. Misunderstanding this leads to incorrect application to non-star-shaped regions like limaçons with inner loops. This tests foundational understanding of formula limitations.

Q24. A student computes the length of \( r = 1 + \cos\theta \) using \( L = \int_0^{2\pi} \sqrt{(1+\cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta} d\theta \) and gets \( 8 \). Is this correct, and what conceptual check validates it?

A. Yes; cardioid length is known to be 8
B. No; should be \( \int_0^{2\pi} \sqrt{2 + 2\cos\theta} d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 2|\cos(\theta/2)| d\theta = 8 \), so actually correct ✅
C. No; missed factor of 2 in derivative term
D. Yes, but only because cardioid is symmetric; asymmetric curves would fail

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Simplifying integrand: \( (1+\cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta = 1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2(\theta/2) \). Square root gives \( 2|\cos(\theta/2)| \). Integral over \( [0,2\pi] \) is \( 2 \times \int_0^\pi 2\cos(\theta/2) d\theta = 8 \) (since cos positive in [0,π]). Result is correct. Validation comes from known cardioid perimeter or dimensional consistency. This confirms computational accuracy and reinforces trigonometric simplification skills in polar calculus.
