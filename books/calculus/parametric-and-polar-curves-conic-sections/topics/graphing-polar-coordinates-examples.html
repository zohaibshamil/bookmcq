What is Graphing polar coordinates examples?

Definition: To graph polar points, plot angle \( \theta \) from polar axis, then move distance \( r \) along that ray (if \( r>0 \)) or opposite ray (if \( r<0 \)). Connect points smoothly to form curve.
Example: Plot \( (2, \pi/2) \) = point at 90°, radius 2 → (0,2). Plot \( (-3, \pi) \): negative means opposite of 180° is 0°, so point at \( r=3 \), angle 0° → (3,0). Graph \( r=2\cos\theta \): points form circle centered at (1,0) radius 1.
Reason: Graphing gives visual understanding of polar equations, showing symmetry and shape, essential for identifying curves like circles, limacons, and roses.

Q1. A student graphs \( r = 2 + 3\cos(\theta) \) and claims the curve passes through the pole because \( r \) can be negative. Which statement best analyzes this error in reasoning regarding polar coordinates?

C.The student failed to convert to Cartesian coordinates first, which is required to verify pole intersection.
D.The equation represents a circle that never touches the pole regardless of the sign of \( r \).
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: This question targets error analysis by addressing the common misconception that negative \( r \) implies passing through the origin. In polar coordinates, the pole corresponds strictly to \( r=0 \). For \( r = 2 + 3\cos(\theta) \), solving \( 2+3\cos(\theta)=0 \) yields valid angles, so it does pass through the pole, but not *because* \( r \) is negative elsewhere. The explanation clarifies that while the conclusion might accidentally be true for this specific limaçon, the student's reasoning linking negative values directly to pole intersection is fundamentally flawed and would fail for equations like \( r = 3 + 2\cos(\theta) \).

Q3. A particle moves along the path \( r = e^{\theta} \) for \( \theta \geq 0 \). If the particle’s angular velocity \( d\theta/dt \) is constant, how does its radial speed \( dr/dt \) change as it spirals outward?

A.Radial speed remains constant because angular velocity is constant.
B.Radial speed decreases inversely proportional to \( r \).
C.Radial speed increases exponentially, maintaining direct proportionality to the current radius. ✅
D.Radial speed oscillates between zero and a maximum value depending on the quadrant.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: This application question links polar calculus to kinematics. Given \( r = e^{\theta} \), differentiating with respect to time using the chain rule gives \( dr/dt = e^{\theta} \cdot d\theta/dt \). Since \( r = e^{\theta} \), this simplifies to \( dr/dt = r \cdot d\theta/dt \). With constant angular velocity, radial speed is directly proportional to \( r \). As the spiral expands, \( r \) grows exponentially, meaning the particle must move radially faster and faster to maintain constant angular rotation. This tests multi-step reasoning connecting parametric derivatives to physical motion interpretation.

Q13. Consider \( r = \tan(\theta) \) for \( \theta \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2) \). As \( \theta \to \pm\pi/2 \), \( r \to \pm\infty \). What geometric feature does this asymptotic behavior create?

Alternatively, converting to Cartesian: \( r^2 = r\sin(\theta) + r\cos(\theta) \) → \( x^2+y^2 = y+x \) → \( (x-1/2)^2 + (y-1/2)^2 = 1/2 \), confirming radius \( \sqrt{1/2} \). Avoiding integration demonstrates mastery of coordinate interplay and recognition of conic forms in polar disguise, valuing structural understanding over algorithmic execution.

Q26. A model uses \( r = |\cos(2\theta)| \) instead of \( r = \cos(2\theta) \). How does the absolute value alter the geometric object fundamentally?

A.It eliminates negative lobes, doubling the visible petal count from 4 to 8.
B.It reflects negative portions into positive quadrants, preserving 4-petal structure but changing traversal. ✅
C.It creates a circle by removing angular dependence.
D.It has no geometric effect since area depends on \( r^2 \).
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: This conceptual understanding question examines absolute value impact. Original \( \cos(2\theta) \) has 4 petals (2 positive, 2 negative tracing same locations). Taking absolute value makes all \( r \geq 0 \), but since negative lobes already occupied same spatial regions as positive ones in rose curves with even \( n \), the geometric shape remains 4 petals. However, parameterization changes: what was traced via negative \( r \) now traces via positive \( r \) at different \( \theta \). Shape identical, traversal different. Option A is tempting but incorrect for even \( n \); absolute value doesn't create new spatial regions here.
