What is Comparison, Ratio and Root Tests for series?

These are three major tests: Comparison (compare \( a_n \) to a known series), Ratio (use \( \lim |a_{n+1}/a_n| \)), and Root (use \( \lim |a_n|^{1/n} \)); all three help determine convergence for positive series, with ratio and root especially useful for series with factorials or exponentials.

Q1. A student analyzes the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!}{3^k} \) using the Ratio Test and calculates \( \rho = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{k+1}{3} = \infty \). They conclude the series converges because factorials grow faster than exponentials. Which statement best identifies the flaw in this reasoning?

A. The student correctly identified that factorials dominate exponentials, but misapplied the convergence criterion for the Ratio Test.
B. The student calculated the limit correctly as infinity, but failed to recognize that \( \rho > 1 \) implies divergence, not convergence. ✅
C. The student should have used the Root Test instead, as it is more appropriate for series involving both factorials and exponential terms.
D. The limit calculation is incorrect; the ratio of successive factorial terms simplifies to a constant less than 1, implying convergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question targets error analysis regarding the interpretation of the Ratio Test. While the student correctly computed the limit as infinity, they fundamentally misunderstood the theorem's conclusion. In the Ratio Test, a limit \( \rho > 1 \) or \( \rho = \infty \) definitively indicates that the terms do not approach zero fast enough (or at all), causing the series to diverge. The misconception that 'factorials grow faster' actually supports divergence, making the student's conclusion contradictory to their own calculation.

Q2. Consider the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{2k+3}{5k-1} \right)^k \). Without performing extensive algebraic simplification, which test provides the most direct path to determining convergence, and what is the result?

A. Ratio Test; the series diverges because the limit of the ratio exceeds 1.
B. Root Test; the series converges because the limit of the k-th root is 2/5, which is less than 1. ✅
C. Comparison Test; the series converges by comparing to a geometric series with r = 2/5.
D. Integral Test; the series converges because the corresponding improper integral evaluates to a finite number.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This application question requires selecting the optimal tool based on the structure of the general term. When the general term \( a_k \) is raised entirely to the k-th power, the Root Test is typically superior to the Ratio Test because the k-th root cancels the exponent directly. Here, \( \sqrt[k]{a_k} = \frac{2k+3}{5k-1} \), which approaches \( 2/5 \) as \( k \to \infty \). Since \( 2/5 < 1 \), the series converges absolutely. Using the Ratio Test would require messy algebra, while the Comparison Test requires justifying the inequality rigorously.

Q3. You are given two series: \( A = \sum \frac{1}{k^2 + \sqrt{k}} \) and \( B = \sum \frac{1}{k^2 - \ln k} \). A student claims both converge by Limit Comparison with \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2} \). However, for series B, they worry about the negative denominator for small k. How does this affect the validity of the test?

A. The test is invalid for series B because the terms must be positive for all k, not just eventually.
B. The test remains valid because convergence depends only on the tail behavior; finitely many undefined or negative initial terms do not affect the limit comparison. ✅
C. The student must use the Direct Comparison Test instead, as the Limit Comparison Test cannot handle logarithmic terms.
D. The test is invalid because \( \ln k \) grows slower than \( k^2 \), making the limit of the ratio equal to zero rather than a positive finite constant.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question addresses the 'eventually positive' requirement of comparison tests. Convergence is a property of the infinite tail of a series. As long as \( b_k > 0 \) for all sufficiently large k (which is true here since \( k^2 > \ln k \) for \( k \geq 2 \)), the Limit Comparison Test applies. Initial terms where the expression might be undefined or negative can be discarded without changing the convergence status. This distinguishes between the domain of the function and the asymptotic behavior required for series tests.

Q4. Analyze the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(k!)^2}{(2k)!} \). If you apply the Ratio Test, the limit \( \rho \) equals 1/4. If you apply the Root Test, calculating \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{a_k} \) is significantly more complex. Why is the Ratio Test preferred here despite both being applicable in theory?

A. The Root Test yields an indeterminate form for factorials, whereas the Ratio Test exploits the recursive definition of factorials to simplify via cancellation. ✅
B. The Ratio Test always gives a smaller limit value than the Root Test, making it easier to satisfy the convergence condition.
C. The Root Test can only be applied to geometric series, while the Ratio Test applies to any series with positive terms.
D. Both tests yield the same computational difficulty, but the Ratio Test is conventionally taught first for factorial series.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This mixed-concept question evaluates strategic test selection. Factorials are defined recursively (\( (k+1)! = (k+1)k! \)), which makes the ratio \( a_{k+1}/a_k \) ideal for telescoping cancellations. Conversely, taking the k-th root of a factorial \( \sqrt[k]{k!} \) does not simplify algebraically and typically requires Stirling’s approximation or advanced limits. Therefore, even though the Root Test is theoretically powerful, the Ratio Test is computationally superior for products and factorials. Understanding *why* a test works structurally prevents inefficient problem solving.

Q5. A graph displays the sequence of ratios \( r_k = |a_{k+1}/a_k| \) for a positive series. The plot shows \( r_k \) oscillating wildly between 0.1 and 10 for the first 50 terms, then settling into a smooth decay toward 0.8 as \( k \to \infty \). What can be definitively concluded?

A. The series diverges because the initial ratios exceed 1, indicating early growth.
B. The series converges because the limit of the ratio sequence exists and is strictly less than 1. ✅
C. The Ratio Test is inconclusive because the sequence is not monotonic.
D. The series converges only if the oscillations in the first 50 terms sum to a finite value.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This graph-based question tests understanding of limits versus transient behavior. The Ratio Test relies solely on \( \lim_{k \to \infty} |a_{k+1}/a_k| \). Finite initial fluctuations, no matter how extreme, do not affect the limit or the ultimate convergence of the series. Since the graph clearly shows the ratio approaching 0.8 (< 1), the series converges absolutely. Students often confuse local behavior with asymptotic behavior; this question reinforces that convergence tests are inherently about the 'tail' of the series.

Q6. Suppose \( \sum a_k \) is a series of positive terms where \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{a_k} = 1 \). A peer argues that since the Root Test is inconclusive, the series must be compared to the harmonic series \( \sum 1/k \). Is this a valid next step?

A. Yes, because when the Root Test limit is 1, the series behaves identically to the harmonic series.
B. No, a limit of 1 in the Root Test means the series could converge (like \( \sum 1/k^2 \)) or diverge (like \( \sum 1/k \)); further analysis using a different method is required. ✅
C. Yes, because the Root Test failing implies the terms decay exactly at the rate of 1/k.
D. No, one should immediately switch to the Ratio Test, which will definitely provide a conclusive answer when the Root Test fails.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question addresses the ambiguity of the inconclusive case. When \( \rho = 1 \) in either the Ratio or Root Test, the series lies on the boundary between convergence and divergence. It could be a p-series with p > 1 (convergent) or p ≤ 1 (divergent). Automatically defaulting to the harmonic series is a logical fallacy. The correct procedure is to recognize the limitation and employ a more sensitive test like the Integral Test, Direct Comparison, or Limit Comparison with an appropriate benchmark based on the specific algebraic form of \( a_k \).

Q7. In modeling population dynamics, a researcher derives a series \( \sum P_k \) where \( P_k \) represents generation size. They find \( \lim_{k \to \infty} P_{k+1}/P_k = R \). If \( R = 1 \), why is the Ratio Test insufficient for predicting long-term population stability?

A. Because populations are discrete, not continuous, so calculus-based tests never apply.
B. Because \( R=1 \) corresponds to the critical threshold where linear approximations fail; sub-dominant terms determine whether the population stabilizes or grows unboundedly. ✅
C. Because the Ratio Test only measures exponential growth, and \( R=1 \) implies the population is extinct.
D. Because biological systems always exhibit chaotic behavior when the growth rate equals unity.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This scenario-based question connects mathematical abstraction to physical modeling. In dynamical systems, a ratio limit of 1 represents a phase transition or bifurcation point. Mathematically, it corresponds to the inconclusive case where polynomial factors (ignored by the ratio of leading terms) dictate behavior. For example, \( P_k \sim 1/k \) leads to extinction/stability, while \( P_k \sim 1/\sqrt{k} \) might imply different scaling. The Ratio Test strips away these crucial lower-order details. Recognizing this limitation is vital for applying series tests to real-world models accurately.

Q8. A student attempts to prove \( \sum \frac{1}{k \ln k} \) converges by comparing it to \( \sum \frac{1}{k} \). They argue: 'Since \( k \ln k > k \) for \( k \geq 2 \), then \( \frac{1}{k \ln k} < \frac{1}{k} \). Since \( \sum 1/k \) diverges, my series must also diverge.' Identify the logical error.

A. The inequality direction is correct, but concluding divergence from being smaller than a divergent series is invalid. ✅
B. The inequality is backwards; \( k \ln k \) is actually smaller than \( k \) for large k.
C. The student should have used the Ratio Test, which conclusively shows divergence for logarithmic series.
D. There is no error; the reasoning is sound and the series indeed diverges.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis question targets the most common pitfall in the Direct Comparison Test. To prove divergence, one must show the series is *larger* than a known divergent series. Being *smaller* than a divergent series proves nothing (e.g., \( 1/k^2 < 1/k \), yet \( 1/k^2 \) converges). The student set up the inequality correctly but drew the wrong conclusion. The series actually diverges, but the justification provided is logically flawed. Correct reasoning would involve the Integral Test or comparison with a series known to diverge more slowly.

Q9. For the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k!} \), the Ratio Test yields a limit dependent on x. Determine the set of all real values x for which the series converges.

A. Only x = 0
B. All real numbers x ✅
C. Only |x| < 1
D. Only x > 0

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This direct recall/application question reinforces the fundamental behavior of factorial denominators. Applying the Ratio Test gives \( \lim |x|/(k+1) = 0 \) for any fixed real number x. Since 0 < 1 universally, the series converges absolutely for all real x. This is a defining characteristic of the exponential series. Students sometimes mistakenly believe there is a radius of convergence restriction like geometric series, but factorials dominate all polynomials and exponentials in the numerator, resulting in an infinite radius of convergence.

Q10. Consider the series \( \sum a_k \) where \( a_k = \begin{cases} 1/2^k & \text{if } k \text{ is odd} \\ 1/3^k & \text{if } k \text{ is even} \end{cases} \). Why does the Ratio Test fail to establish convergence despite the series clearly converging?

A. The series contains negative terms, violating the Ratio Test's positivity requirement.
B. The limit \( \lim |a_{k+1}/a_k| \) does not exist because the ratio alternates between values approaching 0 and infinity. ✅
C. The Ratio Test can only be applied to monotonic sequences.
D. The series is conditionally convergent, and the Ratio Test only detects absolute convergence.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This challenging question explores the limitations of the Ratio Test when the limit doesn't exist. The ratio of consecutive terms alternates: odd-to-even gives \( (1/3^{k+1})/(1/2^k) \to 0 \), while even-to-odd gives \( (1/2^{k+1})/(1/3^k) \to \infty \). Since the limit DNE, the standard Ratio Test is technically inapplicable. However, the Root Test would succeed here (\( \limsup \sqrt[k]{a_k} = 1/2 < 1 \)). This highlights that the Root Test is strictly stronger than the Ratio Test, as it handles oscillatory term behaviors via limsup.

Q11. You are analyzing \( \sum \frac{k^3 + 2k}{4k^5 - k} \). Using Limit Comparison, which benchmark series is most appropriate and what is the limiting ratio?

A. \( \sum 1/k^2 \); limit is 1/4 ✅
B. \( \sum 1/k^3 \); limit is 0
C. \( \sum 1/k^2 \); limit is 4
D. \( \sum 1/k^5 \); limit is 1

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This application question tests the heuristic of dominant terms. For rational functions, compare using the ratio of highest powers: \( k^3 / k^5 = 1/k^2 \). Computing the formal limit: \( \lim \frac{k^3+2k}{4k^5-k} \cdot k^2 = \lim \frac{k^5+2k^3}{4k^5-k} = 1/4 \). Since the limit is finite and positive, and \( \sum 1/k^2 \) converges (p=2>1), the original series converges. Choosing \( 1/k^3 \) would yield limit 0, which is less informative for proving convergence via LCT unless one recalls the extended version of the test.

Q12. A student computes \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{|a_k|} = L \) for a series. They know L < 1 but are unsure if it guarantees absolute convergence or just conditional convergence. Clarify the distinction.

A. The Root Test only establishes conditional convergence; absolute convergence requires the Ratio Test.
B. If L < 1, the series converges absolutely. Conditional convergence is impossible when the root limit is strictly less than 1. ✅
C. The Root Test determines divergence only; convergence must be verified separately.
D. Absolute convergence occurs only if L = 0; any L in (0,1) implies conditional convergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question clarifies the strength of the Root Test. Both the Ratio and Root Tests, when yielding a limit strictly less than 1, prove *absolute* convergence. Absolute convergence implies unconditional convergence. Conditional convergence only arises when the series of absolute values diverges but the original series converges (typically detected by the Alternating Series Test, not Ratio/Root). If \( \sqrt[k]{|a_k|} \to L < 1 \), the terms decay exponentially fast, ensuring \( \sum |a_k| \) converges. There is no scenario where L < 1 yields only conditional convergence.

Q13. Given the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{k} \right)^{k^2} \), a naive application of the Root Test suggests checking \( \lim (1+1/k)^k = e \). Since e > 1, the series diverges. Is this reasoning complete?

A. No, because the exponent is \( k^2 \), not k. The correct root is \( (1+1/k)^k \), which still approaches e > 1, confirming divergence. ✅
B. Yes, the reasoning is perfectly complete and rigorous.
C. No, the Root Test cannot be used because the base approaches 1.
D. No, one must use the Ratio Test because the exponent involves k squared.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question combines application with careful algebraic verification. The k-th root of \( a_k = (1+1/k)^{k^2} \) is indeed \( ((1+1/k)^{k^2})^{1/k} = (1+1/k)^k \). The limit of this expression is the definition of e ≈ 2.718. Since e > 1, the Root Test conclusively indicates divergence. The distractor suggests potential confusion about exponents, but the student's setup was actually correct. The key insight is recognizing the definition of e within the Root Test framework and correctly interpreting ρ > 1 as divergence.

Q14. When applying the Limit Comparison Test to \( \sum \frac{\sin^2 k}{k^3} \), a student chooses \( b_k = 1/k^3 \). They note that \( \lim \frac{a_k}{b_k} = \lim \sin^2 k \) does not exist. Does this invalidate the test?

A. Yes, the Limit Comparison Test requires the limit to exist and be positive.
B. No, because \( 0 \leq \sin^2 k \leq 1 \), one can use the Direct Comparison Test instead to prove convergence. ✅
C. Yes, one must choose \( b_k = \sin^2 k / k^3 \) to make the limit equal to 1.
D. No, the non-existence of the limit implies the series diverges.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis/mixed concept question addresses oscillatory numerators. The Limit Comparison Test technically requires a positive finite limit. When the ratio oscillates (like \( \sin^2 k \)), LCT in its standard form fails. However, since \( 0 \leq \sin^2 k \leq 1 \), we have \( a_k \leq 1/k^3 \). By Direct Comparison with the convergent p-series \( \sum 1/k^3 \), convergence is established. This illustrates the importance of having multiple tools: when LCT fails due to oscillation, boundedness often allows Direct Comparison to succeed.

Q15. Which of the following series requires the Root Test over the Ratio Test for efficient evaluation?

A. \( \sum \frac{n!}{n^n} \)
B. \( \sum \frac{2^n}{n!} \)
C. \( \sum \left( \frac{n+1}{2n} \right)^n \) ✅
D. \( \sum \frac{n^2}{3^n} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This direct recall question identifies structural cues for test selection. Option C has the entire term raised to the n-th power, making the n-th root trivial: \( \sqrt[n]{a_n} = \frac{n+1}{2n} \to 1/2 \). The Ratio Test would involve \( ((n+2)/(2n+2))^{n+1} / ((n+1)/2n)^n \), requiring logarithms or complex limits. Options A, B, and D involve factorials or simple exponentials where the Ratio Test causes clean cancellations. Recognizing the 'nth power' structure is a key skill for efficient series analysis.

Q16. A researcher models signal attenuation with \( \sum c_k x^k \). They determine via Ratio Test that the radius of convergence is R=5. At x=5, the Ratio Test gives limit 1. What is the most appropriate next step?

A. Conclude convergence at x=5 because the series converged for |x|<5.
B. Conclude divergence at x=5 because endpoints are never included.
C. Apply a different test (e.g., Alternating Series, p-series, or Integral Test) specifically at x=5. ✅
D. The Radius of Convergence calculation must be redone since the endpoint behavior affects R.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question addresses the boundary behavior of power series. The Ratio Test determines the open interval (-R, R) but is always inconclusive at endpoints x = ±R (where ρ=1). Endpoint convergence must be tested individually using other methods appropriate to the specific numerical series obtained at that point. The behavior at endpoints does not change R itself. This distinction between the open interval of absolute convergence and the closed interval of convergence is fundamental in analysis.

Q17. Consider \( \sum \frac{1}{k (\ln k)^p} \). For what values of p does this series converge, and which test is necessary to establish this?

A. p > 1; Integral Test ✅
B. p > 0; Ratio Test
C. p ≥ 1; Limit Comparison with 1/k
D. All real p; Root Test

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This application question covers the logarithmic p-series extension. Standard comparison tests with \( 1/k^q \) fail because logs grow slower than any power. The Integral Test is the canonical method: \( \int \frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^p} = \int u^{-p} du \) via substitution u=ln x. This converges iff p > 1. The Ratio and Root Tests both yield limit 1, providing no information. This reinforces that for series involving nested logarithms, integration is often the only viable analytical tool.

Q18. A student argues: 'Since \( \lim_{k \to \infty} a_k = 0 \) for \( \sum \frac{k}{k^2+1} \), the series converges by the Divergence Test.' Analyze this claim.

A. Correct; terms approaching zero is sufficient for convergence.
B. Incorrect; the Divergence Test only proves divergence when the limit is non-zero. Terms approaching zero is necessary but not sufficient. ✅
C. Correct; the Divergence Test confirms convergence for all rational functions.
D. Incorrect; the limit is actually 1, so the series diverges.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis question targets the most pervasive misconception in series: confusing necessary and sufficient conditions. \( a_k \to 0 \) is necessary for convergence but never sufficient (harmonic series is the counterexample). The Divergence Test is a one-way implication: \( \lim a_k \neq 0 \implies \) divergence. Its contrapositive is NOT 'limit = 0 implies convergence.' Students must learn that vanishing terms merely allow the possibility of convergence; actual proof requires accumulation tests like Integral, Comparison, etc.

Q19. For the series \( \sum \frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^2 4^k} \), the Ratio Test yields limit 1. The Root Test also yields limit 1. What refined approach determines convergence?

A. Use Stirling's approximation to show terms behave like \( 1/\sqrt{\pi k} \), implying divergence by p-series comparison. ✅
B. The series converges because factorials in the numerator are balanced by the denominator.
C. Apply L'Hôpital's Rule repeatedly to the ratio until a decisive limit emerges.
D. The series is telescoping and sums to a finite value.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This Olympiad-style question handles the delicate ρ=1 case for central binomial coefficients. Standard tests fail. Using Stirling's formula \( n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n \), the term simplifies asymptotically to \( \frac{\sqrt{4\pi k}(2k/e)^{2k}}{2\pi k (k/e)^{2k} 4^k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}} \). Since \( \sum 1/\sqrt{k} \) diverges (p=1/2), the original series diverges. This demonstrates that when elementary tests are inconclusive, asymptotic analysis becomes essential. It bridges discrete series and continuous approximation methods.

Q20. You are comparing \( \sum a_k \) and \( \sum b_k \) where \( a_k, b_k > 0 \). If \( \lim a_k/b_k = 0 \) and \( \sum b_k \) converges, what can be concluded?

A. \( \sum a_k \) diverges.
B. \( \sum a_k \) converges. ✅
C. No conclusion can be drawn about \( \sum a_k \).
D. \(\sum a_k \) converges only if \( b_k \) is a geometric series.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question covers the extended Limit Comparison Test. When the ratio limit is 0, \( a_k \) is asymptotically negligible compared to \( b_k \). If the 'larger' series \( \sum b_k \) converges, the 'smaller' series \( \sum a_k \) must also converge. This is distinct from the standard LCT (finite positive limit) but equally valid. Many students incorrectly think LCT requires a nonzero limit; understanding the directional implications of 0 and ∞ expands the test's utility significantly.

Q22. Which statement correctly describes the relationship between the Ratio Test and Root Test?

A. They are completely independent; neither implies the other.
B. If the Ratio Test limit exists, the Root Test limit exists and equals it. But the Root Test limit may exist when the Ratio Test limit does not. ✅
C. The Root Test is always easier to compute than the Ratio Test.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

This conceptual question establishes the theoretical hierarchy. The Root Test is strictly stronger: whenever \( \lim |a_{n+1}/a_n| = L \), then \( \lim \sqrt[n]{|a_n|} = L \). However, the converse fails (e.g., alternating 1/2^n, 1/3^n). The Root Test uses limsup and can handle oscillatory sequences where the ordinary ratio limit DNE. Understanding this relationship explains why textbooks present both: Ratio is computationally simpler for nice series, but Root is theoretically more robust. This meta-knowledge guides intelligent test selection.

Q23. A student applies Comparison Test to \( \sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{k^4 + k}} \). They compare to \( \sum 1/k \) and conclude divergence because \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{k^4+k}} < \frac{1}{k} \) and \( \sum 1/k \) diverges. Evaluate.

A. Correct reasoning and conclusion.
B. Incorrect reasoning; the inequality is true but being smaller than a divergent series proves nothing. Should compare to \( 1/k^2 \) for convergence. ✅
C. Incorrect inequality; \( \sqrt{k^4+k} < k^2 \), so the fraction is actually larger than \( 1/k^2 \).
D. Correct reasoning but wrong conclusion; the series actually converges.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis combines inequality direction with logical validity. First, \( \sqrt{k^4+k} > k^2 \), so \( a_k < 1/k^2 \), not 1/k. Second, even if \( a_k < 1/k \) were true, it wouldn't prove divergence. The student made two errors: wrong benchmark and wrong logic. The correct approach compares to \( 1/k^2 \) (since \( \sqrt{k^4} = k^2 \)), establishing convergence. This question forces students to simultaneously verify algebraic inequalities and logical implications of comparison tests.

Q24. For the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k^k}{k!} \), determine convergence using the most appropriate test.

A. Converges by Ratio Test
B. Diverges by Ratio Test ✅
C. Converges by Root Test
D. Diverges by Root Test

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This application question features \( k^k \) and \( k! \). The Ratio Test is natural: \( \frac{(k+1)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!} \cdot \frac{k!}{k^k} = \frac{(k+1)(k+1)^k}{(k+1)k^k} = (1+1/k)^k \to e \). Since e ≈ 2.718 > 1, the series diverges. Note that the Root Test would give \( \lim \frac{k}{\sqrt[k]{k!}} = e \) (using \( \sqrt[k]{k!} \sim k/e \)), also showing divergence, but requires knowing the asymptotic of \( \sqrt[k]{k!} \). The Ratio Test uses only elementary limits. This reinforces that \( k^k \) grows much faster than \( k! \), contrary to some intuitions.

Q25. A graph shows partial sums \( S_n \) of a positive series approaching a horizontal asymptote. Another graph shows the ratio \( a_{n+1}/a_n \) approaching 1 from below. Are these consistent?

A. No; if the ratio approaches 1, the series must diverge.
B. Yes; the ratio approaching 1 from below is consistent with slow convergence (e.g., p-series with p>1). ✅
C. No; convergence requires the ratio to approach a value strictly less than 1.
D. Yes; but only if the partial sums approach the asymptote exponentially.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This graph-based/mixed concept question reconciles visual evidence with test limitations. A ratio limit of 1 is inconclusive analytically, but visually, if ratios stay below 1 and partial sums level off, convergence is plausible (e.g., \( \sum 1/n^2 \) has ratio \( \to 1 \) yet converges). The key is 'from below': ratios consistently < 1 suggest decreasing terms, compatible with convergence. Ratios approaching 1 from above would suggest divergence. This nuanced interpretation bridges graphical intuition and the technical inconclusiveness of ρ=1.

Q26. In environmental modeling, pollutant concentration follows \( C_k = \frac{2^k + k!}{3^k + k^k} \). Does \( \sum C_k \) converge?

A. Diverges; numerator dominated by k!, denominator by k^k, and k! > k^k.
B. Converges; numerator dominated by k!, denominator by k^k, and k^k >> k!. ✅
C. Diverges; both numerator and denominator dominated by exponentials with base > 1.
D. Converges; Ratio Test yields limit 2/3 < 1.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This scenario-based question requires identifying dominant terms in complex expressions. Numerator: \( k! \) dominates \( 2^k \). Denominator: \( k^k \) dominates \( 3^k \). So \( C_k \sim k!/k^k \). From previous knowledge, \( k!/k^k \to 0 \) very rapidly (ratio → 1/e < 1). Thus the series converges. Students must parse competing growth rates: \( k^k >> k! >> c^k >> k^p \). Misidentifying dominance (e.g., thinking 3^k dominates k^k) leads to wrong conclusions. This tests hierarchical understanding of asymptotic growth in applied contexts.

Q27. A student uses Limit Comparison on \( \sum \frac{\arctan k}{k^2} \) with \( b_k = 1/k^2 \). They get \( \lim \arctan k = \pi/2 \). They conclude convergence. Is this valid?

A. Invalid; arctan is transcendental, so LCT doesn't apply.
B. Valid; π/2 is a positive finite constant, and \( \sum 1/k^2 \) converges. ✅
C. Invalid; the limit must be exactly 1 for LCT to work.
D. Valid; but only because arctan k < π/2 for all k.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question confirms proper LCT usage with transcendental functions. The limit \( \pi/2 \) is finite and positive, satisfying LCT conditions perfectly. The nature of the function (transcendental vs algebraic) is irrelevant; only the asymptotic ratio matters. Since \( \sum 1/k^2 \) converges, so does the original series. Distractors exploit fears about non-algebraic terms or over-specific requirements (limit=1). LCT is robust precisely because it accommodates any positive finite scaling factor.

Q28. For \( \sum \frac{(n!)^3}{(3n)!} \), the Ratio Test gives limit 1/27. A student claims this means the sum equals 1/27. Explain the error.

A. The limit 1/27 refers to the ratio of consecutive terms, not the series sum. The sum must be found by other means. ✅
B. The student confused the Ratio Test with the Geometric Series formula; the sum is actually a/(1-r).
C. The calculation is wrong; the limit should be 1/9, and the sum is 1/8.
D. There is no error; for factorial series, the ratio limit equals the sum.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis distinguishes between test statistics and series values. The Ratio Test limit ρ indicates convergence behavior, never the actual sum (except coincidentally in geometric series). Students sometimes conflate ρ with S, especially after studying geometric series where r appears in both the test and sum formula. For non-geometric series, finding the sum requires telescoping, known expansions, or advanced techniques. This question reinforces that convergence tests answer 'does it converge?' not 'what does it converge to?'.

Q29. Which series demonstrates that the Root Test can succeed where the Ratio Test fails due to non-existent limits?

A. \( \sum \frac{1}{n^2} \)
B. \( \sum \frac{2 + (-1)^n}{3^n} \) ✅
C. \( \sum \frac{n!}{n^n} \)
D. \( \sum \frac{1}{n \ln n} \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This mixed concept question provides a concrete counterexample to Ratio Test universality. In option B, terms alternate between \( 3/3^n \) and \( 1/3^n \). The ratio alternates between ~3 and ~1/3, so \( \lim a_{n+1}/a_n \) DNE. However, \( \sqrt[n]{a_n} \) alternates between \( \sqrt[n]{3}/3 \) and \( 1/3 \), both approaching 1/3. Since 1/3 < 1, Root Test confirms convergence. This exemplifies the Root Test's superiority via limsup for oscillatory sequences, a subtle but important theoretical point.

Q30. A student analyzes \( \sum \frac{k^2 2^k}{k!} \). They correctly apply Ratio Test getting limit 0. They then state 'Since 0 < 1, the series converges conditionally.' Critique.

A. Correct application and conclusion.
B. Incorrect classification; limit < 1 in Ratio Test implies absolute convergence, not conditional. ✅
C. Incorrect calculation; the limit should be 2, not 0.
D. Incorrect test choice; should have used Root Test for factorials.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question tests precise terminology. When Ratio or Root Test yields ρ < 1, the series converges *absolutely*. Conditional convergence requires \( \sum |a_k| \) to diverge while \( \sum a_k \) converges, which cannot happen when ρ < 1 (since ρ < 1 proves \( \sum |a_k| \) converges). The student's convergence conclusion is right, but the classification is wrong. Precision in distinguishing absolute vs conditional convergence is essential for understanding rearrangement properties and deeper analysis.

Q31. Consider \( \sum a_k \) where \( a_k > 0 \). If \( \sum a_k \) converges, which must be true?

A. \( \lim a_{k+1}/a_k < 1 \)
B. \( \lim \sqrt[k]{a_k} < 1 \)
C. \( \lim a_k = 0 \) ✅
D. \( a_k \leq 1/k^2 \) for all k

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This direct recall question reinforces necessary conditions. Only \( a_k \to 0 \) is guaranteed for any convergent series. The ratio and root limits could equal 1 (e.g., \( \sum 1/k^2 \)). The inequality \( a_k \leq 1/k^2 \) isn't necessary (e.g., \( \sum 1/(k^2 + \sin k) \) might occasionally exceed \( 1/k^2 \)). This fundamental fact underpins the Divergence Test and serves as a first sanity check before applying sophisticated convergence tests.

Q32. In financial mathematics, a perpetuity with growing payments has present value \( \sum P_0 (1+g)^k / (1+r)^k \). Using series tests, under what condition does this converge?

A. Always, since it's a financial instrument.
B. Only if g < r, by Geometric Series test with ratio (1+g)/(1+r). ✅
C. Only if g ≤ r, by Ratio Test.
D. Only if r > 0, regardless of g.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This scenario-based question applies geometric series to finance. The series is geometric with ratio \( \rho = (1+g)/(1+r) \). Convergence requires \( |\rho| < 1 \), i.e., \( 1+g < 1+r \implies g < r \). If g ≥ r, the PV is infinite (model breaks down). This connects abstract convergence criteria to real-world constraints: growth rate must be less than discount rate. Students see that mathematical divergence corresponds to economic impossibility, reinforcing the practical meaning of convergence tests.

Q33. A student compares \( \sum \frac{1}{k \ln k} \) to \( \sum \frac{1}{k} \) using LCT. They get \( \lim \frac{1/(k \ln k)}{1/k} = \lim \frac{1}{\ln k} = 0 \). They conclude 'inconclusive'. Is this correct?

A. Yes; LCT requires a positive finite limit.
B. Partially; LCT standard form is inconclusive, but since limit=0 and \( \sum 1/k \) diverges, no conclusion follows about the smaller series. Need Integral Test. ✅
C. No; limit=0 with divergent comparator proves the original series converges.
D. No; the limit calculation is wrong; it should be 1.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This nuanced question addresses LCT edge cases. When \( \lim a_k/b_k = 0 \) and \( \sum b_k \) diverges, we learn nothing (smaller series could converge or diverge). The student correctly identified inconclusiveness but missed the strategic implication: switch to Integral Test. Option B captures this perfectly. Many students memorize 'LCT needs positive finite limit' without understanding the directional information available at 0 or ∞. This promotes flexible problem-solving beyond rote test application.

Q34. For \( \sum \frac{x^k}{k^2} \), the Ratio Test gives R=1. At x=-1, the series becomes \( \sum (-1)^k/k^2 \). What is the convergence status at this endpoint?

A. Diverges by Divergence Test
B. Converges absolutely by p-series comparison ✅
C. Converges conditionally by Alternating Series Test
D. Inconclusive without further testing

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This application question handles endpoint analysis. At x=-1, we get \( \sum (-1)^k/k^2 \). Taking absolute values gives \( \sum 1/k^2 \), a convergent p-series. Thus, the series converges *absolutely* at x=-1, not just conditionally. Students often reflexively apply AST to alternating series without checking absolute convergence first. Absolute convergence is stronger and preferable. This question reinforces checking absolute convergence before settling for conditional convergence at endpoints.

Q35. A computational algorithm estimates \( \sum a_k \) by computing successive ratios. It outputs ratios: 0.9, 0.95, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995,... approaching 1. The programmer concludes convergence. Why is this dangerous?

A. Computers cannot handle infinite precision.
B. Approaching 1 from below suggests possible convergence, but doesn't guarantee it; \( \sum 1/k \) has ratios → 1 and diverges. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis connects numerical computation to theoretical caution. Numerically observing ratios approaching 1 is ambiguous: \( \sum 1/k^2 \) (convergent) and \( \sum 1/k \) (divergent) both have ratios → 1. Without knowing the *rate* of approach or having analytical proof, numerical evidence is unreliable near the boundary. This highlights why analytical tests are indispensable: computation can suggest but not prove convergence when ρ≈1. It warns against over-reliance on empirical patterns in series analysis.

Q36. Which modification to \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2} \) would make the Ratio Test inconclusive while preserving convergence?

A. Multiply terms by 2
B. Add \( (-1)^k/k^3 \) to each term
C. Replace \( k^2 \) with \( k(k+1) \) ✅
D. Change exponent to 1

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This Olympiad-style question probes test sensitivity. Option C gives \( \sum 1/(k(k+1)) \), which telescopes and converges. But ratio \( \frac{k(k+1)}{(k+1)(k+2)} = \frac{k}{k+2} \to 1 \), making Ratio Test inconclusive. Original \( \sum 1/k^2 \) also has ratio → 1, but the question asks for a modification that *preserves* convergence while being inconclusive—both are, but C introduces a structurally different convergent series where Ratio Test fails equally. Actually, all p-series have ratio→1. The key insight is that Ratio Test is inherently weak for polynomial-decay series; it only shines for exponential/factorial decay. This question deepens understanding of test domains.

Q37. A student analyzes \( \sum \frac{k!}{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2k+1)} \). They struggle with the denominator. How should they proceed with Ratio Test?

A. Recognize denominator as double factorial; ratio simplifies to \( \frac{k+1}{2k+3} \to 1/2 < 1 \), so converges. ✅
B. Use Root Test instead since products are hard.
C. Compare to geometric series with r=1/2.
D. The series diverges because factorial grows faster than any product.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This application question tests handling of non-standard products. The denominator is the product of odd integers. Writing \( a_{k+1}/a_k \), the denominator ratio is \( \frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2k+1)}{1\cdot3\cdots(2k+1)(2k+3)} = \frac{1}{2k+3} \). Combined with numerator \( (k+1)!/k! = k+1 \), the full ratio is \( \frac{k+1}{2k+3} \to 1/2 \). Since 1/2 < 1, convergence follows. This demonstrates that unfamiliar products often telescope nicely in ratios, rewarding algebraic persistence over test-switching.

Q38. True or False: If \( \sum |a_k| \) diverges, then \( \sum a_k \) must diverge.

B. False; the series may still converge conditionally (e.g., alternating harmonic series). ✅
C. True; unless the terms are all positive.
D. False; the Ratio Test would have detected this.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This direct recall question tests the definition of conditional convergence. Absolute divergence does NOT imply divergence. The alternating harmonic series \( \sum (-1)^k/k \) is the canonical counterexample: \( \sum 1/k \) diverges, but the alternating version converges. This distinction is foundational. Students must internalize that absolute convergence is sufficient but not necessary for convergence. Conditional convergence occupies the space between absolute convergence and outright divergence.

The Ratio Test correctly identifies divergence, but physicists still use these series. This reveals that mathematical convergence isn't always required for physical usefulness. Students learn that series tests describe analytic behavior, but applied contexts may leverage divergent series pragmatically. This broadens perspective beyond pure math.

Q41. When comparing \( \sum \frac{\ln k}{k^3} \) to \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2} \) via LCT, the limit is \( \lim \frac{\ln k}{k} = 0 \). What does this imply?

Students often think limit=0 means 'inconclusive', but when the comparator converges, 0 is conclusive for convergence. This directional understanding of LCT avoids unnecessary escalation to Integral Test when comparison suffices.
