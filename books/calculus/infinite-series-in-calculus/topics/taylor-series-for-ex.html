Q1. When approximating \( e^{-5} \) using the Maclaurin series for \( e^x \), a student observes significant loss of precision compared to approximating \( e^{5} \). Which analysis best explains this phenomenon in the context of numerical stability?

🔹 A. The series for \( e^{-5} \) diverges because the terms grow without bound.
🔹 B. The alternating signs in the series for \( e^{-5} \) cause catastrophic cancellation when summing large terms of opposite sign.
🔹 C. The remainder estimation theorem provides a larger error bound for negative exponents than positive ones.
🔹 D. The Maclaurin series is only valid for \( x > 0 \), requiring a Taylor shift for negative inputs.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Maclaurin series for \( e^{-5} \) is an alternating series with terms that initially increase in magnitude before decreasing. Summing large positive and negative numbers leads to catastrophic cancellation, where significant digits are lost due to finite machine precision. In contrast, \( e^5 \) involves only positive terms, avoiding this subtraction error. This highlights that mathematical convergence does not guarantee numerical stability, necessitating alternative strategies like computing \( 1/e^5 \) for negative arguments.

Q2. A computational model requires evaluating \( e^x \) for very large \( x \). If one uses the standard partial sum \( S_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{x^k}{k!} \), which statement correctly identifies the primary limitation as \( x \to \infty \)?

🔹 A. The radius of convergence decreases as \( x \) increases, eventually becoming zero.
🔹 B. The number of terms required for fixed relative accuracy grows linearly with \( x \), but intermediate terms overflow floating-point limits long before convergence.
🔹 C. The series converges conditionally rather than absolutely for large \( x \), making term ordering critical.
🔹 D. The Lagrange error bound becomes undefined because \( e^x \) is unbounded on \( [0, \infty) \).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While the exponential series converges for all real \( x \), practical computation faces overflow issues. For large \( x \), the terms \( x^k/k! \) grow enormously before eventually decaying. Even if the final sum is representable, intermediate terms may exceed floating-point maximums. Furthermore, achieving a fixed relative error requires roughly \( O(x) \) terms, making direct summation computationally prohibitive. This necessitates range reduction techniques or asymptotic expansions rather than naive series evaluation for large arguments.

Padé approximants often outperform Taylor polynomials of comparable degree because they incorporate information about the function's global structure through rational approximation. While \( P_4 \) matches derivatives up to order 4, \( R_{2,2} \) effectively captures asymptotic behavior and curvature that polynomials miss. For \( e^x \), which has no poles and specific growth characteristics, the rational form reduces truncation error significantly. This illustrates that matching local derivatives is necessary but not sufficient for optimal approximation; functional form matters.

Q4. In deriving the remainder bound for \( e^x \) on \( [-1, 0] \), why is it mathematically valid and strategically superior to use \( M=1 \) instead of \( M=e^x \) or \( M=e^{-1} \)?

🔹 A. Because \( e^x \) is decreasing on this interval, its maximum absolute derivative value occurs at \( x=-1 \), so \( M=e^{-1} \) is actually required.
🔹 B. Because \( |f^{(n+1)}(c)| = e^c \leq e^0 = 1 \) for all \( c \in [-1,0] \), providing a tighter uniform bound than using endpoint values incorrectly.
🔹 C. Because the remainder estimation theorem only applies when \( M \) is an integer.
🔹 D. Because \( e^x \) is negative on this interval, so absolute values must be taken, yielding \( M=1 \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

On \( [-1, 0] \), \( f^{(n+1)}(x) = e^x \) is strictly increasing, so its maximum occurs at the right endpoint \( x=0 \), giving \( M=e^0=1 \). Using \( M=e^{-1} \) would be incorrect as it underestimates the derivative’s maximum. The strategic advantage of \( M=1 \) is that it yields a clean, computable bound \( |R_n| \leq \frac{1}{(n+1)!} \) independent of unknown constants. This demonstrates careful analysis of monotonicity in error bounding, avoiding both overestimation and invalid underestimation.

Q5. A student claims that since \( \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!} = 0 \) for all \( x \), the Maclaurin series for \( e^x \) converges uniformly to \( e^x \) on \( (-\infty, \infty) \). What is the flaw in this reasoning?

🔹 A. Pointwise convergence of terms to zero implies uniform convergence of the series only on compact sets, not the entire real line.
🔹 B. The limit being zero only guarantees divergence, not convergence.
🔹 C. Uniform convergence requires the ratio test limit to be less than 1 everywhere, which fails at infinity.
🔹 D. The statement is actually correct; the exponential series is uniformly convergent on all reals.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

While the series converges pointwise everywhere and uniformly on any bounded interval \( [-R, R] \), it does NOT converge uniformly on \( (-\infty, \infty) \). Uniform convergence on an unbounded domain would require \( \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}} |e^x - S_n(x)| \to 0 \), but \( e^x \) grows faster than any polynomial, so the tail remains arbitrarily large for sufficiently negative partial sums evaluated at large positive \( x \). Confusing pointwise term decay with uniform series convergence is a subtle but critical distinction in analysis.

Graphs visually confirm this asymmetry, illustrating that infinite radius of convergence doesn't imply uniform approximation quality.

Q8. To compute \( e^{10} \) accurately using series, one might use \( e^{10} = (e^5)^2 \) or \( e^{10} = (e^{0.1})^{100} \). Which strategy minimizes cumulative rounding error and why?

🔹 A. \( (e^5)^2 \) because fewer operations reduce error propagation despite larger intermediate values.
🔹 B. \( (e^{0.1})^{100} \) because each series evaluation is highly accurate, and exponentiation amplifies accuracy.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

Computing \( e^{0.1} \) via series is extremely accurate with few terms, but raising it to the 100th power compounds relative errors multiplicatively: if \( \tilde{a} = a(1+\delta) \), then \( \tilde{a}^{100} \approx a^{100}(1+100\delta) \). Conversely, computing \( e^5 \) requires more terms but only one squaring operation, limiting error amplification. Despite \( e^5 \) being larger, the single squaring step keeps error manageable. This trade-off between per-operation accuracy and error propagation depth is central to numerical algorithm design for special functions.

Q9. A student attempts to bound the error in approximating \( e^{0.5} \) using \( P_3(0.5) \) and writes \( |R_3| \leq \frac{e^{0.5} (0.5)^4}{4!} \). Why is this bound problematic for practical computation?

🔹 B. The factorial should be \( 3! \) not \( 4! \).

🔹 D. The bound is actually correct and practically useful.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Lagrange remainder formula requires an upper bound \( M \) on \( |f^{(4)}(c)| \) for \( c \) between 0 and 0.5. Since \( f^{(4)}(x)=e^x \) is increasing, \( M=e^{0.5} \). But if we don’t know \( e^{0.5} \) exactly, we cannot evaluate this bound numerically. Practitioners replace \( e^{0.5} \) with a known upper estimate like \( e^{0.5} < 2 \) or use iterative refinement. Recognizing this circularity is essential for turning theoretical error bounds into actionable computational guarantees.

Q11. Suppose you need to approximate \( e^x \) on \( [0,1] \) with maximum error \( < 10^{-6} \). Comparing Maclaurin polynomials vs. Chebyshev economization, which statement is true regarding efficiency?

🔹 A. Maclaurin is always optimal because it matches derivatives at 0.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Maclaurin polynomials minimize error near \( x=0 \) but allow larger errors near \( x=1 \). This reflects the fundamental difference between local (Taylor) and global (minimax) approximation paradigms. Q12. In a physics simulation, \( e^{-t/\tau} \) is approximated by \( 1 - t/\tau \) for \( t \ll \tau \). If the simulation timestep \( \Delta t \) satisfies \( \Delta t / \tau = 0.1 \), what is the leading-order error introduced per step, and how does it accumulate over \( N \) steps?

🔹 A. Error per step is \( O((\Delta t/\tau)^2) \); total error after \( N \) steps is \( O(N (\Delta t/\tau)^2) \).
🔹 B. Error per step is \( O(\Delta t/\tau) \); total error is \( O(1) \).
🔹 C. Error per step is \( O((\Delta t/\tau)^3) \); total error is negligible.
🔹 D. Error per step is \( O((\Delta t/\tau)^2) \); total error is \( O(\sqrt{N} (\Delta t/\tau)^2) \) due to random walk.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Taylor expansion shows \( e^{-x} = 1 - x + x^2/2 - \cdots \), so truncating after linear term gives local error \( \approx x^2/2 \). With \( x = \Delta t / \tau = 0.1 \), per-step error is \( O(0.01) \). Over \( N \) steps covering fixed time \( T = N \Delta t \), total error accumulates as \( N \cdot O((\Delta t)^2) = O(T \Delta t) \), which is first-order globally. This linear accumulation (not square-root) arises because truncation error is systematic, not stochastic. Recognizing error order is vital for choosing appropriate timesteps in simulations.

Awareness of such limitations prevents misinterpretation of truncated series in contexts where sign preservation is physically meaningful, like probability densities.

Q15. When approximating \( e^x \) for \( x \in [-1,1] \), which error metric is minimized by the Maclaurin polynomial \( P_n(x) \) compared to other degree-\( n \) polynomials?

🔹 A. Maximum absolute error on \( [-1,1] \)
🔹 B. Mean squared error on \( [-1,1] \)
🔹 C. Error and first \( n \) derivatives at \( x=0 \)
🔹 D. Relative error at endpoints

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

By definition, the Maclaurin polynomial \( P_n(x) \) is constructed so that \( P_n^{(k)}(0) = f^{(k)}(0) \) for \( k=0,1,\dots,n \). It does not minimize max error (Chebyshev does) or MSE (Legendre projection does). This local optimality at zero comes at the cost of potentially poor performance away from zero. Understanding what Taylor polynomials actually optimize clarifies when they are appropriate versus when global approximation methods are needed. This foundational concept underpins all subsequent discussions of series approximation quality.

This reinforces that multiple representations exist for different purposes: conceptual understanding vs. numerical efficiency.

Students often overlook this subtlety, assuming pointwise convergence suffices for continuity. Highlighting this gap strengthens understanding of why stronger convergence notions are necessary for preserving analytical properties under limits.

Q22. In proving \( e^x \) equals its Maclaurin series, one shows \( R_n(x) \to 0 \). Why is it insufficient to merely note that \( \frac{x^n}{n!} \to 0 \)?

Proving \( f(x) = \sum f^{(k)}(0)x^k/k! \) requires showing the specific remainder \( R_n(x) = f(x)-S_n(x) \to 0 \), not just that terms vanish. Term decay is necessary but not sufficient for representation. This distinction separates formal power series from actual function representation, a cornerstone of real analysis often glossed over in computational contexts.

Q23. When approximating \( e^{A} \) for a matrix \( A \), why can't we directly apply scalar Maclaurin series convergence criteria?

This highlights that extending scalar concepts to operators demands careful re-examination of underlying assumptions. Matrix exponentials are fundamental in differential equations and quantum mechanics, making this distinction critically important beyond pure mathematics.

Q25. Which property of \( e^x \) makes its Maclaurin series particularly well-suited for symbolic integration compared to numerical quadrature?

📖 Explanation:

Integrating \( \sum x^k/k! \) term-by-term yields \( \sum x^{k+1}/((k+1)k!) + C \), which is straightforward symbolically. While \( e^x \)'s self-differentiation helps, the key advantage for series is operational simplicity: integration reduces to index shifting. Numerical quadrature works fine for exponentials, and symbolic methods don't always prefer series. But when dealing with compositions like \( e^{x^2} \) lacking elementary antiderivatives, series provide tractable symbolic expressions where closed forms fail. This showcases series as bridges between computable and non-integrable functions.

Q27. Why does the Maclaurin series for \( e^x \) converge faster for \( x=0.1 \) than for \( x=1 \), even though both are within radius of convergence?

🔹 A. The ratio of successive terms \( |a_{k+1}/a_k| = |x|/(k+1) \) is smaller for \( x=0.1 \), causing faster decay after initial terms.

🔹 D. Both converge at identical rates; perceived difference is illusory.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

Q29. A student derives the Maclaurin series for \( e^{2x} \) by substituting \( 2x \) into \( e^u \)'s series. Another computes derivatives of \( e^{2x} \) directly. Do these yield identical series, and why?

🔹 A. Yes, because substitution preserves analyticity and Taylor coefficients are unique.
🔹 B. No, because chain rule introduces extra factors.
🔹 C. Only if \( |2x| < 1 \).
🔹 D. They differ by a constant factor.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Analytic functions have unique power series representations within their radius of convergence. Substitution \( u=2x \) into \( \sum u^k/k! \) gives \( \sum (2x)^k/k! = \sum 2^k x^k/k! \). Direct differentiation yields \( f^{(k)}(0) = 2^k e^0 = 2^k \), so series is \( \sum 2^k x^k/k! \). Identical results confirm consistency of methods. Uniqueness theorem guarantees this equivalence for all analytic functions. This reinforces that valid manipulations of convergent series preserve the represented function, building confidence in algebraic techniques for generating new series from known ones.

Q31. Which statement correctly contrasts the Maclaurin series for \( e^x \) and \( \ln(1+x) \) regarding approximation utility near zero?

Q32. When using Maclaurin series to solve \( y' = y, y(0)=1 \) via power series method, why is the resulting series guaranteed to be \( e^x \)?

🔹 A. The recurrence relation for coefficients uniquely determines \( a_k = 1/k! \), matching \( e^x \)'s series.
🔹 B. Any solution to \( y'=y \) must be exponential.

🔹 D. Initial condition \( y(0)=1 \) forces the series to be exponential.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Assuming \( y=\sum a_k x^k \), substitution gives \( \sum k a_k x^{k-1} = \sum a_k x^k \), yielding recurrence \( a_{k} = a_{k-1}/k \). With \( a_0=1 \), this uniquely gives \( a_k=1/k! \). Since \( e^x \) satisfies the ODE and has this series, uniqueness of ODE solutions plus uniqueness of power series coefficients guarantees identity. This connects differential equations, series, and function theory elegantly. It shows how structural constraints (ODE + IC) fully determine the series, reinforcing deep links between analytical objects.

Q39. When modeling population growth \( P(t) = P_0 e^{rt} \) with uncertain \( r \), why might series approximation be preferable to closed-form \( e^{rt} \) in sensitivity analysis?

🔹 A. Series expresses \( P(t) \) as polynomial in \( r \), enabling direct differentiation w.r.t. \( r \) for sensitivity coefficients without chain rule complications.
🔹 B. Closed-form exponentials cannot be differentiated.
🔹 C. Series converge faster for uncertain parameters.
🔹 D. Sensitivity analysis requires discrete models.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

But series form allows term-wise sensitivity decomposition, revealing which orders dominate uncertainty propagation. For perturbation methods or polynomial chaos expansions, series enable analytical sensitivity structures inaccessible via black-box exponentials. This shows series as tools for structural insight beyond mere computation.
