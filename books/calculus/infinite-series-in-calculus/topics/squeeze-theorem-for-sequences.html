Q1. A student attempts to prove that the sequence \( a_n = \frac{\sin(n)}{n} \) converges to 0 by stating that since \( -1 \leq \sin(n) \leq 1 \), then \( -1 \leq a_n \leq 1 \). As \( n \to \infty \), the bounds are constant, so the limit must exist. What is the fundamental flaw in this application of the Squeezing Theorem?

🔹 A. The student failed to divide the inequality by \( n \) correctly.
🔹 B. The outer sequences do not converge to the same finite limit; they remain constant at -1 and 1.
🔹 C. The function \( \sin(n) \) is not continuous for integer values of \( n \).
🔹 D. The Squeezing Theorem only applies to monotonic sequences, which \( \sin(n)/n \) is not.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Squeezing Theorem requires that both the lower bounding sequence and the upper bounding sequence converge to the exact same limit \( L \). In this student's reasoning, the bounds are constants -1 and 1. Since \( \lim_{n \to \infty} (-1) = -1 \) and \( \lim_{n \to \infty} (1) = 1 \), and \( -1 \neq 1 \), the theorem cannot be applied to deduce the limit of the middle sequence. The correct bounds would be \( -1/n \) and \( 1/n \), which both converge to 0.

Q2. Consider the sequence defined by \( b_n = \frac{n!}{n^n} \). Without using Stirling's approximation or L'Hôpital's Rule, which of the following represents the most effective pair of bounding sequences to apply the Squeezing Theorem to determine the limit?

🔹 A. \( 0 \leq b_n \leq \frac{1}{n} \)
🔹 B. \( \frac{1}{n^n} \leq b_n \leq \frac{n}{n^n} \)
🔹 C. \( 0 \leq b_n \leq \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \)
🔹 D. \( \frac{n}{e^n} \leq b_n \leq \frac{n^2}{e^n} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

To squeeze \( n!/n^n \), we expand it as \( \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{2}{n} \cdots \frac{n}{n} \). Every factor except the first is less than or equal to 1. Thus, the entire product is bounded above by \( \frac{1}{n} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdots = \frac{1}{n} \). Since \( b_n > 0 \) for all \( n \), we have \( 0 \leq b_n \leq \frac{1}{n} \). As \( n \to \infty \), both 0 and \( 1/n \) approach 0, confirming the limit is 0 via the Squeezing Theorem. This avoids complex calculus tools.

Q3. Given three sequences where \( x_n \leq y_n \leq z_n \) for all \( n \geq 100 \). If \( \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = 5 \) and \( \lim_{n \to \infty} z_n = 5 \), but \( y_n \) is undefined for \( n < 100 \), what can be concluded about \( \lim_{n \to \infty} y_n \)?

🔹 A. The limit does not exist because the sequence is undefined for initial terms.
🔹 B. The limit is 5, because convergence depends only on eventual behavior.
🔹 C. The limit exists but cannot be determined without knowing the values for \( n < 100 \).
🔹 D. The Squeezing Theorem is invalid here because the inequality does not hold for all positive integers.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The definition of the limit of a sequence concerns the behavior as \( n \to \infty \). Finite initial terms, or even a finite number of undefined terms, do not affect the existence or value of the limit. The Squeezing Theorem explicitly allows for inequalities to hold 'eventually' (i.e., for all \( n \) beyond some index \( N \)). Since the bounding sequences both converge to 5 for large \( n \), the middle sequence must also converge to 5 regardless of its early behavior.

If \( f(x) \to L \) and \( g(x) \to L \) as \( x \to \infty \), but \( a_n \) is only defined at integer points, why is it valid to use continuous functions \( f \) and \( g \) as bounds?

🔹 A. It is never valid; bounds must be discrete sequences.

The Squeezing Theorem applies to sequences based on their values at integer indices. Even if the bounding functions \( f(x) \) and \( g(x) \) are continuous, we only evaluate them at integer points \( n \) to create bounding sequences \( f(n) \) and \( g(n) \). If these evaluated sequences satisfy the inequality and share a common limit \( L \), the theorem guarantees \( a_n \to L \). The continuous nature of the envelope functions helps visualize the 'squeezing' effect but the rigorous justification relies on the discrete evaluation at integers.

Q5. Which of the following scenarios best illustrates a case where the Squeezing Theorem is necessary because standard limit laws fail?

🔹 A. Finding the limit of \( \frac{3n^2 + 1}{2n^2 - 5} \) by dividing numerator and denominator by \( n^2 \).
🔹 B. Evaluating \( \lim_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{n} \) by taking logarithms and using L'Hôpital's Rule.

🔹 D. Calculating the sum of a geometric series with ratio \( r = 1/2 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Standard algebraic limit laws require the individual limits to exist. For \( \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}} \), the term \( (-1)^n \) has no limit, making the quotient law inapplicable directly. While one could use absolute convergence, the Squeezing Theorem provides a direct path: \( -\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \leq a_n \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \). Both bounds go to 0, forcing the oscillating sequence to 0. This highlights the theorem's utility for handling bounded oscillation combined with decay, where pure algebra fails.

Q6. A student claims that if \( a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n \) and \( \lim a_n = \lim c_n = L \), then \( b_n \) must eventually become constant and equal to \( L \). Is this claim true or false, and why?

🔹 A. True; once squeezed, the sequence has no room to vary.
🔹 B. False; the sequence must approach \( L \) but can continue to oscillate or vary indefinitely while staying within shrinking bounds.
🔹 C. True; the definition of convergence implies equality after some point.
🔹 D. False; the Squeezing Theorem only guarantees that \( b_n \) is bounded, not that it converges.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Convergence to a limit \( L \) means the terms get arbitrarily close to \( L \), not that they become identical to \( L \). A classic counterexample is \( b_n = L + \frac{(-1)^n}{n} \). This sequence is squeezed between \( L - \frac{1}{n} \) and \( L + \frac{1}{n} \), both converging to \( L \). However, \( b_n \) never equals \( L \) and continues to oscillate forever. The theorem constrains the range of values, ensuring proximity to the limit, but does not enforce constancy or monotonicity.

Q7. Suppose you are modeling the error \( E_n \) of a numerical algorithm and establish that \( |E_n| \leq \frac{K}{n^p} \) for some constant \( K > 0 \). For which values of \( p \) can you definitively conclude via the Squeezing Theorem that the error vanishes as \( n \to \infty \)?

🔹 A. Only when \( p = 1 \).
🔹 B. Only when \( p \) is an integer.
🔹 C. For any real number \( p > 0 \).
🔹 D. For any real number \( p \geq 0 \).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

We can set up the inequality \( 0 \leq |E_n| \leq \frac{K}{n^p} \). The lower bound is constantly 0. The upper bound \( K n^{-p} \) approaches 0 as \( n \to \infty \) if and only if the exponent \( p \) is strictly positive. If \( p = 0 \), the bound is constant \( K \), providing no squeezing to 0. If \( p < 0 \), the bound diverges. Therefore, for any \( p > 0 \), the Squeezing Theorem guarantees \( |E_n| \to 0 \), implying the algorithm's error vanishes asymptotically.

Q8. Identify the incorrect step in the following proof attempting to show \( \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n + \cos(n)}{n} = 1 \): Step 1: \( -1 \leq \cos(n) \leq 1 \). Step 2: \( n-1 \leq n+\cos(n) \leq n+1 \). Step 3: Divide by \( n \): \( 1 - \frac{1}{n} \leq a_n \leq 1 + \frac{1}{n} \). Step 4: Since limits of bounds are 1, the limit is 1. 🔹 C. Step 4
🔹 D. Step 5

📖 Explanation:

Steps 1 through 4 constitute a perfectly valid application of the Squeezing Theorem to prove that the limit is 1. The error lies exclusively in Step 5. Convergence to a limit does not imply that the sequence becomes equal to that limit. The sequence \( \frac{n + \cos(n)}{n} = 1 + \frac{\cos(n)}{n} \) fluctuates around 1 for every finite \( n \); it is never exactly 1 (except potentially at isolated points). This distractor tests the distinction between asymptotic behavior and exact equality.

Q9. Let \( a_n = \sqrt[n]{3^n + 5^n} \). To apply the Squeezing Theorem effectively, one should recognize the dominant term. Which pair of bounds leads most directly to the limit?

🔹 A. \( 3 \leq a_n \leq 5 \)
🔹 B. \( 5 \leq a_n \leq 5 \cdot \sqrt[n]{2} \)
🔹 C. \( \sqrt[n]{5^n} \leq a_n \leq \sqrt[n]{2 \cdot 5^n} \)
🔹 D. \( 0 \leq a_n \leq 8 \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Directly bounding by constants like 3 and 5 is insufficient as they don't share a limit. Recognizing \( 5^n \) as dominant, we write \( 5^n < 3^n + 5^n < 5^n + 5^n = 2 \cdot 5^n \). Taking the nth root gives \( 5 < a_n < 5 \cdot 2^{1/n} \). As \( n \to \infty \), \( 2^{1/n} \to 1 \), so the upper bound approaches 5. The lower bound is constantly 5. Both converge to 5, squeezing \( a_n \) to 5. This demonstrates identifying asymptotic dominance to construct tight, convergent bounds rather than loose static ones.

Q10. In the context of the Squeezing Theorem, why is the condition 'for all \( n \) beyond some index \( N \)' mathematically significant compared to 'for all \( n \geq 1 \)'?

🔹 A. It allows the theorem to apply to sequences with undefined or erratic initial terms without affecting the limit.
🔹 B. It makes the theorem weaker and less useful for rigorous proofs.
🔹 C. It implies that the limit depends on the value of \( N \).
🔹 D. It is merely a notational convenience with no theoretical impact.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Limits describe long-term behavior. Many meaningful sequences may be undefined at \( n=0 \) (e.g., involving \( 1/n \)) or exhibit irregular behavior initially before settling into a pattern. Requiring the inequality to hold for all \( n \geq 1 \) would unnecessarily exclude such sequences. By allowing the condition to hold eventually (for \( n \geq N \)), the theorem remains robust and applicable to virtually all convergent scenarios, reinforcing that finite prefixes are irrelevant to infinite limits.

The Squeezing Theorem relies solely on the magnitude of the oscillating component being bounded. Whether \( \sin(n) \), \( \sin(n^2) \), or \( \sin(e^n) \) appears, the range is always \( [-1, 1] \). Thus \( -1/n \leq d_n \leq 1/n \) holds regardless of oscillation frequency. 💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

While \( 0 \leq a_n \leq \frac{n+1}{n} \to 1 \) is technically true, it fails to squeeze the sequence to a unique value because the bounds converge to different limits (0 and 1). This problem illustrates that finding valid inequalities is not enough; they must be sharp enough to coincide asymptotically. 🔹 A. Direct substitution of absolute value properties.

This is a specific corollary often derived directly from the Squeezing Theorem. Since \( -|x| \leq x \leq |x| \) for any real number, substituting \( a_n \) gives \( -|a_n| \leq a_n \leq |a_n| \). If \( |a_n| \to 0 \), then \( -|a_n| \to 0 \) as well. Both outer sequences converge to 0, so by the Squeezing Theorem, \( a_n \) must also converge to 0. Q14. You are given \( a_n = \frac{n^2 + 3n}{n^3 - n} \). A peer suggests using \( 0 \leq a_n \leq \frac{2n^2}{n^3/2} \) for \( n \geq 2 \). Is this a valid squeezing argument for showing the limit is 0?

🔹 A. No, because the upper bound simplifies to \( 4/n \) which goes to 0, but the inequality \( a_n \leq \frac{2n^2}{n^3/2} \) is false for small \( n \).
🔹 B. Yes, because for sufficiently large \( n \), the numerator is less than \( 2n^2 \) and denominator greater than \( n^3/2 \), and both bounds go to 0.
🔹 C. No, because the Squeezing Theorem cannot be used for rational functions.
🔹 D. Yes, but only if verified for all \( n \geq 1 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For large \( n \), \( n^2 + 3n < 2n^2 \) and \( n^3 - n > n^3/2 \) are both true. Thus the inequality holds eventually. The upper bound simplifies to \( 4/n \), which approaches 0. The lower bound is 0 (since terms are positive for \( n>1 \)). Since both bounds converge to 0, the squeeze is valid. The key insight is verifying that the simplified bounds are indeed valid overestimates/underestimates asymptotically, not just algebraically similar.

Q15. Which statement correctly distinguishes the Squeezing Theorem for sequences from the Intermediate Value Theorem for continuous functions?

🔹 A. Squeezing deals with limits at infinity using two bounding sequences; IVT deals with attaining intermediate values on a closed interval.
🔹 B. Squeezing requires continuity; IVT does not.
🔹 C. IVT applies to sequences; Squeezing applies only to functions.
🔹 D. They are essentially the same theorem stated differently.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

These theorems address fundamentally different concepts. The Squeezing Theorem (for sequences) establishes convergence to a limit \( L \) by trapping terms between two other sequences converging to \( L \). It says nothing about attaining specific values. The Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees that a continuous function on \( [a,b] \) takes every value between \( f(a) \) and \( f(b) \). Confusing them is a common conceptual error; one is about asymptotic confinement, the other about surjectivity on intervals.

Q16. Suppose \( a_n = \frac{\lfloor n \rfloor}{n} \). Can the Squeezing Theorem be used to find the limit, even though \( \lfloor n \rfloor \) is a step function?

🔹 A. No, floor functions are discontinuous and incompatible with squeezing.
🔹 B. Yes, using \( n-1 < \lfloor n \rfloor \leq n \) leads to bounds converging to 1.
🔹 C. Yes, but only if \( n \) is restricted to integers where \( \lfloor n \rfloor = n \).
🔹 D. No, because the sequence is constant.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Even though the floor function is discontinuous, it satisfies strict algebraic inequalities: \( n-1 < \lfloor n \rfloor \leq n \) for all real \( n \). Dividing by \( n \) (for \( n>0 \)) gives \( 1 - \frac{1}{n} < a_n \leq 1 \). As \( n \to \infty \), \( 1 - 1/n \to 1 \) and the upper bound is constantly 1. Thus, \( a_n \to 1 \). This demonstrates that the Squeezing Theorem works perfectly with discrete/discontinuous components as long as analytic bounds exist.

Q18. In a computational simulation, you observe that a sequence \( x_n \) satisfies \( |x_n - L| \leq \epsilon_n \) where \( \epsilon_n \) is a known error tolerance decreasing to 0. How does this relate to the Squeezing Theorem?

🔹 A. It is unrelated; this is purely numerical analysis.
🔹 B. It is equivalent to squeezing \( x_n \) between \( L - \epsilon_n \) and \( L + \epsilon_n \).
🔹 C. It proves \( x_n \) is monotonic.
🔹 D. It only works if \( \epsilon_n \) decreases geometrically.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Rearranging \( |x_n - L| \leq \epsilon_n \) yields \( -\epsilon_n \leq x_n - L \leq \epsilon_n \), or \( L - \epsilon_n \leq x_n \leq L + \epsilon_n \). Define \( a_n = L - \epsilon_n \) and \( c_n = L + \epsilon_n \). Since \( \epsilon_n \to 0 \), both \( a_n \to L \) and \( c_n \to L \). This is precisely the hypothesis of the Squeezing Theorem. This connection shows that error bounds in numerical methods are practically applications of squeezing, providing theoretical guarantees for computational accuracy.

Q19. Why can't we use the Squeezing Theorem to prove that \( a_n = (-1)^n \) diverges?

🔹 A. Because the sequence is bounded.
🔹 B. Because we cannot find two convergent sequences with the same limit that trap \( (-1)^n \).
🔹 C. Because the Squeezing Theorem only proves convergence, never divergence.
🔹 D. Because \( (-1)^n \) is not defined for non-integers.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Squeezing Theorem is a sufficient condition for convergence, not a tool for proving divergence directly. To apply it, you need bounding sequences sharing a limit. For \( (-1)^n \), any valid lower bound must be \( \leq -1 \) infinitely often and any upper bound \( \geq 1 \) infinitely often. No pair of sequences converging to the same \( L \) can satisfy this. The inability to construct such bounds reflects the sequence's intrinsic divergence, but the theorem itself doesn't output 'diverges'; it simply fails to apply.

Q20. Given \( a_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+2}} + \dots + \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \), which bounding strategy correctly establishes the limit?

🔹 A. Replace each term with the largest term \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}} \) for upper bound and smallest \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \) for lower bound, multiplied by \( n \).
🔹 B. Use integral test bounds immediately.
🔹 C. Assume all terms equal \( \frac{1}{n} \).
🔹 D. Bound by 0 and 1.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

There are \( n \) terms. Each term is between \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \) and \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}} \). Summing gives \( \frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \leq a_n \leq \frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+1}} \). Simplifying: lower bound is \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/n}} \to 1 \); upper bound is \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/n^2}} \to 1 \). Both converge to 1, so \( a_n \to 1 \). This technique of bounding sums by (number of terms) × (min/max term) is a classic and powerful application of squeezing for sequences defined as sums.

Q21. If \( a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n \) and \( \lim a_n = \lim c_n = L \), but \( b_n \) is complex-valued, does the standard Squeezing Theorem apply as stated in real analysis?

🔹 D. Only if \( b_n \) is purely imaginary.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The standard Squeezing Theorem relies on the order property of real numbers (\( \leq \)). Complex numbers do not form an ordered field; you cannot say \( z_1 \leq z_2 \). For complex sequences, one uses the modulus: if \( |b_n - L| \leq \epsilon_n \) with \( \epsilon_n \to 0 \) (real), then \( b_n \to L \). This is effectively squeezing the real-valued distance \( |b_n - L| \) to 0. Recognizing domain restrictions prevents misapplication of real-analysis theorems to complex contexts.

Q24. Which modification to \( a_n = \frac{\sin(n)}{n} \) would make the Squeezing Theorem inapplicable for proving convergence to 0?

🔹 D. Adding constant: \( \frac{\sin(n)}{n} + 1 \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

For \( n \sin(n) / n = \sin(n) \), bounds are -1 and 1, which don't converge to same limit. Squeezing fails. For \( \sin(n^2)/n \) or \( \sin(n)/\sqrt{n} \), bounds still go to 0. Adding constant shifts limit to 1, but squeezing still works with adjusted bounds. Only when the oscillation amplitude doesn't decay (or grows) does squeezing to a point fail. This tests understanding that squeezing requires decaying envelopes.

Q25. In proving \( \lim \sqrt[n]{n} = 1 \), one might use \( 1 \leq \sqrt[n]{n} \leq 1 + \sqrt{\frac{2}{n}} \). Where does the upper bound come from?

🔹 A. Binomial expansion of \( (1 + \delta_n)^n = n \).
🔹 B. Taylor series of \( \ln(n) \).
🔹 C. Geometric mean inequality.
🔹 D. Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Set \( \sqrt[n]{n} = 1 + \delta_n \) with \( \delta_n > 0 \). Then \( n = (1+\delta_n)^n \geq 1 + n\delta_n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\delta_n^2 \) (binomial theorem, keeping quadratic term). Ignoring linear term: \( n > \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\delta_n^2 \implies \delta_n^2 < \frac{2}{n-1} < \frac{2}{n} \) for \( n>2 \). Thus \( \delta_n < \sqrt{2/n} \). This constructs a tight upper bound converging to 1. This derivation showcases advanced algebraic manipulation to create squeezable bounds where simple inspection fails.

Q26. A graph shows \( a_n \) bouncing between \( y = \frac{1}{x} \) and \( y = -\frac{1}{x} \), touching each envelope infinitely often. What can be definitively concluded?

🔹 A. \( a_n \) is alternating.
🔹 B. \( \lim a_n = 0 \).
🔹 C. \( a_n \) is monotonic for \( n > 10 \).
🔹 D. The envelopes are tangent to \( a_n \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Visual contact with decaying envelopes \( \pm 1/x \) at integer points means \( -1/n \leq a_n \leq 1/n \) holds (at least at touchpoints, and presumably between). Since both envelopes → 0, squeezing forces \( a_n \to 0 \). Alternation isn't guaranteed (could touch same side twice). Monotonicity clearly false. Tangency is visual interpretation, but convergence is the rigorous mathematical consequence guaranteed by the depicted bounding behavior.

Q27. Why is \( 0 \leq a_n \leq b_n \) with \( b_n \to 0 \) sufficient, whereas \( a_n \leq b_n \) with \( b_n \to 0 \) alone is insufficient?

🔹 A. Because \( a_n \) could diverge to \( -\infty \).
🔹 B. Because \( a_n \) must be positive.
🔹 C. Because the Squeezing Theorem requires symmetry.
🔹 D. Because limits must be positive.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Without a lower bound, \( a_n \) could be \( -n \), \( -n^2 \), etc., while still satisfying \( a_n \leq b_n \to 0 \). The upper bound controls from above but says nothing about downward behavior. Squeezing requires confinement from BOTH sides to prevent escape in either direction. The non-negativity condition \( 0 \leq a_n \) serves as the crucial lower bound anchoring the sequence, preventing negative divergence.

Q28. For \( a_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n \sin(k/n) \), why is squeezing with constant bounds 0 and 1 inadequate?

🔹 A. The sum has \( n \) terms, so total could grow.
🔹 B. Bounds don't share a limit.
🔹 C. Sine is negative sometimes.
🔹 D. The expression is actually a Riemann sum converging to \( \int_0^1 \sin x \, dx \), not 0 or 1.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

While \( 0 \leq \sin(k/n) \leq 1 \) implies \( 0 \leq a_n \leq 1 \), these bounds don't coincide. The sequence actually converges to \( 1 - \cos(1) \approx 0.46 \). Squeezing with crude bounds only places it in [0,1]. To find exact limit, recognize Riemann sum structure or use tighter analytical bounds. This illustrates squeezing's limitation: it confirms boundedness/convergence only when bounds are sharp; otherwise, other techniques (integration, asymptotics) are needed for precise values.

Q29. If \( |a_n - 3| \leq \frac{1}{\ln(n)} \) for \( n \geq 2 \), what is the limit and justification?

🔹 A. 3, by Squeezing Theorem since \( 1/\ln(n) \to 0 \).
🔹 B. Does not exist because \( \ln(n) \) grows slowly.
🔹 C. 3, but only by L'Hôpital's Rule.
🔹 D. Cannot determine without sign information.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Rewrite as \( 3 - \frac{1}{\ln n} \leq a_n \leq 3 + \frac{1}{\ln n} \). Since \( \ln n \to \infty \), \( 1/\ln n \to 0 \). Both bounds → 3. Slow growth of log doesn't matter; any unbounded denominator drives fraction to 0. This reinforces that squeezing works with ANY null sequence as envelope, regardless of decay rate, as long as it genuinely approaches 0.

Q30. A student argues: 'I found \( a_n \leq b_n \) and \( b_n \to 5 \), so \( a_n \to 5 \).' What missing premise invalidates this?

🔹 A. \( a_n \) must be increasing.
🔹 B. A matching lower bound converging to 5.
🔹 C. \( b_n \) must converge monotonically.
🔹 D. \( a_n \) must be positive.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Upper bound alone only tells us \( \limsup a_n \leq 5 \). The sequence could converge to 0, -100, or diverge to \( -\infty \). Convergence to specifically 5 requires confinement from below by something also approaching 5. This is the core mechanism of squeezing: bilateral constraint. Unilateral bounds provide only partial information about asymptotic behavior.

🔹 D. Model is invalid.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Mathematically, this is squeezing to \( P^* \). Biologically, it means deviations from equilibrium shrink over time; the system exhibits asymptotic stability toward \( P^* \). Unlike permanent oscillation or extinction, the population settles at a predictable steady state. This connects abstract convergence to tangible ecological resilience, showing how squeezing formalizes 'returning to equilibrium' in dynamic systems.

Q32. Which sequence CANNOT be shown to converge to 0 via Squeezing Theorem using elementary bounds?

🔹 A. \( \frac{\cos(n)}{n^2} \)
🔹 B. \( \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

First three have decaying amplitudes: bounded numerator / growing denominator → 0. Last one oscillates between -1 and 1 forever; no decaying envelope exists. Q33. When applying Squeezing Theorem to \( a_n = \frac{n^2 + (-1)^n n}{n^2 + 1} \), why is splitting into cases (even/odd n) unnecessary?

🔹 A. Because \( (-1)^n \) is bounded by ±1 uniformly.
🔹 B. Because the sequence is monotonic.
🔹 C. Because limits don't depend on parity.
🔹 D. Because denominator dominates regardless of sign.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Although \( (-1)^n \) alternates, it's always in [-1,1]. So \( n^2 - n \leq \text{numerator} \leq n^2 + n \). Dividing by \( n^2+1 \) gives bounds both → 1. Parity-specific analysis is redundant when uniform bounds suffice. This efficiency is squeezing's power: handle oscillation globally via magnitude bounds rather than case-by-case enumeration.

Q34. If \( a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n \) and \( \lim a_n = \lim c_n = L \), but \( b_n \) is undefined at infinitely many points (e.g., \( b_n = \frac{\sin(n\pi)}{n} \) interpreted as 0/0 at integers), what happens?

🔹 A. Theorem fails entirely.
🔹 B. As long as \( b_n \) is defined for all sufficiently large \( n \), limit exists and equals L.
🔹 C. Undefined points make limit nonexistent.
🔹 D. Must redefine \( b_n \) at those points first.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Sequences are functions on integers. If undefined at finitely many points, limit unaffected. If undefined at infinitely many points BUT defined for all \( n \geq N \), still fine. Only if undefined arbitrarily far out (no tail) is limit meaningless. For \( \sin(n\pi)/n \), it's actually 0 at all integers (since sin(nπ)=0), so no issue. But hypothetically, eventual definition suffices. This clarifies domain requirements.
