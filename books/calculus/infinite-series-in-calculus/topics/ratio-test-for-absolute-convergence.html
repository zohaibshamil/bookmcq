What is Ratio test for absolute convergence?

The ratio test can be used for absolute convergence by applying it to \( \sum |a_n| \): if \( \lim \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| < 1 \), then \( \sum a_n \) converges absolutely; if >1, diverges; if =1, it's inconclusive, so the ratio test is actually a test for absolute convergence.

Q1. A student applies the ratio test to the series \( \sum (-1)^k \frac{k!}{k^k} \) and calculates \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = \frac{1}{e} \). They conclude the series converges absolutely. However, another student argues that because the terms involve factorials, the root test would have been more appropriate and might yield a different conclusion regarding absolute convergence. Which analysis is correct?

A. The first student is correct; the limit is \( 1/e < 1 \), proving absolute convergence regardless of the test used. ✅
B. The second student is correct; the root test is required for factorials and would show divergence.
C. Both students are partially correct; the series converges conditionally but not absolutely.
D. The first student made an algebraic error; the limit should be \( e \), implying divergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The ratio test is perfectly valid and often preferred for series involving factorials like \( k! \). The calculated limit \( \rho = 1/e \) is indeed less than 1, which definitively establishes absolute convergence. While the root test can also handle factorials (via Stirling's approximation), it will always yield the same convergence result as the ratio test when both limits exist. The misconception that the root test yields different conclusions for absolute convergence is false; if one test proves absolute convergence via a limit strictly less than 1, the other must agree. Therefore, the first student's application and conclusion are entirely correct.

Q4. A student attempts to determine the convergence of \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!}{(2k)!} x^k \) using the ratio test. 💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question targets a common algebraic pitfall in factorial manipulation during the ratio test. The student correctly identified the setup but erred in simplifying the ratio of double factorials. Q8. You are comparing the efficiency of the Ratio Test versus the Root Test for the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{2k+1}{3k-1} \right)^k \). Which statement best justifies the preference for one test over the other in this specific scenario?

A. The Ratio Test is superior because it avoids nth roots of polynomials.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This mixed-concepts question evaluates strategic test selection. When the general term \( a_k \) involves an expression raised to the \( k \)-th power, i.e., \( (b_k)^k \), the Root Test is typically far more efficient because \( \sqrt[k]{(b_k)^k} = b_k \) (for positive terms), reducing the problem to finding \( \lim b_k \). Applying the Ratio Test here would require expanding \( \left( \frac{2(k+1)+1}{3(k+1)-1} \right)^{k+1} / \left( \frac{2k+1}{3k-1} \right)^k \), leading to messy algebra involving \( (1 + \text{small})^k \) forms that essentially recreate the root test logic indirectly. Thus, recognizing structural cues like \( (\dots)^k \) guides optimal method selection, saving time and reducing error risk. The Root Test directly exploits the term's structure.

Q9. A physics model yields a series \( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2} z^n \). To ensure the model remains physically meaningful, the series must converge absolutely. Using the ratio test, determine the precise boundary of the domain of validity and analyze the behavior at that boundary.

A. The series converges absolutely for \( |z| < 1/4 \); at \( |z|=1/4 \), it diverges. ✅
B. The series converges absolutely for \( |z| < 4 \); at \( |z|=4 \), it converges conditionally.
C. The series converges absolutely for \( |z| < 1/4 \); at \( |z|=1/4 \), it converges absolutely.
D. The series converges absolutely for all \( z \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Applying the ratio test: \( \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| = \frac{(2n+2)!}{((n+1)!)^2} \frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!} |z| = \frac{(2n+2)(2n+1)}{(n+1)^2} |z| \to 4|z| \). Convergence requires \( 4|z| < 1 \Rightarrow |z| < 1/4 \). At the boundary \( z = 1/4 \), the terms become \( \frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2 4^n} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \) by Stirling's approximation. Since \( \sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \) diverges (p-series with p=1/2), the series diverges at the boundary. This multi-step analysis combines ratio test execution, asymptotic analysis of binomial coefficients, and p-series knowledge to fully characterize the domain. The physical implication is that the model breaks down exactly at \( |z|=1/4 \), defining a sharp phase transition or singularity in the system.

Q10. Suppose \( \sum a_k \) is a series of nonzero terms and \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = L \). If we define a new series \( \sum b_k \) where \( b_k = k^p a_k \) for some fixed real number \( p \), how does the ratio test limit for \( \sum b_k \) compare to \( L \)?

A. The new limit is \( L \) if \( p > 0 \) and \( \infty \) if \( p < 0 \).
B. The new limit is always \( L \), regardless of the value of \( p \).
C. The new limit is \( L \cdot \lim_{k \to \infty} (1 + 1/k)^p = L \). ✅
D. The ratio test becomes inconclusive for \( \sum b_k \) whenever \( L = 1 \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This question probes the robustness of the ratio test under polynomial perturbations. Computing the ratio for \( b_k \): \( \left| \frac{b_{k+1}}{b_k} \right| = \left| \frac{(k+1)^p a_{k+1}}{k^p a_k} \right| = \left( \frac{k+1}{k} \right)^p \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = \left(1 + \frac{1}{k}\right)^p \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| \). As \( k \to \infty \), \( (1+1/k)^p \to 1 \) for any finite real \( p \). Thus, the new limit is \( 1 \cdot L = L \). This demonstrates that polynomial factors do not affect the radius of convergence determined by the ratio test; only exponential or factorial factors change the limit. This insight explains why the ratio test captures the 'exponential order' of magnitude of terms, ignoring polynomial corrections. Option C correctly expresses this limit product.

This direct recall/error analysis question addresses the most common misconception about the ratio test. The case \( \rho = 1 \) is the 'indeterminate zone' where the test provides absolutely no information. Both the convergent p-series \( \sum 1/k^2 \) and the divergent harmonic series \( \sum 1/k \) yield \( \rho = 1 \). Similarly, \( \sum (-1)^k/k \) converges conditionally with \( \rho=1 \). Therefore, observing \( \rho=1 \) necessitates switching to a more sensitive test (integral, comparison, etc.). The student's heuristic is dangerous and mathematically unsound. Option B provides the definitive counterexample pair that disproves the universal claim, reinforcing that \( \rho=1 \) is a signal to change methods, not a verdict.

Q12. Consider the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k \binom{2k}{k}} \). Apply the ratio test to find the radius of convergence. Then, explain why the presence of the central binomial coefficient in the denominator significantly alters the radius compared to \( \sum x^k/k \).

A. Radius is 4; the central binomial coefficient grows as \( 4^k/\sqrt{\pi k} \), effectively introducing a \( 1/4^k \) factor that expands the convergence disk. ✅
B. Radius is 1; the binomial coefficient is polynomial and doesn't affect exponential growth.
C. Radius is 1/4; the binomial coefficient accelerates divergence.
D. Radius is 4; the binomial coefficient cancels the k in the denominator.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For \( a_k = \frac{x^k}{k \binom{2k}{k}} \), the ratio is \( \left| \frac{x^{k+1}}{(k+1)\binom{2k+2}{k+1}} \cdot \frac{k \binom{2k}{k}}{x^k} \right| = |x| \frac{k}{k+1} \frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{\binom{2k+2}{k+1}} \). Using \( \binom{2k}{k} \sim \frac{4^k}{\sqrt{\pi k}} \), the ratio of binomials approaches \( 1/4 \). Thus \( \rho = |x|/4 \). Convergence requires \( |x| < 4 \). In contrast, \( \sum x^k/k \) has radius 1. The central binomial coefficient \( \binom{2k}{k} \) grows exponentially as \( 4^k \), acting like a geometric damping factor \( (1/4)^k \) in the denominator. This exponential suppression counteracts the growth of \( x^k \), quadrupling the radius of convergence. This illustrates how combinatorial factors can fundamentally reshape analytic domains, a key insight in generatingfunctionology.

Q13. A student computes the ratio test for \( \sum \frac{(k!)^2}{(2k)!} x^k \) and obtains \( \rho = |x|/4 \). They then assert that at \( x = -4 \), the series converges absolutely because the alternating signs help convergence. Critique this assertion.

A. Correct; alternating series always converge at the boundary if \( \rho=1 \).
B. Incorrect; absolute convergence at \( x=-4 \) depends solely on \( \sum |a_k(-4)^k| \), which is the same as at \( x=4 \). Alternating signs are irrelevant for absolute convergence. ✅
C. Correct; the ratio test for absolute convergence specifically accounts for sign alternation.
D. Incorrect; the series diverges at \( x=-4 \) because the terms do not approach zero.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question disentangles absolute convergence from conditional convergence. Absolute convergence of \( \sum a_k x^k \) at \( x = -4 \) is defined as convergence of \( \sum |a_k (-4)^k| = \sum |a_k| 4^k \). This is identical to checking absolute convergence at \( x = 4 \). The alternating nature of terms at negative \( x \) only matters for *conditional* convergence. If the series of absolute values diverges at the boundary (which it does here, as terms \( \sim 1/\sqrt{k} \)), then it does *not* converge absolutely, regardless of sign alternation. The student confuses the criteria for absolute vs. conditional convergence. Option B correctly identifies that absolute convergence ignores signs, making the student's justification invalid.

Q14. You are given that \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| = 2 \) for a series \( \sum a_k \). A colleague suggests multiplying each term by \( r^k \) to force convergence. What is the minimum condition on \( r \) to guarantee the modified series \( \sum a_k r^k \) converges absolutely?

A. \( |r| < 2 \)
B. \( |r| < 1/2 \) ✅
C. \( |r| > 2 \)
D. \( |r| = 1/2 \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This application question tests understanding of how geometric scaling interacts with the ratio test. For the modified series \( b_k = a_k r^k \), the ratio is \( \left| \frac{a_{k+1} r^{k+1}}{a_k r^k} \right| = \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \right| |r| \to 2|r| \). For absolute convergence, we need \( 2|r| < 1 \Rightarrow |r| < 1/2 \). This demonstrates that multiplying by \( r^k \) effectively rescales the convergence radius by \( 1/r \). If the original series diverges because terms grow like \( 2^k \), we must dampen them with a factor decaying faster than \( 2^{-k} \). This principle underlies generating function manipulations and Borel summation techniques, where scaling parameters are tuned to access regions of convergence.

Q15. Which of the following series represents a case where the Ratio Test for Absolute Convergence is theoretically applicable but practically inferior to the Root Test due to the structure of the general term?

A. \( \sum \frac{n!}{n^n} \)
B. \( \sum \left( \frac{n+1}{2n} \right)^n \) ✅
C. \( \sum \frac{2^n}{n!} \)
D. \( \sum \frac{n^2}{3^n} \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While the ratio test works for all these series, it is 'practically inferior' for \( \sum \left( \frac{n+1}{2n} \right)^n \) because the term is naturally expressed as \( (b_n)^n \). Applying the ratio test requires handling \( \left( \frac{n+2}{2(n+1)} \right)^{n+1} / \left( \frac{n+1}{2n} \right)^n \), which involves limits of the form \( (1 + c/n)^n \to e^c \), adding unnecessary complexity. The root test simply takes the nth root, yielding \( \frac{n+1}{2n} \to 1/2 \) immediately. For factorials (A, C) or simple exponentials (D), the ratio test is actually simpler or comparable. This question trains recognition of structural cues that favor one test over another, optimizing problem-solving efficiency.

Q16. In error analysis of a computational algorithm, the truncation error is bounded by the tail of a series \( \sum a_k \). If the ratio test shows \( \lim |a_{k+1}/a_k| = \rho < 1 \), which bound best estimates the remainder \( R_N = \sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty} a_k \) for large N?

A. \( R_N \approx a_N \)
B. \( R_N \approx \frac{a_{N+1}}{1-\rho} \) ✅
C. \( R_N \approx \frac{a_N}{1-\rho} \)
D. \( R_N \approx a_{N+1} \rho^N \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When the ratio of consecutive terms approaches \( \rho < 1 \), the tail of the series behaves asymptotically like a geometric series with ratio \( \rho \). Specifically, for large \( N \), \( a_{N+k} \approx a_{N+1} \rho^{k-1} \). Summing this geometric progression gives \( R_N \approx \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} a_{N+1} \rho^j = \frac{a_{N+1}}{1-\rho} \). This is a powerful practical estimate in numerical analysis, far superior to just using the first neglected term \( a_{N+1} \) (which underestimates error) or integral bounds (which may be hard to compute). Option B correctly uses \( a_{N+1} \) as the starting term of the geometric approximation. Note: Some texts use \( a_N/(1-\rho) \) as a looser bound, but \( a_{N+1}/(1-\rho) \) is the asymptotic estimate for the *remainder after N terms*. Given standard conventions, B is the precise asymptotic estimator.

Q17. A student analyzes \( \sum \frac{k!}{k^k} x^k \) and finds \( \rho = |x|/e \). They then claim the series converges absolutely for \( x = e \) because Stirling's formula shows terms behave like \( \sqrt{2\pi k} (k/e)^k / k^k \cdot e^k = \sqrt{2\pi k} \), which goes to infinity, so... wait, they conclude convergence. D. Stirling's formula cannot be used at the boundary of convergence.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

First, the ratio test limit is indeed \( 1/e \), so \( \rho = |x|/e \). At \( x=e \), \( \rho=1 \), so the ratio test is inconclusive. Second, applying Stirling's: \( k! \sim \sqrt{2\pi k} (k/e)^k \). Substituting into \( a_k e^k \): \( \frac{\sqrt{2\pi k} (k/e)^k}{k^k} e^k = \sqrt{2\pi k} \). Since \( \sqrt{2\pi k} \to \infty \), the terms do not approach zero; they blow up. By the divergence test, the series diverges at \( x=e \). The student's conclusion of convergence is wrong, and their intermediate Stirling calculation (if they got \( \to \infty \)) actually proves divergence, not convergence. Option B captures both the correct asymptotic behavior and the logical contradiction in the student's claim. This integrates ratio test boundaries, asymptotic analysis, and basic divergence criteria.
