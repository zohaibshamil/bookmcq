Q1. A student attempts to evaluate the infinite sum \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 3(0.5)^{k-1} \) by identifying \( a=3 \) and \( r=0.5 \), obtaining a sum of 6. However, they then claim that \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 3(0.5)^k \) must also equal 6 because the ratio is identical. Which statement best analyzes this error?

🔹 A. The student is correct because changing the starting index does not affect the sum of a convergent geometric series.
🔹 B. The student incorrectly assumed the first term remains 3; in the second series, the first term is actually 1.5, making the sum 3.
🔹 C. The student failed to check convergence first; the second series diverges because the exponent matches the index.
🔹 D. The error lies in using \( k-1 \) instead of \( k \); the formula \( a/(1-r) \) only applies when the exponent is exactly \( k \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question targets error analysis regarding index shifting in geometric series. While both series converge with the same ratio \( r=0.5 \), their sums differ because the initial terms are distinct. The series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 3(0.5)^{k-1} \) starts at 3, yielding \( 3/(1-0.5)=6 \). Conversely, \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 3(0.5)^k \) begins at \( 3(0.5)^1 = 1.5 \), resulting in a sum of \( 1.5/(1-0.5)=3 \). Recognizing that the standard formula requires identifying the actual first term, not just the coefficient outside the power, is crucial for avoiding this common misconception.

Q2. Consider a physical model where a ball bounces to a height of \( h_n = H \cdot r^n \) after the \( n \)-th bounce, with \( 0 < r < 1 \). 💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Modeling total distance in bouncing problems requires distinguishing between the initial drop and subsequent up-down pairs. The ball falls \( H \) initially. Afterward, it travels up \( Hr \) and down \( Hr \), then up \( Hr^2 \) and down \( Hr^2 \), creating a geometric series of paired distances: \( 2Hr + 2Hr^2 + \dots \). This secondary series has first term \( 2Hr \) and ratio \( r \), summing to \( \frac{2Hr}{1-r} \). Adding the initial drop \( H \) gives \( H + \frac{2Hr}{1-r} \). Options ignoring the factor of 2 or misplacing the initial term fail to capture the complete physical trajectory described.

Q4. Analyze the following argument: 'Since \( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \) oscillates between 0 and 1, its Cesàro mean is 0.5. Therefore, in the context of standard calculus convergence tests for geometric series, the sum equals 0.5.' Why is this reasoning invalid in standard analysis?

🔹 A. Cesàro summation is equivalent to standard convergence for all alternating series.
🔹 B. Standard geometric series convergence strictly requires \( |r| < 1 \); since \( |-1| = 1 \), the series diverges regardless of averaging methods.
🔹 C. The series actually converges to 0 because the terms approach zero on average.
🔹 D. The argument is valid; 0.5 is the accepted sum in all mathematical contexts including engineering.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question addresses error analysis and conceptual boundaries. While generalized summation methods like Cesàro or Abel summation can assign values to divergent series, standard calculus defines convergence via partial sums. For a geometric series \( \sum ar^n \), convergence occurs if and only if \( |r| < 1 \). Here \( r=-1 \), so \( |r|=1 \), causing partial sums to oscillate indefinitely without approaching a limit. Thus, in standard analysis, the series diverges and has no sum. Confusing generalized summability with standard convergence is a critical conceptual error that undermines rigorous limit definitions used throughout calculus.

Q5. If the graph of the sequence of partial sums \( S_n \) for a geometric series approaches a horizontal asymptote \( y=L \) from below in a monotonic increasing fashion, what can be definitively concluded about the first term \( a \) and common ratio \( r \)?

🔹 B. \( a > 0 \) and \( -1 < r < 0 \)
🔹 C. \( a < 0 \) and \( 0 < r < 1 \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Interpreting graphs of partial sums reveals properties of the underlying series. A horizontal asymptote indicates convergence, requiring \( |r| < 1 \). Monotonic increase implies each added term \( ar^n \) is positive. Since \( r^n \) is always positive for \( 0 < r < 1 \), \( a \) must be positive. If \( r \) were negative, partial sums would oscillate around the limit rather than approaching monotonically. If \( a \) were negative with positive \( r \), the sum would decrease toward the asymptote. Thus, only \( a > 0 \) and \( 0 < r < 1 \) produces the described graphical behavior, linking visual intuition to algebraic parameters.

Q10. A medication dose decays geometrically in the bloodstream. A patient takes 100mg daily, and 20% of previous day's dose remains at next administration. What is the steady-state concentration immediately after taking a dose?

Q14. Compare the rates of convergence for \( S_A = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (0.1)^n \) and \( S_B = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (0.9)^n \). Which requires fewer terms to achieve 0.001 accuracy?

🔹 A. \( S_B \) because larger ratio means faster approach to limit.
🔹 B. \( S_A \) because smaller ratio causes terms to diminish more rapidly.
🔹 C. Both require identical terms since both converge.
🔹 D. Cannot determine without computing partial sums explicitly.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Conceptual understanding of convergence speed. Geometric series error after \( N \) terms is proportional to \( r^{N+1}/(1-r) \). Smaller \( |r| \) means exponential decay dominates, reaching tolerance faster. For \( r=0.1 \), terms drop by factor 10 each step; for \( r=0.9 \), decay is slow. To get error <0.001, \( S_A \) needs ~3 terms (\( 0.1^3=0.001 \)), while \( S_B \) needs ~60+ terms since \( 0.9^N \approx 0.001 \Rightarrow N \approx \ln(0.001)/\ln(0.9) \approx 65 \). Thus smaller ratio enables efficient approximation. This principle guides practical computation choices in numerical methods.

Q19. Which statement correctly distinguishes between the sequence \( \{ar^n\} \) and the series \( \sum ar^n \) regarding convergence?

Q20. If \( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n \) is geometric with sum \( S \), and \( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n \) is geometric with sum \( T \), is \( \sum (a_n + b_n) \) necessarily geometric?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

Conceptual understanding of series operations. Sum of two geometric series is geometric iff they have identical ratios. However, if ratios match (\( r \)), sum is \( \sum (a+b)r^n \), geometric with sum \( (a+b)/(1-r) = S+T \). This shows closure properties depend on parameter alignment, testing deeper structural understanding beyond mere summation formulas.

Q22. Analyze the convergence of \( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{3^n + 2^n}{5^n} \).

🔹 A. Diverges because numerator grows exponentially.
🔹 B. Converges as sum of two geometric series with ratios \( 3/5 \) and \( 2/5 \).
🔹 C. Converges only if rewritten as single geometric series.
🔹 D. Diverges by ratio test since limit is 1.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Decomposition strategy for complex series. Split into \( \sum (3/5)^n + \sum (2/5)^n \). Both are geometric with \( |r|<1 \), hence convergent. Sum of convergent series converges. Ratio test on original gives \( \lim \frac{3^{n+1}+2^{n+1}}{5^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{5^n}{3^n+2^n} = \lim \frac{3(3/5)^n + 2(2/5)^n}{(3/5)^n + (2/5)^n} \cdot \frac{1}{5} \to \frac{3}{5} < 1 \), confirming convergence. Decomposition simplifies analysis and reveals structure. This technique applies broadly to linear combinations of exponentials, emphasizing algebraic manipulation before mechanical testing.
