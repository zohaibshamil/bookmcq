📝 Comparison test for series

📖 From Calculus • 10. Infinite Series in Calculus • 40 questions available

What is Comparison test for series?

If \( 0 \le a_n \le b_n \) for all large \( n \), and \( \sum b_n \) converges, then \( \sum a_n \) converges; if \( a_n \ge b_n \ge 0 \) and \( \sum b_n \) diverges, then \( \sum a_n \) diverges; for example, \( \sum \frac{1}{n^2+1} \) converges by comparing to \( \sum \frac{1}{n^2} \).

Q1. A student analyzes the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k} - 0.5} \) and claims it converges because for large \( k \), the term behaves like \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \), which is a known benchmark. Which statement best identifies the flaw in this reasoning regarding the Comparison Test?

A. The student correctly identified the asymptotic behavior but applied the Limit Comparison Test instead of the Direct Comparison Test.
B. The student assumed that asymptotic similarity guarantees convergence without verifying the inequality direction required for the Direct Comparison Test. ✅
C. The student failed to recognize that \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \) actually diverges, making the comparison irrelevant for proving convergence.
D. The student should have used the Ratio Test because the denominator contains a subtraction operation.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The core misconception here is confusing asymptotic equivalence with the strict inequality requirements of the Direct Comparison Test. While the terms behave similarly, the Direct Comparison Test requires finding a specific convergent series with larger terms to prove convergence, or a divergent series with smaller terms to prove divergence. Simply stating they are 'similar' is insufficient; one must rigorously establish \( a_k \leq b_k \) or \( a_k \geq b_k \). Furthermore, since \( \sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \) diverges, comparing to it cannot prove convergence regardless of inequality direction.

Q2. Consider the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k^2 + 3k}{2k^4 - k} \). When applying the Limit Comparison Test, which choice of comparison series \( b_k \) provides the most efficient path to a conclusive result while avoiding algebraic complexity?

A. \( b_k = \frac{1}{k^2} \) because the numerator degree is 2 and denominator degree is 4. ✅
B. \( b_k = \frac{1}{k^4} \) because the highest power in the denominator dominates.
C. \( b_k = \frac{k}{2k^4} \) because it preserves the coefficient structure.
D. \( b_k = \frac{1}{k^3} \) because it represents the difference in degrees between numerator and denominator.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Selecting the correct comparison series involves identifying the dominant terms in both numerator and denominator. Here, the numerator behaves like \( k^2 \) and the denominator like \( 2k^4 \), so the general term behaves like \( \frac{k^2}{2k^4} = \frac{1}{2k^2} \). Choosing \( b_k = \frac{1}{k^2} \) yields a finite, non-zero limit. Choosing \( \frac{1}{k^4} \) would yield a limit of infinity, and \( \frac{1}{k^3} \) would yield zero, both of which require additional theoretical justification (extended limit comparison test) rather than providing immediate confirmation via the standard theorem.

Q3. You are given two series with positive terms, \( \sum a_k \) and \( \sum b_k \). If \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{a_k}{b_k} = 0 \) and \( \sum b_k \) converges, what can be definitively concluded about \( \sum a_k \)?

A. \( \sum a_k \) diverges because the ratio approaching zero implies \( a_k \) is negligible.
B. \( \sum a_k \) converges because \( a_k \) becomes insignificant relative to the convergent series \( b_k \). ✅
C. No conclusion can be drawn because the limit is not a positive finite number.
D. \( \sum a_k \) converges only if \( a_k < b_k \) for all \( k \), not just eventually.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question tests the extended version of the Limit Comparison Test often overlooked in basic instruction. When the limit is zero, it means \( a_k \) is of a smaller order of magnitude than \( b_k \). If the 'larger' series \( \sum b_k \) converges, then the 'smaller' series \( \sum a_k \) must also converge. This is distinct from the standard case where the limit is finite and positive. Students often incorrectly believe the test fails when the limit is zero, but it actually provides strong evidence for convergence when compared against a convergent benchmark.

Q4. Analyze the series \( \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k \ln k} \). Why does the Direct Comparison Test fail to determine its divergence when using the harmonic series \( \sum \frac{1}{k} \) as the benchmark?

A. Because \( \frac{1}{k \ln k} > \frac{1}{k} \) for \( k \geq 2 \), and comparing a larger series to a divergent series is inconclusive.
B. Because \( \frac{1}{k \ln k} < \frac{1}{k} \) for \( k \geq 2 \), and showing a series is smaller than a divergent series proves nothing. ✅
C. Because the logarithmic function oscillates, preventing a consistent inequality.
D. Because the harmonic series converges, making it an invalid benchmark for divergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Direct Comparison Test has strict directional logic. To prove divergence, you must show your series is *larger* than a known divergent series. Since \( \ln k > 1 \) for \( k \geq 2 \), we have \( k \ln k > k \), which implies \( \frac{1}{k \ln k} < \frac{1}{k} \). Being smaller than a divergent series (the harmonic series) does not guarantee divergence; the series could still converge. This highlights why the Integral Test or Cauchy Condensation Test is often necessary for logarithmic series where direct comparison to p-series fails due to inequality direction.

Graphical analysis guides the choice of the sophisticated benchmark needed for the Limit Comparison Test.

Q6. A student attempts to prove that \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin^2 k}{k^2} \) converges by arguing that since \( \sin^2 k \leq 1 \), the series is less than \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2} \). Is this application of the Comparison Test valid?

Q9. In modeling population dynamics, a discrete growth model yields a summation term \( \sum \frac{n! e^n}{n^{n+1}} \). Standard p-series comparisons fail. Which advanced comparison technique is most suitable for determining convergence?

A. Direct Comparison with \( \sum \frac{1}{n} \)
B. Limit Comparison with \( \sum \frac{1}{n^2} \)
C. Stirling’s Approximation combined with Limit Comparison ✅
D. Integral Test with substitution \( u = \ln x \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Factorials combined with exponentials and powers suggest Stirling's approximation \( n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n} (\frac{n}{e})^n \). Substituting this into the general term gives \( \frac{\sqrt{2\pi n} (n/e)^n e^n}{n^{n+1}} = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi n} n^n}{n^{n+1}} = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{n^{1/2}} \). This reveals the series behaves like \( \sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \), which diverges. Direct comparison is impossible due to complexity, and the Integral Test is intractable with factorials. This problem synthesizes asymptotic analysis tools with the Limit Comparison Test framework, representing a higher-order application typical in advanced calculus or mathematical biology contexts.

Q10. A student claims that since \( \frac{k+1}{k^3+k} < \frac{k+1}{k^3} \) and \( \sum \frac{k+1}{k^3} \) converges, the original series converges. Evaluate the validity of this specific inequality setup.

The student correctly manipulated the inequality. Since \( k^3 + k > k^3 \) for \( k \geq 1 \), the reciprocal satisfies \( \frac{1}{k^3+k} < \frac{1}{k^3} \). Multiplying by the positive numerator \( (k+1) \) preserves the inequality: \( \frac{k+1}{k^3+k} < \frac{k+1}{k^3} = \frac{1}{k^2} + \frac{1}{k^3} \). Since both \( \sum 1/k^2 \) and \( \sum 1/k^3 \) converge, their sum converges. Thus, the original series is bounded above by a convergent series. This tests algebraic manipulation skills within the context of establishing valid comparison inequalities, ensuring students don't just memorize forms but understand fraction properties.

Q13. For the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{3^k + k}{4^k - k^2} \), which simplified form best serves as the comparison series \( b_k \) in the Limit Comparison Test?

A. \( \frac{3^k}{4^k} \) ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Choosing polynomial benchmarks like \( 1/k \) or \( 1/k^2 \) would yield limits of 0 or infinity, complicating the analysis. Recognizing exponential dominance is a key skill in selecting appropriate benchmarks for mixed-type series. This ensures the Limit Comparison Test yields a clean, finite, non-zero limit.

Q20. Why is the series \( \sum \frac{1}{k + \sqrt{k}} \) frequently used as a pedagogical example for the Limit Comparison Test rather than the Direct Comparison Test?

This question highlights the pragmatic motivation for learning multiple tests: efficiency and reduction of algebraic friction. 📖 Explanation:

While not a direct application of the standard Comparison Test (which requires positive terms), this problem uses the underlying logic of asymptotic dominance. If \( \sum a_k \) converges and \( \sum b_k \) diverges (with positive terms), then \( a_k + b_k \sim b_k \). By Limit Comparison with \( b_k \), \( \lim \frac{a_k+b_k}{b_k} = 1 \). Since \( \sum b_k \) diverges, \( \sum (a_k+b_k) \) diverges. This synthesizes algebraic properties of series with comparison logic. It prevents students from treating tests as isolated silos and promotes viewing convergence as a property of dominant asymptotic behavior.

Q22. A student uses the Comparison Test to analyze \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2 + \sin k} \). They argue \( \frac{1}{k^2 + \sin k} \leq \frac{1}{k^2 - 1} \). Why is this technically risky despite leading to the correct conclusion?

A. Because \( k^2 - 1 \) can be zero or negative for small \( k \), violating the positive term requirement. ✅
B. Because \( \sin k \) is sometimes negative.
C. Because the inequality is backwards.
D. Because \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2-1} \) diverges.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Comparison Test strictly requires positive terms. For \( k=1 \), \( k^2-1 = 0 \), making the bound undefined. Even for \( k=2 \), it's positive, but the domain issue at the start violates formal conditions. A safer bound is \( \frac{1}{k^2 + \sin k} \leq \frac{1}{k^2 - 1} \) only for \( k \geq 2 \), or better yet, \( \frac{1}{k^2 - 1} \) replaced by \( \frac{2}{k^2} \) for all \( k \geq 1 \). This question emphasizes rigor: correct conclusions derived from technically flawed premises are still mathematical errors. Students must respect domain constraints even when the asymptotic intuition is sound.

Q23. Which series represents the 'slowest' divergent series commonly used as a benchmark in the Comparison Test hierarchy?

A. Harmonic series \( \sum 1/k \)
B. \( \sum 1/(k \ln k) \)
C. \( \sum 1/(k \ln k \ln(\ln k)) \) ✅
D. Geometric series with \( r=1 \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Standard curriculum focuses on \( 1/k \), but advanced analysis recognizes the logarithmic hierarchy. \( \sum \frac{1}{k \ln k \ln(\ln k)} \) diverges more slowly than \( \sum \frac{1}{k \ln k} \), which diverges more slowly than \( \sum 1/k \). Using these as benchmarks allows resolution of extremely subtle divergence cases where \( 1/k \) is too coarse. This question exposes students to the infinite gradation of divergence rates, challenging the binary 'converge/diverge' mindset and introducing the concept of logarithmic scales in series analysis, relevant for Olympiad-level problems.

Q24. When applying the Limit Comparison Test to \( \sum \frac{\sqrt{k+1} - \sqrt{k}}{k} \), rationalizing the numerator is a strategic step. What simplified form emerges as the ideal benchmark?

A. \( \frac{1}{k} \)
B. \( \frac{1}{k^{3/2}} \) ✅
C. \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \)
D. \( \frac{1}{k^2} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Rationalizing: \( \frac{(\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt{k})(\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt{k})}{k(\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt{k})} = \frac{1}{k(\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt{k})} \). For large \( k \), denominator \( \approx k(2\sqrt{k}) = 2k^{3/2} \). Thus, term \( \approx \frac{1}{2k^{3/2}} \). Benchmark is \( 1/k^{3/2} \). This converges (\( p=1.5 \)). Without rationalization, the indeterminate form \( \infty - \infty \) obscures the true decay rate. This problem integrates algebraic preprocessing with test selection, demonstrating that comparison tests often require preparatory simplification to reveal the underlying asymptotic structure.

Q25. True or False: If \( 0 \leq a_k \leq b_k \) and \( \sum b_k \) diverges, then \( \sum a_k \) must diverge.

A. true
B. false ✅
C.
D.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This is the most common logical fallacy in series testing. Being smaller than a divergent series proves nothing. The smaller series could converge (e.g., \( 1/k^2 < 1/k \)) or diverge (e.g., \( 1/(2k) < 1/k \)). The Comparison Test only yields conclusions when the inequality aligns with the known behavior: smaller than convergent implies convergent; larger than divergent implies divergent. All other combinations are inconclusive. Mastery of this logical matrix is foundational for avoiding false positives in convergence proofs.

Q26. In a computational simulation, you encounter \( \sum \frac{k!}{k^k} \). Why is the Comparison Test generally inferior to the Ratio Test for this specific series?

A. Because factorial growth creates complex inequalities that are hard to bound with simple p-series. ✅
B. Because the Ratio Test is always faster.
C. Because the series alternates.
D. Because \( k^k \) is not a polynomial.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

While one *could* compare \( k!/k^k \) to a geometric series using Stirling's approximation, establishing the necessary inequality rigorously is difficult. The Ratio Test, however, exploits the recursive structure of factorials naturally: \( \frac{(k+1)!}{(k+1)^{k+1}} \cdot \frac{k^k}{k!} = \frac{k+1}{(k+1)^{k+1}} k^k = (\frac{k}{k+1})^k \to 1/e < 1 \). This is algebraically self-contained. The Comparison Test forces external benchmarking against unrelated functions, whereas Ratio Test uses internal structure. Choosing the right tool based on term structure is a higher-order metacognitive skill.

Q27. Analyze the series \( \sum \frac{1}{k^2 + k \sin k} \). Despite the oscillating sine term, why does it converge?

A. Because \( k^2 \) dominates \( k \sin k \), keeping the term bounded above by \( C/k^2 \). ✅
B. Because \( \sin k \) averages to zero.
C. Because it is an alternating series.
D. Because \( k \sin k \) is always positive.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Although \( \sin k \) oscillates, \( |k \sin k| \leq k \). Thus \( k^2 + k \sin k \geq k^2 - k \). For \( k \geq 2 \), \( k^2 - k \geq k^2/2 \). So \( \frac{1}{k^2 + k \sin k} \leq \frac{2}{k^2} \). Since \( \sum 2/k^2 \) converges, the original series converges absolutely. The oscillation doesn't prevent bounding; it just requires careful absolute value estimation. This counters the misconception that oscillating terms automatically require alternating series tests or prevent direct comparison. Dominant polynomial terms stabilize the behavior.

Q28. Which scenario renders the Limit Comparison Test completely inapplicable?

A. When the limit is 1.
B. When terms are not eventually positive. ✅
C. When the benchmark series is geometric.
D. When the limit is irrational.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Limit Comparison Test fundamentally relies on the preservation of sign to equate convergence behaviors. If terms oscillate in sign (and aren't absolutely convergent), the ratio limit might exist but carry no implication about conditional convergence. For example, \( a_k = (-1)^k/\sqrt{k} \) and \( b_k = 1/\sqrt{k} \) have ratio limit magnitude 1, but \( \sum a_k \) converges conditionally while \( \sum b_k \) diverges. Positivity is the non-negotiable hypothesis. This distinguishes structural prerequisites from mere computational outcomes.

The inequality \( e^{-k}/k < 1/k \) is correct. The error is purely logical: being smaller than a divergent series proves nothing. Q30. For the series \( \sum \frac{\arctan k}{k^2} \), which constant bound makes the Direct Comparison Test trivial?

Q31. Consider \( \sum \frac{k^p}{k^q + 1} \). Under what condition does the Limit Comparison Test with \( b_k = k^{p-q} \) guarantee convergence?

Q34. Which visual feature in a log-log plot of terms \( a_k \) vs \( k \) indicates suitability for p-series comparison?

Q35. For \( \sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}} \), why is comparing to \( \sum 1/k \) via DCT inconclusive for proving convergence, but useful for proving divergence?
