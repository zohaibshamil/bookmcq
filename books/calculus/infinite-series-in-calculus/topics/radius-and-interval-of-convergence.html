What is Radius and interval of convergence?

For a power series \( \sum c_n (x-a)^n \), the radius of convergence \( R \) is found by \( \frac{1}{R} = \lim |c_{n+1}/c_n| \) or \( \lim \sqrt[n]{|c_n|} \); the series converges absolutely for \( |x-a| < R \), diverges for \( |x-a| > R \), and the interval of convergence is the set of \( x \) values where it converges (including endpoints checked separately).

Q1. A student claims that because the power series \( \sum c_k x^k \) converges at \( x = 4 \), it must also converge absolutely at \( x = -3 \). Which statement best evaluates this claim based on the properties of power series centered at zero?

A. The claim is always true because convergence at a positive endpoint implies absolute convergence everywhere inside the radius.
B. The claim is false because convergence at one endpoint does not guarantee absolute convergence at other points within the interval, only conditional convergence might exist.
C. The claim is true because \( |-3| < |4| \), so \( x=-3 \) lies strictly inside the interval of absolute convergence defined by the known convergence point. ✅
D. The claim is uncertain without knowing if the series converges conditionally or absolutely at \( x=4 \); however, since \( |-3| < 4 \), it definitely converges absolutely regardless of endpoint behavior.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

According to the fundamental theorem of power series, if a series converges at a specific value \( x = x_1 \), it converges absolutely for all \( x \) such that \( |x| < |x_1| \). Since \( |-3| = 3 \) and \( 3 < 4 \), the point \( x = -3 \) is strictly interior to the disk of convergence determined by \( x=4 \). Therefore, absolute convergence is guaranteed at \( x=-3 \) irrespective of whether the convergence at \( x=4 \) was conditional or absolute. This tests understanding of the geometric nature of convergence intervals centered at the origin.

Q2. Consider the power series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(x-2)^k}{k} \). After applying the ratio test, a student finds the radius of convergence is \( R=1 \). They conclude the interval of convergence is \( (1, 3) \). What is the critical error in this analysis?

A. The student failed to check the endpoints \( x=1 \) and \( x=3 \) separately, as the ratio test is inconclusive when the limit equals 1. ✅
B. The student calculated the radius incorrectly; the radius should be determined by the root test for this specific series form.
C. The student assumed the center was 0 instead of 2, leading to an incorrect interval setup.
D. The student forgot that the harmonic series diverges, which affects the radius calculation itself.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The ratio test determines the open interval of absolute convergence but provides no information about the boundary points where the limit of the ratio equals exactly 1. For this series, at \( x=3 \), the series becomes the harmonic series \( \sum 1/k \) which diverges, while at \( x=1 \), it becomes the alternating harmonic series \( \sum (-1)^k/k \) which converges conditionally. Thus, the correct interval is \( [1, 3) \). Failing to test endpoints individually is a common procedural error that leads to incomplete solutions in power series problems.

Q3. Given the graph of a function \( f(x) \) represented by a power series centered at \( x=0 \), you observe vertical asymptotes at \( x = -5 \) and \( x = 5 \). The function is continuous and smooth between these asymptotes. Based solely on this graphical information, what can be definitively inferred about the radius of convergence \( R \)?

A. \( R \) must be exactly 5 because singularities bound the interval of convergence. ✅
B. \( R \) could be greater than 5 if the series representation extends beyond the visible graph.
C. \( R \) must be less than 5 because the function is undefined at the asymptotes.
D. \( R \) is unrelated to the location of asymptotes for real-valued functions.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For a real power series centered at \( x_0 \), the radius of convergence \( R \) is the distance from the center to the nearest singularity in the complex plane. On the real line, vertical asymptotes represent singularities where the function ceases to be analytic. Since the nearest singularities are at \( \pm 5 \) and the center is 0, the radius of convergence cannot exceed 5. Furthermore, since the function is smooth between them, the series converges up to these points, making \( R=5 \). This connects graphical analysis of singularities directly to the analytic concept of convergence radius.

Q4. Two power series \( \sum a_k x^k \) and \( \sum b_k x^k \) have radii of convergence \( R_a = 3 \) and \( R_b = 5 \) respectively. Let \( S(x) = \sum (a_k + b_k)x^k \). A student argues that the radius of convergence for \( S(x) \) must be exactly 3. Under what specific condition would this student's conclusion be incorrect?

A. If the terms \( a_k \) and \( b_k \) cancel each other out significantly for large \( k \), potentially increasing the radius beyond 3. ✅
B. If both series diverge at their respective endpoints.
C. If \( R_a \) was determined by the ratio test and \( R_b \) by the root test.
D. The student's conclusion is never incorrect; the radius of a sum is always the minimum of the individual radii.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Generally, the radius of convergence of a sum is at least the minimum of the individual radii. However, if the coefficients \( a_k \) and \( b_k \) are related such that their sum decays faster than either sequence individually (e.g., \( b_k = -a_k \) for large \( k \)), the resulting series may have a larger radius of convergence. For instance, if \( a_k = 1/3^k \) and \( b_k = -1/3^k + 1/10^k \), then \( R_a=3, R_b=3 \), but \( a_k+b_k = 1/10^k \), yielding \( R=10 \). Thus, assuming \( R_S = \min(R_a, R_b) \) ignores potential cancellation effects.

Q5. When finding the interval of convergence for \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!(x+1)^k}{(2k)!} \), which testing method is most efficient and why?

A. Ratio Test, because factorials simplify nicely in ratios of consecutive terms. ✅
B. Root Test, because it handles factorials better than the ratio test.
C. Integral Test, because factorials relate to gamma functions.
D. Comparison Test, comparing directly to a geometric series.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Ratio Test is specifically designed for series involving factorials and exponentials because the ratio \( \frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k} \) allows for algebraic cancellation of factorial terms (e.g., \( (2(k+1))! / (2k)! \)). The Root Test would require evaluating \( \sqrt[k]{k!} \), which is more complex and often requires Stirling's approximation. The Integral Test applies to functions, not discrete factorial sequences directly. Therefore, recognizing the structural cue of factorials immediately points to the Ratio Test as the optimal tool for determining the radius of convergence efficiently.

Experimental data shows the model fails catastrophically at \( T = T_0 + 150 \) K but works perfectly at \( T = T_0 + 100 \) K. Assuming the nearest physical singularity dictates convergence, what is the most likely radius of convergence for this series?

A. Exactly 150 K
B. At least 100 K but likely no more than 150 K ✅
C. Exactly 100 K
D. Greater than 150 K

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

In applied modeling, the radius of convergence corresponds to the distance to the nearest singularity or phase transition in the physical system. Since the model works at \( \Delta T = 100 \), the radius \( R \) must be \( \geq 100 \). Since it fails at \( \Delta T = 150 \), \( R \) must be \( \leq 150 \). It is unlikely to be exactly 150 unless the failure point coincides precisely with a mathematical singularity, but physically, the valid domain is bounded by these observations. This question links abstract convergence concepts to empirical validation in scientific modeling.

Q7. Analyze the following incorrect solution step: 'For the series \( \sum \frac{x^k}{k^2} \), the ratio test gives limit \( L = |x| \). Setting \( L < 1 \) gives \( |x| < 1 \). At \( x=1 \), \( L=1 \), so the series diverges.' What is the fundamental flaw in this reasoning?

A. Applying the ratio test conclusion to the endpoint where \( L=1 \); the test is inconclusive there. ✅
B. Calculating the limit \( L \) incorrectly for this series.
C. Assuming the series starts at \( k=1 \) instead of \( k=0 \).
D. Confusing absolute convergence with conditional convergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Ratio Test states that if \( \lim |u_{k+1}/u_k| = L < 1 \), the series converges absolutely, and if \( L > 1 \), it diverges. However, if \( L = 1 \), the test yields absolutely no information. In this specific case, at \( x=1 \), the series becomes \( \sum 1/k^2 \), which is a convergent p-series. The student erroneously treated the inconclusive case \( L=1 \) as a divergence criterion. Endpoints must always be tested using separate methods like the p-series test, integral test, or alternating series test.

Q8. Which of the following modifications to the series \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k x^k \) with radius \( R \) will result in a new series with radius of convergence \( \sqrt{R} \)?

A. Replacing \( x \) with \( x^2 \) ✅
B. Multiplying each term by \( x \)
C. Differentiating the series term-by-term
D. Integrating the series term-by-term

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Substituting \( x^2 \) for \( x \) transforms the series into \( \sum c_k (x^2)^k = \sum c_k x^{2k} \). For this new series to converge, we require \( |x^2| < R \), which simplifies to \( |x| < \sqrt{R} \). Thus, the radius of convergence becomes \( \sqrt{R} \). Differentiation and integration preserve the original radius \( R \). Multiplying by \( x \) shifts indices but does not change the radius. This tests understanding of how variable substitutions affect the domain of convergence versus operations that preserve it.

Q9. You are given two series: \( A(x) = \sum a_k x^k \) converging on \( (-2, 2) \) and \( B(x) = \sum b_k x^k \) converging on \( (-3, 3] \). Consider the product series formed by Cauchy multiplication. On which interval is the product series guaranteed to converge absolutely?

A. \( (-2, 2) \) ✅
B. \( (-3, 3) \)
C. \( [-2, 2] \)
D. \( (-2, 3] \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Cauchy product of two power series converges absolutely at least on the intersection of their intervals of absolute convergence. Series A converges absolutely on \( (-2, 2) \). Series B converges absolutely on \( (-3, 3) \) (note: convergence at endpoint 3 may be conditional). The intersection of the open intervals of absolute convergence is \( (-2, 2) \). While the product might converge on a larger set depending on specific coefficients, it is only mathematically guaranteed on the smaller of the two radii. This emphasizes the distinction between guaranteed bounds and potential extensions.

Q10. A student computes the radius of convergence for \( \sum \frac{(2x)^k}{k!} \) and obtains \( R = 1/2 \). Another student obtains \( R = \infty \). Who is correct and why?

A. The second student is correct because the factorial in the denominator dominates any exponential growth in the numerator, leading to infinite radius. ✅
B. The first student is correct because the coefficient of \( x^k \) includes \( 2^k \), which scales the radius by \( 1/2 \).
C. Both are partially correct depending on whether they used the ratio or root test.
D. The first student is correct because \( e^{2x} \) has a finite radius of convergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Rewriting the series as \( \sum \frac{2^k}{k!} x^k \), applying the ratio test yields \( \lim |\frac{2^{k+1} x^{k+1}}{(k+1)!} \cdot \frac{k!}{2^k x^k}| = \lim |\frac{2x}{k+1}| = 0 \) for any fixed \( x \). Since the limit is 0 for all real numbers, the series converges everywhere, meaning \( R = \infty \). The first student likely confused this with a geometric series \( \sum (2x)^k \) where \( R=1/2 \). Recognizing the dominance of factorials over exponentials is crucial for correctly identifying infinite radii in Taylor-type series.

Q11. Suppose \( f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k (x-3)^k \) has a radius of convergence \( R=4 \). If we define \( g(x) = f'(x) \), what is the interval of convergence for \( g(x) \)?

A. Exactly \( (-1, 7) \)
B. At least \( (-1, 7) \), but endpoints must be re-evaluated ✅
C. Exactly \( [-1, 7] \)
D. Exactly \( (-1, 7] \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Differentiation of a power series preserves the radius of convergence \( R \), so \( g(x) \) also has \( R=4 \) centered at 3, giving the open interval \( (-1, 7) \). However, differentiation can destroy convergence at the endpoints. A series might converge conditionally at an endpoint while its derivative diverges there (e.g., \( \sum x^k/k^2 \) vs \( \sum x^{k-1}/k \)). Therefore, while the interior is safe, the endpoints of the derived series must be tested independently and cannot be assumed to match the original series' endpoint behavior.

Q12. Consider the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k (x-5)^{2k}}{k \cdot 4^k} \). A student applies the ratio test and sets up \( \lim |\frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k}| = \frac{|x-5|^2}{4} < 1 \). Solving this gives \( |x-5| < 2 \). Why is treating this as a standard power series in \( (x-5) \) potentially misleading regarding the 'radius'?

A. Because the series contains only even powers, it is effectively a series in \( u=(x-5)^2 \) with radius 4 in u-space, translating to radius 2 in x-space. ✅
B. Because the alternating sign changes the radius calculation.
C. Because the ratio test cannot handle even powers directly.
D. There is no misleading aspect; the radius is simply 2.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This series lacks odd powers of \( (x-5) \), making it a power series in the variable \( u = (x-5)^2 \). Applying the ratio test to \( u \) gives convergence for \( |u| < 4 \). Translating back to \( x \), we get \( |x-5|^2 < 4 \implies |x-5| < 2 \). While the numerical answer \( R=2 \) is correct for \( x \), conceptualizing it purely as a standard series in \( (x-5) \) obscures the structure. Standard formulas for radius \( R = \lim |c_k/c_{k+1}| \) assume consecutive powers; here, coefficients for odd powers are zero, causing standard ratio limits on \( c_k \) to fail or oscillate. One must treat it as a series in \( (x-5)^2 \).

Q13. Which scenario best illustrates a series where the interval of convergence is a single point?

A. A series where coefficients grow super-exponentially, e.g., \( \sum k! x^k \) ✅
B. A series with alternating signs and decreasing coefficients
C. A geometric series with ratio \( r=1 \)
D. A Taylor series for \( e^x \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For \( \sum k! x^k \), the ratio test gives \( \lim |(k+1)! x^{k+1} / k! x^k| = \lim |(k+1)x| = \infty \) for any \( x \neq 0 \). Thus, the series diverges for all non-zero \( x \) and converges only at the center \( x=0 \). This represents the extreme case where \( R=0 \). Geometric series with \( r=1 \) diverge everywhere except trivially, and \( e^x \) converges everywhere. Alternating decreasing series typically have \( R \geq 1 \). Understanding \( R=0 \) cases is vital for recognizing the limitations of power series representations.

How should the 'effective' interval of convergence be interpreted in this applied context?

The correct interpretation distinguishes between analytical convergence (\( R=10 \)) and applicability (\( t \leq 8 \)). Blindly trusting the mathematical radius without considering domain-specific constraints is a common pitfall in applied calculus and engineering.

Q15. Given \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k x^k \) with radius \( R \), consider the integrated series \( \int_0^x (\sum c_k t^k) dt = \sum \frac{c_k}{k+1} x^{k+1} \). If the original series diverges at \( x=R \), what can be said about the integrated series at \( x=R \)?

A. It may converge or diverge; integration can improve convergence behavior at endpoints. ✅
B. It must also diverge because integration preserves the radius.
C. It must converge because dividing by \( k+1 \) makes terms smaller.
D. It converges only if the original series converged absolutely.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Integration increases the denominator by a factor of roughly \( k \), which can turn a divergent endpoint into a convergent one. For example, \( \sum x^k \) diverges at \( x=1 \), but its integral \( \sum x^{k+1}/(k+1) \) converges at \( x=1 \) (alternating harmonic-like behavior after substitution/sign adjustment). Conversely, if divergence is strong enough, integration might not fix it. Thus, unlike the interior where behavior is identical, endpoints require independent testing after integration. This highlights the asymmetry between differentiation (which worsens endpoint convergence) and integration (which can improve it).

Q16. A student analyzes \( \sum \frac{x^k}{k} \) and correctly identifies \( R=1 \). They then analyze \( \sum \frac{x^{2k}}{k} \) and claim \( R=1 \) because 'the coefficients are the same'. Why is this reasoning flawed despite getting the wrong numerical intuition?

C. The second series has radius \( \sqrt{1} = 1 \), so the answer is numerically correct but the reasoning ignores the squaring effect. ✅
D. The second series diverges everywhere.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

While the numerical radius happens to be 1 in this specific case (since \( \sqrt{1}=1 \)), the student's reasoning 'coefficients are the same' is dangerous. If the original radius were 4, the new radius would be \( \sqrt{4}=2 \), not 4. The presence of \( x^{2k} \) means the series behaves like \( \sum y^k/k \) with \( y=x^2 \). The convergence condition is on \( y \), which translates to a square root constraint on \( x \). Correct reasoning must account for the power transformation, not just coefficient similarity, to avoid errors in general cases.

Q19. A series \( \sum c_k (x-a)^k \) converges at \( x = a+R \) but diverges at \( x = a-R \). Which of the following must be true about the coefficients \( c_k \)?

A. They cannot be all positive, otherwise convergence at one endpoint would imply convergence at the other. ✅
B. They must alternate in sign strictly.
C. They must decrease monotonically to zero.
D. They must satisfy \( c_k = 0 \) for all even \( k \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

If all coefficients \( c_k \) were non-negative, then \( \sum c_k R^k \) and \( \sum c_k (-R)^k \) would involve terms with magnitudes \( |c_k R^k| \). Absolute convergence at \( x=a+R \) would imply \( \sum |c_k R^k| < \infty \), which would force absolute (and thus conditional) convergence at \( x=a-R \) as well. Since convergence behaviors differ at the two endpoints, the series cannot consist solely of positive terms; there must be sign variations or cancellations allowing conditional convergence at one end but not the other. This probes deep understanding of absolute vs. conditional convergence symmetry.

Even inside the interval of convergence, the rate of convergence is not uniform. Near the boundary \( R \), the terms \( c_k x^k \) decay much more slowly than near the center. Consequently, a fixed number of terms provides high accuracy centrally but poor accuracy near the edge. This is a fundamental property of power series approximation. Practical application requires adaptive truncation or alternative expansions near boundaries. Understanding this prevents misuse of series approximations in numerical computing where precision matters.

Q21. Consider the series \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (2 + (-1)^k) x^k \). The ratio test limit does not exist due to oscillation. How should one proceed to find the radius of convergence?

A. Use the Root Test or recognize the series as a sum of two geometric series with radii 1 and 1. ✅
B. Apply L'Hopital's rule to the ratio sequence.
C. Conclude the radius is undefined.
D. Average the upper and lower limits of the ratio sequence.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

When \( \lim |a_{k+1}/a_k| \) fails to exist, the Ratio Test is inapplicable. The Root Test uses \( \limsup \sqrt[k]{|a_k|} \), which handles oscillating coefficients robustly. Here, \( \sqrt[k]{2+(-1)^k} \to 1 \), giving \( R=1 \). Alternatively, decomposing into \( 2\sum x^k + \sum (-x)^k \) reveals two geometric series both with \( R=1 \); their sum shares this radius. This tests adaptability when standard tools fail and knowledge of alternative methods like limsup or series decomposition. Rigid adherence to the ratio test is a common limitation in student problem-solving.

Q22. A student finds the interval of convergence for \( \sum \frac{(x-3)^k}{k} \) is \( [2, 4) \). They then integrate the series to get \( \sum \frac{(x-3)^{k+1}}{k(k+1)} \). Without testing, they assume the new interval is also \( [2, 4) \). Is this assumption safe?

A. No, integration can add convergence at previously divergent endpoints, so the interval might become \( [2, 4] \). ✅
B. Yes, integration never changes endpoint behavior.
C. No, integration always removes convergence at endpoints.
D. Yes, because the radius remains 1.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Integration improves convergence properties. At \( x=4 \), the original series is harmonic (\( \sum 1/k \), divergent). The integrated series has terms \( \sim 1/k^2 \), which converges absolutely. Thus, the new interval likely includes \( x=4 \). At \( x=2 \), original is alternating harmonic (convergent); integrated is absolutely convergent. So the interval expands to \( [2, 4] \). Assuming endpoint behavior is invariant under integration is a critical error. Students must understand that calculus operations on series can alter boundary inclusion even when preserving the radius.

Horizontal asymptotes describe behavior at infinity and do not limit the local radius (e.g., \( \arctan x \) has horizontal asymptotes but finite \( R \) due to complex singularities, though visually on real line it looks smooth; however, vertical asymptotes are definitive real-line blockers). Local extrema and inflection points are features of smooth functions and do not impede convergence. Identifying visual singularities is key to predicting convergence domains from graphs.

Q24. You are given that \( \sum c_k x^k \) converges for \( x \in (-4, 4) \). You construct a new series \( \sum c_k x^{3k} \). What is the interval of convergence for this new series?

This transformation compresses the interval of convergence because higher powers of \( x \) grow faster, reaching the divergence threshold sooner. Students often mistakenly keep the original interval or apply square roots instead of cube roots. Correctly mapping variable substitutions to domain transformations is essential for handling composite power series.

Q25. Why is it insufficient to rely solely on the Ratio Test to determine the complete interval of convergence for any power series?

A. Because the Ratio Test only determines the open interval of absolute convergence and is silent on endpoint behavior. ✅
B. Because the Ratio Test only works for series with positive terms.
C. Because the Ratio Test cannot distinguish between conditional and absolute convergence inside the interval.
D. Because the Ratio Test is less accurate than the Root Test.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Ratio Test provides a limit \( L \). If \( L < 1 \), absolute convergence; if \( L > 1 \), divergence. But at the boundary where \( L=1 \), the test is mathematically inconclusive. Since the interval of convergence includes potential endpoint convergence (conditional or absolute), the Ratio Test alone cannot fully specify the interval. Endpoint analysis requires supplementary tests. This is a foundational definition recall question ensuring students understand the scope and limitations of their primary tool.

Q26. A series \( \sum a_k (x-c)^k \) has radius \( R \). If we multiply the series by \( (x-c)^m \) where \( m \) is a positive integer, what happens to the radius of convergence?

A. It remains exactly \( R \). ✅
B. It increases to \( R+m \).
C. It decreases to \( R/m \).
D. It becomes infinite.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Multiplying by \( (x-c)^m \) simply shifts the indices of the series: \( \sum a_k (x-c)^{k+m} = \sum b_j (x-c)^j \) where \( b_j = a_{j-m} \) for \( j \geq m \). The asymptotic behavior of the coefficients \( b_j \) is identical to \( a_k \) for large indices. Since radius depends on the tail behavior of coefficients, it remains unchanged. This tests understanding that radius is an asymptotic property invariant under finite shifts or polynomial multiplication, distinguishing it from operations like substitution that alter growth rates.

Q27. In modeling heat diffusion, a solution involves \( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} A_n e^{-\lambda_n t} x^n \). Treating this as a power series in \( x \) with time-dependent coefficients, how does increasing time \( t \) generally affect the radius of convergence, assuming \( \lambda_n > 0 \) and increases with \( n \)?

A. The radius increases because exponential decay suppresses high-order coefficients more strongly. ✅
B. The radius decreases because time evolution introduces instability.
C. The radius remains constant regardless of time.
D. The radius oscillates with time.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Coefficients are \( c_n(t) = A_n e^{-\lambda_n t} \). As \( t \) increases, \( e^{-\lambda_n t} \) decays faster for larger \( n \) (since \( \lambda_n \) increases). This rapid suppression of high-order terms improves convergence properties, effectively increasing the radius. Physically, diffusion smooths out gradients, making the solution more analytic over larger spatial domains as transients die out. This connects PDE behavior to series convergence, illustrating interdisciplinary application of radius concepts.

Q28. A student calculates \( R=5 \) for \( \sum c_k x^k \). They are asked to find the interval for \( \sum c_k (2x)^k \). They answer \( (-5, 5) \). What misconception drives this error?

A. Failing to recognize that substituting \( 2x \) scales the variable, requiring \( |2x| < 5 \implies |x| < 2.5 \).
B. Believing that coefficients determine radius exclusively, ignoring the variable scaling. ✅
C. Thinking the radius is a property of the function, not the series representation.
D. Assuming the new series diverges faster.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The radius is tied to the argument of the series. \( \sum c_k u^k \) converges for \( |u|<5 \). With \( u=2x \), we need \( |2x|<5 \). The student likely viewed \( R \) as an intrinsic constant attached to the coefficient sequence \( c_k \) independent of the variable form. This static view of radius ignores the functional composition. Correct thinking treats radius as a constraint on the entire term \( c_k(\text{variable})^k \). Identifying this specific misconception helps target instruction on variable substitution.

Q29. Which of the following series has an interval of convergence that is NOT symmetric about its center?

A. None; power series intervals are always symmetric about the center in the real domain. ✅
B. A series with only even powers.
C. A series with alternating signs.
D. A series centered at a non-zero value.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

By definition, a real power series \( \sum c_k (x-a)^k \) converges on an interval \( (a-R, a+R) \) possibly including endpoints. This interval is inherently symmetric around \( a \). Asymmetry can only arise in endpoint inclusion (e.g., \( [a-R, a+R) \)), but the open interval of absolute convergence is always symmetric. Options B, C, D describe series that still possess symmetric open intervals. This question tests the fundamental geometric definition of power series domains, dispelling notions of inherent asymmetry in convergence regions.

Q30. You have a series with radius \( R=2 \). You differentiate it twice. What is the radius of the resulting series, and what risk exists regarding the interval?

A. Radius remains 2, but the series may lose convergence at both endpoints. ✅
B. Radius becomes 4, improving convergence.
C. Radius remains 2, and endpoint behavior is preserved.
D. Radius becomes 1, restricting the domain.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Differentiation preserves the radius of convergence \( R \). However, each differentiation can potentially cause divergence at endpoints where the original series converged conditionally. After two derivatives, the risk of losing endpoint convergence is compounded. For example, \( \sum x^k/k^3 \) converges at \( x=1 \); first derivative \( \sum x^{k-1}/k^2 \) converges; second derivative \( \sum x^{k-2}/k \) diverges at \( x=1 \). Thus, while \( R \) is stable, the closed interval of convergence can shrink. This tests nuanced understanding of operational effects on series domains.

Q31. Consider \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k!} \) and \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k! x^k \). Comparing their radii reveals what fundamental principle about coefficient growth?

A. Factorial decay in denominator yields infinite radius; factorial growth in numerator yields zero radius. ✅
B. Both have infinite radius due to factorials.
C. Both have zero radius due to factorials.
D. Growth rate only matters for geometric series.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

These are canonical examples representing extremes. \( 1/k! \) decays super-exponentially, allowing convergence for all \( x \) (\( R=\infty \)). \( k! \) grows super-exponentially, overwhelming any \( x^k \) for \( x \neq 0 \) (\( R=0 \)). This contrast establishes the direct inverse relationship between coefficient growth rate and convergence radius. Understanding these benchmarks allows quick estimation of radii for intermediate cases. It reinforces that radius is fundamentally a measure of coefficient asymptotics.

Q32. A student uses the Root Test on \( \sum (\frac{k}{k+1})^{k^2} x^k \) and finds \( \lim \sqrt[k]{|a_k|} = 1/e \). They conclude \( R = 1/e \). What is the error?

A. The limit is \( 1/e \), so convergence requires \( |x|/e < 1 \implies |x| < e \). Thus \( R=e \), not \( 1/e \).
B. The Root Test cannot be used here.
C. The limit should be evaluated as \( e \), making \( R=1/e \).
D. The student calculated the limit correctly but inverted the radius formula. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The Root Test states convergence when \( \lim \sqrt[k]{|a_k x^k|} = L|x| < 1 \). Here \( L = 1/e \). So \( |x|/e < 1 \implies |x| < e \). The radius is the reciprocal of the limit \( L \). The student correctly computed the limit but failed to take the reciprocal to find \( R \). Confusing the limit value with the radius itself is a frequent algebraic slip. This question targets precise application of the convergence criterion formula.

Q33. In a computational algorithm, you need to evaluate \( \sum c_k x^k \) near the boundary \( x \approx R \). Why might rearranging terms or using Euler summation be necessary despite theoretical convergence?

A. Theoretical convergence guarantees a limit exists but not computational feasibility; slow convergence near boundary causes excessive truncation error. ✅
B. The series actually diverges numerically even if it converges analytically.
C. Computers cannot handle infinite sums near boundaries.
D. Rearrangement changes the sum to a more accurate value.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Near \( R \), terms decay slowly. Achieving machine precision might require millions of terms, introducing round-off errors and computation time. Acceleration techniques like Euler summation transform slowly convergent series into rapidly convergent ones without changing the limit. This bridges pure analysis (existence of sum) and numerical analysis (efficient computation). Recognizing that theoretical convergence ≠ practical computability is a higher-order insight for applied mathematics.

Q34. Given \( f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k x^k \) with \( R=3 \), and \( g(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} b_k x^k \) with \( R=5 \). If \( h(x) = f(x)g(x) \), what is the guaranteed radius of convergence for the Maclaurin series of \( h(x) \)?

A. At least 3 ✅
B. Exactly 3
C. Exactly 5
D. At least 5

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The product of two analytic functions is analytic wherever both are analytic. Both series converge absolutely for \( |x| < 3 \). Thus, their Cauchy product converges absolutely for \( |x| < 3 \). While \( h(x) \) might be analytically continuable beyond \( |x|=3 \) (if singularities of \( f \) are removable in the product), we are only guaranteed convergence on the intersection of the original disks. Hence, \( R_h \geq 3 \). This tests conservative reasoning about combined domains versus optimistic assumptions.

Q36. Why does the Taylor series for \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \) centered at 0 have radius \( R=1 \) despite the function being smooth and defined for all real \( x \)?

A. Complex singularities at \( x = \pm i \) are distance 1 from the origin, limiting real convergence. ✅
B. The function's derivatives grow too fast for real x > 1.
C. The series actually converges everywhere; R=1 is a misconception.
D. Real analysis cannot explain this; it requires Fourier analysis.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This is the classic counterexample showing real smoothness ≠ analyticity globally. The function has poles at \( \pm i \) in the complex plane. The radius of convergence of a real Taylor series is the distance to the nearest complex singularity. Distance from 0 to \( \pm i \) is 1. Thus \( R=1 \). Real-variable intuition fails here. Understanding this requires bridging real and complex analysis, a pinnacle concept in series theory explaining why some smooth functions have finite series domains.

Q37. A student claims that if \( \sum c_k x^k \) has radius \( R \), then \( \sum c_k x^{2k} \) has radius \( R^2 \). Evaluate this claim.

A. False; substituting \( u=x^2 \) gives convergence for \( |u|<R \), so \( |x|<\sqrt{R} \). The new radius is \( \sqrt{R} \). ✅
B. True; squaring the variable squares the radius.
C. False; the radius remains \( R \) because coefficients are unchanged.
D. False; the radius becomes \( 2R \) due to doubling powers.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The student confuses the transformation rule. Let \( u=x^2 \). Original series converges for \( |u|<R \). Substitute back: \( |x^2|<R \implies |x|<\sqrt{R} \). The radius transforms via the inverse of the power substitution. Claiming \( R^2 \) suggests misunderstanding functional composition. This error arises from misapplying algebraic rules to analytic domains. Correcting it reinforces the link between variable change and domain scaling.
