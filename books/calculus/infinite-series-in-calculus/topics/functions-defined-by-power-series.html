What is Functions defined by power series?

A power series \( \sum c_n (x-a)^n \) defines a function \( f(x) \) on its interval of convergence, and this function is infinitely differentiable inside that interval; examples include \( e^x \), \( \sin x \), and \( \ln(1+x) = \sum (-1)^{n+1} \frac{x^n}{n} \) for \( -1 < x \le 1 \).

Q1. A function \( f(x) \) is defined by a power series centered at \( x=0 \) with radius of convergence \( R=3 \). If \( g(x) = f'(x) \), which statement best describes the interval of convergence for \( g(x) \) compared to \( f(x) \)?

A. The interval of convergence for \( g(x) \) is always identical to that of \( f(x) \), including endpoints.
B. The radius of convergence for \( g(x) \) is 3, but convergence at the endpoints \( x=\pm 3 \) may differ from \( f(x) \). ✅
C. The radius of convergence for \( g(x) \) decreases to a value less than 3 because differentiation amplifies high-frequency terms.
D. The radius of convergence for \( g(x) \) increases beyond 3 because differentiation smooths out singularities.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Differentiation of a power series preserves the radius of convergence \( R \), meaning \( g(x) \) definitely converges on \( (-3, 3) \). However, behavior at the specific endpoints \( x = \pm R \) is not guaranteed to remain the same. A series might converge conditionally at an endpoint while its derivative diverges there due to the multiplication by \( n \) in the coefficient term. Therefore, one must re-test endpoints separately after differentiation.

Q2. Consider the function defined by \( f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} \). Without recognizing this as a known trigonometric function, how would you rigorously determine the value of \( f''(0) + f(0) \)?

A. Compute the first two derivatives term-by-term, evaluate at zero, and sum the results using the series definition. ✅
B. Recognize the series as cosine and immediately state the answer is zero based on trigonometric identities.
C. Integrate the series twice to find a relationship between \( f(x) \) and its derivatives, then solve the differential equation.
D. Use the ratio test to find the radius of convergence, which dictates the value of the function at the center.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

While recognizing the series as \( \cos x \) yields the answer quickly, the question requires using the properties of power series directly. Differentiating term-by-term gives \( f'(x) = \sum \frac{(-1)^k (2k) x^{2k-1}}{(2k)!} \) and \( f''(x) = \sum \frac{(-1)^k (2k)(2k-1) x^{2k-2}}{(2k)!} \). Evaluating at \( x=0 \), only the constant terms survive. For \( f(0) \), the \( k=0 \) term is 1. For \( f''(0) \), the \( k=1 \) term is -1. Summing them gives 0, demonstrating that operations on series are valid independent of closed-form identification.

Q3. A student attempts to find the Maclaurin series for \( h(x) = \ln(1+x^2) \) by integrating the geometric series for \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \). They obtain \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+1} \). What is the fundamental error in this derivation?

A. The student forgot to add the constant of integration, which is non-zero for logarithmic functions.
B. The student integrated the series for \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \) correctly but failed to recognize that the integral of \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \) is \( \arctan(x) \), not \( \ln(1+x^2) \).
C. The student should have differentiated \( \ln(1+x^2) \) first to get \( \frac{2x}{1+x^2} \), expanded that, and then integrated. ✅
D. The radius of convergence changes upon integration, making the resulting series invalid for the original function.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The student confused the antiderivative of \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \) with \( \ln(1+x^2) \); it is actually \( \arctan(x) \). To correctly find the series for \( \ln(1+x^2) \), one should differentiate to get \( \frac{2x}{1+x^2} = 2x \sum (-1)^k x^{2k} \). Integrating this correct expansion yields \( \sum (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k+2}}{k+1} \). This highlights the importance of verifying calculus operations before applying series manipulations. Simply matching forms without checking derivatives leads to plausible-looking but incorrect series representations.

Q4. Given the graph of a partial sum \( S_n(x) \) approximating a function \( f(x) \) defined by a power series, you observe that \( S_n(x) \) oscillates wildly near \( x=R \) but stabilizes quickly near \( x=0 \). What does this graphical behavior imply about the function's analytic structure?

A. The function has a singularity on the circle of convergence \( |x|=R \), limiting the domain of uniform convergence. ✅
B. The partial sums are calculated incorrectly; power series approximations should always be monotonic near the boundary.
C. The function is discontinuous at \( x=R \), causing the Gibbs phenomenon regardless of the number of terms.
D. The radius of convergence is actually smaller than \( R \), and the stabilization near zero is coincidental.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Power series converge uniformly on compact subsets strictly inside the radius of convergence. Wild oscillation or divergence of partial sums near the boundary \( x=R \) typically indicates the presence of a singularity (pole, branch point, or essential singularity) on the circle of convergence in the complex plane. Even if the function appears well-behaved on the real line at \( x=R \), complex singularities dictate the radius. The graph visually confirms the theoretical limit of the series representation's validity.

Q5. You are modeling a physical system where displacement \( y(t) \) satisfies \( y' = -ky \) with \( y(0)=A \). Instead of solving the ODE directly, you assume a power series solution \( y(t) = \sum c_n t^n \). How does this approach fundamentally differ from finding a Taylor series for a known function?

A. There is no difference; both methods require computing successive derivatives at \( t=0 \).
B. The coefficients are determined recursively by substituting the series into the differential equation rather than by differentiating a known closed form. ✅
C. This method only works if the solution is a polynomial; otherwise, the series will diverge.
D. The power series method assumes the solution is periodic, whereas the Taylor series method does not.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When the function is unknown, we cannot compute \( f^{(n)}(0) \) directly. Instead, we substitute \( \sum c_n t^n \) and \( \sum n c_n t^{n-1} \) into the ODE. Matching coefficients of like powers of \( t \) generates a recurrence relation (e.g., \( c_{n+1} = \frac{-k}{n+1}c_n \)). This constructs the series from the governing law itself. This distinction is crucial in physics where equations define functions (like Bessel functions) that have no elementary closed form, making the recursive series definition primary.

Q6. Let \( f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n x^n \) converge for \( |x|<1 \). If \( \lim_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n} = L \), and we define \( g(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n x^{2n} \), what is the radius of convergence for \( g(x) \)?

A. \( 1/L \)
B. \( \sqrt{1/L} \) ✅
C. \( 1/L^2 \)
D. \( 2/L \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Using the Root Test on \( g(x) \), we examine \( \lim |a_n x^{2n}|^{1/n} = \lim |a_n|^{1/n} |x|^2 = L|x|^2 \). For convergence, we require \( L|x|^2 < 1 \), which implies \( |x|^2 < 1/L \) or \( |x| < \sqrt{1/L} \). This demonstrates how variable substitution within a power series transforms the radius of convergence. Students often mistakenly apply the original radius \( 1/L \) without accounting for the exponent change. This problem tests deep understanding of the Root Test mechanism versus memorized formulas.

Q7. Which of the following best explains why the function \( f(x) = e^{-1/x^2} \) (with \( f(0)=0 \)) cannot be represented by its Maclaurin series in any neighborhood of zero, despite being infinitely differentiable?

A. The function is not continuous at \( x=0 \), violating the necessary condition for series representation.
B. All derivatives at zero are identically zero, so the Maclaurin series is the zero function, which does not equal \( f(x) \) for \( x \neq 0 \). ✅
C. The radius of convergence is zero because the derivatives grow too factorially fast.
D. The function has a vertical asymptote at \( x=0 \) that prevents polynomial approximation.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This is a classic counterexample in analysis. Although \( f(x) \) is smooth (\( C^\infty \)), \( f^{(n)}(0) = 0 \) for all \( n \). Thus, the Taylor series is \( \sum 0 \cdot x^n = 0 \). Since \( f(x) > 0 \) for \( x \neq 0 \), the series fails to represent the function anywhere except the center. This proves that infinite differentiability is necessary but not sufficient for a function to be analytic (representable by a power series). It distinguishes between smooth functions and analytic functions.

Q8. A researcher computes the first four non-zero terms of the Maclaurin series for \( \tan(x) \) by dividing the series for \( \sin(x) \) by \( \cos(x) \). Why is this algebraic division method often preferred over direct differentiation for higher-order terms?

A. Direct differentiation of \( \tan(x) \) produces increasingly complex products and chains, while series division uses systematic arithmetic on coefficients. ✅
B. Series division guarantees a larger radius of convergence than direct differentiation.
C. Direct differentiation introduces rounding errors that accumulate faster than algebraic division.
D. The quotient rule cannot be applied to infinite series, making division the only valid method.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Computing \( f^{(n)}(0) \) for \( \tan(x) \) directly involves messy higher-order derivatives of secants and tangents. Conversely, knowing \( \sin x = x - x^3/6 + \dots \) and \( \cos x = 1 - x^2/2 + \dots \), one can perform polynomial long division or equate coefficients in \( (\sum a_n x^n)(\cos x) = \sin x \). This reduces calculus problems to algebra, minimizing computational error and providing a scalable algorithm for generating terms. This efficiency makes series manipulation superior for constructing expansions of composite or quotient functions.

Q9. Suppose \( f(x) = \int_0^x e^{-t^2} dt \). You wish to approximate \( f(0.5) \) with error less than \( 10^{-4} \). After expanding the integrand and integrating term-by-term, you obtain an alternating series. How do you determine the minimum number of terms required?

A. Find \( n \) such that the absolute value of the \( (n+1) \)-th term in the integrated series is less than \( 10^{-4} \). ✅
B. Find \( n \) such that the remainder bound \( \frac{M}{(n+1)!}(0.5)^{n+1} < 10^{-4} \) using the Lagrange error formula.
C. Compute partial sums until two consecutive sums differ by less than \( 10^{-4} \).
D. Use the ratio test to ensure the series converges sufficiently fast at \( x=0.5 \).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since the integrated series for \( \int e^{-t^2} dt \) is alternating with decreasing magnitude terms for small \( x \), the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem applies. The error in approximating by the \( n \)-th partial sum is bounded strictly by the magnitude of the first neglected term \( |a_{n+1}| \). This is far simpler and sharper than the Lagrange remainder for this specific series type. Identifying the series structure (alternating vs. general) is a critical step in selecting the most efficient error estimation strategy.

Q10. If a power series \( \sum c_n (x-a)^n \) represents a function \( f(x) \) on an interval \( I \), and you integrate this series term-by-term to get \( F(x) \), which property is NOT automatically preserved?

A. The radius of convergence.
B. The continuity of the represented function within the open interval.
C. The differentiability of the represented function within the open interval.
D. The convergence behavior specifically at the endpoints of the interval. ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Term-by-term integration preserves the radius of convergence \( R \). Within \( (a-R, a+R) \), the new series represents a continuous, differentiable antiderivative. However, endpoint behavior can change dramatically. A series might diverge at an endpoint while its integral converges there (e.g., geometric series vs. log series). Integration tends to 'improve' convergence at boundaries because coefficients are divided by \( n \). Therefore, one cannot assume the interval of convergence remains identical; endpoints must always be re-evaluated after integration.

Q11. Consider the binomial series expansion for \( (1+x)^m \) where \( m \) is not a positive integer. Why does this series necessarily have a radius of convergence \( R=1 \), unlike the exponential series which has \( R=\infty \)?

A. The coefficients involve factorials in the denominator that grow slower than the numerator.
B. The function \( (1+x)^m \) has a singularity or branch point at \( x=-1 \), which limits the radius of convergence to the distance to the nearest singularity. ✅
C. The binomial theorem only applies to values between -1 and 1 by definition.
D. The ratio test always yields a limit of 1 for any algebraic function.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For non-integer \( m \), \( (1+x)^m \) is multi-valued or undefined for \( x \le -1 \) (depending on branch cuts). In complex analysis, the radius of convergence of a Taylor series centered at 0 is exactly the distance to the nearest singularity. Here, the singularity is at \( x=-1 \), so \( R=1 \). In contrast, \( e^x \) is entire (no singularities in the finite complex plane), giving \( R=\infty \). This connects real-variable series behavior to complex analytic structure, explaining why algebraic functions typically have finite radii.

Q12. A student claims that since \( \frac{1}{1-x} = \sum x^n \) for \( |x|<1 \), substituting \( x=2 \) proves that \( \sum 2^n = -1 \). Which analysis best refutes this claim using the concept of functions defined by power series?

A. The series diverges at \( x=2 \), so the equality holds only within the interval of convergence; outside, the series has no sum. ✅
B. The formula \( \frac{1}{1-x} \) is only valid for positive integers.
C. Analytic continuation allows the sum to be -1, so the student is technically correct in a generalized sense.
D. The student made an arithmetic error; the sum should be positive.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The identity \( f(x) = \sum c_n x^n \) is conditional on \( x \) being within the interval of convergence. At \( x=2 \), the series \( \sum 2^n \) diverges to infinity; it does not possess a sum in the standard sense. While analytic continuation extends the *function* \( 1/(1-x) \) to \( x=2 \), it does not extend the *series representation*. Confusing the function with its specific series representation outside the convergence disk is a fundamental misconception. The series ceases to represent the function beyond \( R=1 \).

Q13. You are given two power series \( A(x) = \sum a_n x^n \) and \( B(x) = \sum b_n x^n \) with radii of convergence \( R_A=2 \) and \( R_B=3 \). What can be definitively stated about the radius of convergence \( R \) of the product series \( A(x)B(x) \)?

A. \( R \) is exactly 2.
B. \( R \) is exactly 3.
C. \( R \ge 2 \). ✅
D. \( R \le 2 \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The product of two power series converges absolutely at least on the intersection of their individual intervals of convergence. Since \( A(x) \) converges for \( |x|<2 \) and \( B(x) \) for \( |x|<3 \), both converge absolutely for \( |x|<2 \). Thus, their Cauchy product converges for \( |x|<2 \), implying \( R \ge 2 \). It could be larger if singularities cancel out (e.g., \( (1-x) \times \frac{1}{1-x} = 1 \)), but it cannot be smaller than the minimum of the two radii. This tests understanding of lower bounds versus exact values.

Q14. In modeling heat transfer, a solution involves the series \( J_0(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{2^{2k}(k!)^2} \). If you need to evaluate \( \int_0^1 x J_0(x) dx \), which strategy exploits the power series definition most effectively?

A. Use numerical integration like Simpson's Rule since Bessel functions are non-elementary.
B. Substitute \( u=x^2 \) to simplify the argument before integrating.
C. Multiply the series by \( x \) and integrate term-by-term to obtain a rapidly converging numerical series. ✅
D. Look up the definite integral in a table of Bessel function identities.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Multiplying by \( x \) gives \( \sum \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2^{2k}(k!)^2} \). Integrating term-by-term from 0 to 1 yields \( \sum \frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+2) 2^{2k} (k!)^2} \). This transforms a difficult special-function integral into a simple alternating series of rational numbers that can be summed to arbitrary precision. This exemplifies the utility of power series: they convert transcendental operations into arithmetic ones. Direct lookup or numerical quadrature misses the opportunity to leverage the series structure for exact, controllable computation.

Q15. Why is it mathematically invalid to find the Maclaurin series for \( \sec(x) \) by simply taking the reciprocal of the coefficients of the Maclaurin series for \( \cos(x) \)?

A. Reciprocals of series coefficients do not correspond to the coefficients of the reciprocal function; series multiplication must yield 1. ✅
B. The Maclaurin series for \( \sec(x) \) does not exist because it is an even function.
C. \( \sec(x) \) has a radius of convergence of zero, making coefficient manipulation impossible.
D. Only odd functions can have their coefficients inverted directly.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

If \( \sec x = \sum s_n x^n \) and \( \cos x = \sum c_n x^n \), then \( (\sum s_n x^n)(\sum c_n x^n) = 1 \). This requires the Cauchy product of the coefficients to satisfy convolution identities (e.g., \( s_0 c_0 = 1, s_0 c_2 + s_2 c_0 = 0 \)), not simple element-wise inversion \( s_n = 1/c_n \). Assuming \( 1/\sum a_n x^n = \sum (1/a_n) x^n \) is a common algebraic fallacy. Correctly finding reciprocal series requires solving a system of linear equations derived from the product condition.

Q16. A function \( f(x) \) is defined by a power series with infinite radius of convergence. Which of the following properties must \( f(x) \) possess?

A. \( f(x) \) must be a polynomial.
B. \( f(x) \) must be bounded on the real line.
C. \( f(x) \) must be infinitely differentiable everywhere. ✅
D. \( f(x) \) must be periodic.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

A power series with \( R=\infty \) defines an entire function. A fundamental theorem states that functions represented by power series are infinitely differentiable within their interval of convergence. Since the interval is \( (-\infty, \infty) \), \( f(x) \) has derivatives of all orders everywhere. It need not be a polynomial (e.g., \( e^x \)), bounded (e.g., \( e^x \)), or periodic (e.g., \( e^x \)). This question tests the basic regularity property implied by power series representation.

Q17. When approximating \( \ln(1.1) \) using the Maclaurin series for \( \ln(1+x) \), why might one prefer to use the series for \( \ln(\frac{1+x}{1-x}) \) with a substituted value instead?

A. The standard series \( \ln(1+x) \) diverges at \( x=0.1 \).
B. The modified series converges much faster because the argument \( x \) becomes significantly smaller. ✅
C. The modified series avoids alternating signs, reducing round-off error.
D. The standard series requires more derivatives to compute.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For \( \ln(1.1) \), standard series uses \( x=0.1 \). Using \( \ln(\frac{1+y}{1-y}) = 2(y + y^3/3 + \dots) \), we set \( \frac{1+y}{1-y} = 1.1 \Rightarrow y = 1/21 \approx 0.0476 \). Since convergence rate depends on the magnitude of the argument, \( y \approx 0.05 \) yields accuracy much faster than \( x=0.1 \). This demonstrates practical numerical analysis: transforming the input to accelerate series convergence is often more efficient than simply adding more terms to a slowly converging expansion.

Q18. Suppose \( f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n \) satisfies \( f'(x) = 2x f(x) \) and \( f(0)=1 \). Without solving the ODE in closed form, what is the coefficient \( a_4 \)?

A. \( 1/2 \) ✅
B. \( 1/4 \)
C. \( 1/8 \)
D. \( 1/16 \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Substituting series into \( f' = 2xf \): \( \sum n a_n x^{n-1} = \sum 2 a_n x^{n+1} \). Shifting indices to match powers of \( x^k \): \( (k+1)a_{k+1} = 2a_{k-1} \) for \( k \ge 1 \). With \( a_0=1 \), we get \( a_1=0 \) (since \( f' \) has no constant term). Then \( 2a_2 = 2a_0 \Rightarrow a_2=1 \). \( 3a_3 = 2a_1 = 0 \). \( 4a_4 = 2a_2 = 2 \Rightarrow a_4 = 1/2 \). This recursive extraction of coefficients bypasses closed-form solutions and tests ability to manipulate series indices within differential equations.

Q19. Which graphical feature of a function \( f(x) \) would immediately suggest that its Maclaurin series has a finite radius of convergence \( R < \infty \)?

A. The function has a horizontal asymptote.
B. The function is symmetric about the y-axis.
C. The function has a vertical asymptote or cusp at some \( x=a \neq 0 \). ✅
D. The function oscillates with increasing frequency.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The radius of convergence of a Maclaurin series is determined by the distance from the center (0) to the nearest singularity in the complex plane. On a real graph, a vertical asymptote, cusp, or point of non-differentiability at \( x=a \) serves as a visible barrier. The series cannot converge beyond this point. Horizontal asymptotes or symmetry do not restrict \( R \) (e.g., \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \) has horizontal asymptote but finite \( R \) due to complex poles; \( e^{-x^2} \) has asymptote and infinite \( R \)). Visible real singularities are definitive indicators.

Q20. You are asked to verify if \( \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^n}{n!} \) represents \( e^x \) for all real \( x \). Which logical step is essential to complete this verification beyond just finding the series?

A. Checking that the series passes the ratio test.
B. Proving that the remainder term \( R_n(x) \) approaches zero as \( n \to \infty \) for all \( x \). ✅
C. Confirming that the derivatives at zero match \( e^x \).
D. Showing that the series converges absolutely.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Matching derivatives at a point generates the Taylor series, but does not guarantee it converges *to the function*. One must prove \( \lim_{n\to\infty} R_n(x) = 0 \). For \( e^x \), the Lagrange remainder involves \( e^c \frac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \). Since factorial growth dominates exponential growth for fixed \( x \), this limit is zero. Without this step, one has only a formal series, not a functional representation. This distinguishes between 'finding a series' and 'proving a representation,' a critical theoretical nuance.

Q21. In the context of functions defined by power series, what is the significance of the identity theorem (uniqueness of power series)?

A. It guarantees that every function has exactly one power series representation.
B. It ensures that if two power series agree on an interval containing the center, their coefficients must be identical. ✅
C. It proves that power series can be differentiated term-by-term.
D. It establishes that the radius of convergence is unique.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Identity Theorem states that if \( \sum a_n (x-c)^n = \sum b_n (x-c)^n \) for all \( x \) in some neighborhood of \( c \), then \( a_n = b_n \) for all \( n \). This justifies methods like undetermined coefficients and series substitution. If we derive a series for \( f(x) \) via any valid method (integration, multiplication, etc.), it *must* be the Taylor series. This uniqueness underpins the consistency of all series manipulation techniques; there is no ambiguity in the representation.

Q22. A physics model yields \( y(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n \). Experimental data shows \( y(x) \) behaves like \( 1/x \) as \( x \to \infty \). Can \( y(x) \) be represented by a single Maclaurin series valid for all \( x > 0 \)?

A. Yes, because Maclaurin series can represent any continuous function.
B. No, because \( 1/x \) behavior at infinity implies a singularity at \( x=0 \) or finite radius preventing global validity. ✅
C. Yes, provided enough terms are included in the partial sum.
D. No, because Maclaurin series only represent polynomials.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Maclaurin series are power series centered at 0. If \( y(x) \sim 1/x \) at infinity, it suggests algebraic decay. More critically, functions with Maclaurin series are analytic at 0. If the function actually behaves like \( 1/x \) globally or has a pole at 0, no Maclaurin series exists. Even if regular at 0, asymptotic behavior at infinity often correlates with finite radius of convergence (singularities in complex plane). A single power series centered at 0 cannot capture behavior across an infinite domain if singularities exist. Global representation requires other tools (asymptotic expansions, Padé approximants).

Q23. When multiplying two power series \( \sum a_n x^n \) and \( \sum b_n x^n \), the coefficient of \( x^n \) in the product is given by the Cauchy product formula. Why is this convolution structure necessary rather than simple term-wise multiplication?

A. Because powers of \( x \) combine additively during multiplication; \( x^k \cdot x^m = x^{k+m} \). ✅
B. Because series multiplication is defined differently from polynomial multiplication.
C. To ensure the resulting series converges faster than the originals.
D. To maintain the alternating sign pattern of the factors.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

When expanding \( (\sum a_k x^k)(\sum b_m x^m) \), the term \( x^n \) arises from all pairs \( (k,m) \) such that \( k+m=n \). Thus, the coefficient is \( \sum_{k=0}^n a_k b_{n-k} \). Simple term-wise multiplication \( a_n b_n x^n \) ignores cross-terms like \( a_1 x \cdot b_{n-1} x^{n-1} \). Understanding this combinatorial structure is essential for correctly computing products like \( e^x \sin x \) or solving ODEs with variable coefficients. It reflects the algebraic structure of the ring of formal power series.

Q24. Consider the function \( f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2} \). Its Maclaurin series has radius \( R=1 \), yet the function is smooth and bounded for all real \( x \). What explains this apparent paradox?

A. The Maclaurin series is incorrect; it should converge everywhere.
B. The radius is limited by complex singularities at \( x = \pm i \), invisible on the real graph. ✅
C. Real functions do not always have convergent Taylor series.
D. The series converges, but to a different function outside \( (-1,1) \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This is the canonical example illustrating that real smoothness does not imply global analyticity. The function \( 1/(1+x^2) \) has poles at \( x = \pm i \) in the complex plane. The radius of convergence of a Taylor series centered at 0 is the distance to the nearest complex singularity, which is \( |i-0|=1 \). Despite perfect behavior on \( \mathbb{R} \), the complex structure constrains the real series. This emphasizes that power series convergence is inherently a complex-analytic property, not merely a real-variable one.

Q25. You approximate \( \sin(0.1) \) using the third-degree Maclaurin polynomial \( P_3(x) = x - x^3/6 \). To justify the error bound, which theorem provides the tightest estimate specifically because the series is alternating?

A. Taylor's Remainder Theorem with Lagrange form.
B. The Alternating Series Estimation Theorem. ✅
C. The Mean Value Theorem.
D. The Integral Test for Remainders.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While Lagrange remainder works generally, for alternating series with decreasing terms, the error is strictly bounded by the first omitted term \( |a_{n+1}| \). For \( \sin(0.1) \), this is \( (0.1)^5/120 \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-8} \). Lagrange would require bounding \( |\cos(c)| \) or \( |\sin(c)| \), yielding a similar but less direct bound. Recognizing the alternating structure simplifies error analysis significantly. This question tests strategic selection of error estimation tools based on series characteristics rather than defaulting to the most general theorem.

Q26. A student derives a series for \( \arctan(x) \) by integrating \( 1/(1+x^2) \). They correctly get \( \sum (-1)^n x^{2n+1}/(2n+1) \) for \( |x|<1 \). They then claim this series equals \( \pi/4 \) at \( x=1 \). Is this claim valid?

A. No, because the geometric series for \( 1/(1+x^2) \) diverges at \( x=1 \).
B. Yes, by Abel's Theorem, since the series converges at \( x=1 \) and \( \arctan(x) \) is continuous there. ✅
C. No, because integration changes the radius of convergence to exclude endpoints.
D. Yes, but only because \( \arctan(1) \) is rational.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Although the derivative series \( \sum (-1)^n x^{2n} \) diverges at \( x=1 \), the integrated series \( \sum (-1)^n/(2n+1) \) converges conditionally by AST. Abel's Theorem guarantees that if a power series converges at an endpoint and the function is continuous there, the series sum equals the function value. Thus, \( \sum (-1)^n/(2n+1) = \arctan(1) = \pi/4 \). This subtle interplay between differentiation, integration, and endpoint continuity is central to rigorous series analysis.

Q27. In solving \( y'' + xy = 0 \) via power series, you obtain a recurrence relating \( a_{n+2} \) to \( a_n \). Why does this typically result in two linearly independent solutions?

A. Because the recurrence splits into separate relations for even and odd indexed coefficients.
B. Because second-order ODEs always have trigonometric solutions.
C. Because the initial conditions \( y(0) \) and \( y'(0) \) can be chosen arbitrarily, corresponding to \( a_0 \) and \( a_1 \).
D. Both A and C are correct explanations. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The recurrence \( a_{n+2} = f(n) a_n \) links terms with step 2. This decouples the sequence into even terms (determined by \( a_0 \)) and odd terms (determined by \( a_1 \)). Since \( a_0 = y(0) \) and \( a_1 = y'(0) \) are arbitrary initial conditions, the general solution is \( y(x) = a_0 y_{even}(x) + a_1 y_{odd}(x) \). This structural splitting naturally generates the two-dimensional solution space required for second-order linear ODEs. Understanding this link between recurrence parity and solution independence is key to the series method.

Q28. Which modification to the standard geometric series \( \sum x^n \) would produce a series representing \( \frac{x}{(1-x)^3} \)?

A. Differentiate twice and multiply by \( x^2/2 \). ✅
B. Integrate twice and divide by \( x \).
C. Differentiate once and multiply by \( x \).
D. Square the original series.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Start with \( \sum x^n = (1-x)^{-1} \). Differentiate: \( \sum n x^{n-1} = (1-x)^{-2} \). Multiply by \( x \): \( \sum n x^n = x(1-x)^{-2} \). Differentiate again: \( \sum n^2 x^{n-1} = \frac{d}{dx}[x(1-x)^{-2}] = (1-x)^{-2} + 2x(1-x)^{-3} \). This path gets complicated. Better: Differentiate \( (1-x)^{-1} \) twice to get \( 2(1-x)^{-3} = \sum n(n-1)x^{n-2} \). Multiply by \( x^2/2 \): \( x^2(1-x)^{-3} = \sum \frac{n(n-1)}{2} x^n \). Adjusting indices/algebra yields the target. Systematic operator application (differentiate/multiply) generates rational function series efficiently.

Q29. If \( f(x) = \sum a_n x^n \) has radius \( R \), what is the radius of convergence for the series representing \( f(x^2) \)?

A. \( R \)
B. \( R^2 \)
C. \( \sqrt{R} \) ✅
D. \( 2R \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Substituting \( x^2 \) for \( x \) means the new series converges when \( |x^2| < R \), i.e., \( |x| < \sqrt{R} \). This is a direct consequence of the definition of radius of convergence. Students sometimes confuse this with differentiation/integration (which preserve \( R \)) or coefficient scaling. Variable transformation fundamentally alters the domain of convergence. This basic fact is essential for composing functions and understanding how series domains map under nonlinear transformations.

Q30. A numerical analyst observes that truncating the Maclaurin series for \( e^x \) at \( n=10 \) gives excellent accuracy for \( x=1 \) but catastrophic error for \( x=-20 \), despite the series converging for all \( x \). What causes this discrepancy?

A. The series diverges for negative \( x \).
B. Round-off error and cancellation effects dominate for large negative arguments when using finite precision arithmetic. ✅
C. The remainder term grows exponentially for negative \( x \).
D. Maclaurin series are only valid for \( x>0 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Theoretically, the series converges for all \( x \). Practically, for \( x=-20 \), terms grow to \( \approx 20^{20}/20! \approx 4 \times 10^7 \) before decaying. Summing huge alternating terms in finite precision causes massive loss of significance (catastrophic cancellation). The true value \( e^{-20} \approx 2 \times 10^{-9} \) is lost in rounding noise. This illustrates that theoretical convergence does not guarantee numerical stability. For large negative arguments, one should compute \( e^{20} \) and take the reciprocal, avoiding alternating large terms.

Q31. Why can't the function \( f(x) = |x| \) be represented by a Maclaurin series on any interval containing zero?

A. It is not continuous at zero.
B. It is not differentiable at zero, and power series functions are infinitely differentiable. ✅
C. Its derivatives grow too fast.
D. It is an even function.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A fundamental property of functions defined by power series is that they are infinitely differentiable (\( C^\infty \)) within their interval of convergence. Since \( |x| \) lacks a first derivative at \( x=0 \), it cannot equal any power series centered at 0. This provides a quick diagnostic: any lack of smoothness at the center immediately precludes power series representation. This contrasts with Fourier series, which can represent non-smooth functions, highlighting the restrictive regularity of power series.

Q32. You are given \( f(x) = \int_0^x \sin(t^2) dt \). To find \( f^{(5)}(0) \), which approach is most efficient?

A. Differentiate \( f(x) \) five times using Leibniz rule.
B. Expand \( \sin(t^2) \) as a series, integrate term-by-term, and read off the coefficient of \( x^5 \). ✅
C. Use L'Hôpital's rule repeatedly on the difference quotient.
D. Numerically approximate the fifth derivative using finite differences.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \( f(x) = \sum a_n x^n \), we know \( a_n = f^{(n)}(0)/n! \). Expanding \( \sin(t^2) = t^2 - t^6/6 + \dots \), integrating gives \( f(x) = x^3/3 - x^7/42 + \dots \). The coefficient of \( x^5 \) is zero, so \( f^{(5)}(0) = 0 \). Direct differentiation of an integral with composite argument is tedious. Series conversion turns differentiation into coefficient inspection. This showcases the power of series as a computational tool for extracting local derivative information without symbolic calculus.

Q33. In the context of modeling, why might a truncated power series be preferred over a closed-form expression involving special functions like Bessel or Hypergeometric functions?

A. Closed-form expressions are always less accurate.
B. Truncated series provide transparent, computable polynomial approximations suitable for embedded systems and insight into local behavior. ✅
C. Special functions cannot be evaluated numerically.
D. Power series always converge faster than special function evaluations.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While special functions are exact, they are often opaque 'black boxes'. A truncated power series \( \sum_{n=0}^N a_n x^n \) is a simple polynomial: easy to differentiate, integrate, optimize, and implement in hardware. For small arguments, it captures dominant physics clearly (e.g., linear vs. quadratic regimes). It trades global exactness for local transparency and computational simplicity. This pragmatic choice drives much of applied mathematics, where interpretability and efficiency often outweigh formal closed-form elegance.

Q34. Suppose \( \sum a_n x^n \) converges at \( x=R \). Does \( \sum n a_n x^{n-1} \) necessarily converge at \( x=R \)?

A. Yes, differentiation preserves convergence at endpoints.
B. No, differentiation can destroy conditional convergence at the boundary. ✅
C. Yes, because \( n a_n \to 0 \) if \( a_n \to 0 \).
D. No, because the radius of convergence decreases.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Differentiation preserves radius \( R \) but not necessarily endpoint convergence. Example: \( \sum (-1)^n x^n / n^2 \) converges absolutely at \( x=1 \). Its derivative \( \sum (-1)^n x^{n-1} / n \) converges conditionally at \( x=1 \). But consider \( \sum (-1)^n x^n / n \); converges conditionally at \( x=1 \). Derivative \( \sum (-1)^n x^{n-1} \) diverges at \( x=1 \). Multiplication by \( n \) amplifies terms, potentially breaking conditional convergence. Endpoint behavior must always be re-tested after differentiation.

Q35. A student writes \( e^{x^2} = (e^x)^2 = (\sum x^n/n!)^2 \). They then square each coefficient to get \( \sum x^{2n}/(n!)^2 \). What is the error?

A. Squaring a series requires the Cauchy product, not squaring individual coefficients.
B. \( e^{x^2} \) is not equal to \( (e^x)^2 \).
C. The exponent should be distributed: \( e^{x^2} = \sum x^{2n}/n! \).
D. Both B and C describe valid corrections depending on interpretation. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

First, \( e^{x^2} \neq (e^x)^2 = e^{2x} \). These are different functions. Second, even if squaring \( e^x \), \( (\sum a_n)^2 \neq \sum a_n^2 \); one needs Cauchy product. Third, the correct series for \( e^{x^2} \) comes from substituting \( x^2 \) into \( e^u \): \( \sum (x^2)^n/n! = \sum x^{2n}/n! \). The student committed multiple conceptual errors: confusing function composition with algebraic operations and misunderstanding series arithmetic. Identifying all layers of error demonstrates comprehensive mastery.
