What is Power series in x formula?

A power series in \( x \) is an infinite series of the form \( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n x^n = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + \cdots \), where \( c_n \) are coefficients; it converges for \( |x| < R \) (radius of convergence) and may converge at endpoints depending on the series.

Q1. A power series \( \sum c_k x^k \) is known to converge at \( x = 4 \) and diverge at \( x = -7 \). Based solely on this information, which of the following statements must be true regarding the radius of convergence \( R \)?

A. The radius of convergence is exactly \( R = 5.5 \).
B. The radius of convergence satisfies \( 4 \leq R < 7 \). ✅
C. The radius of convergence satisfies \( 4 \leq R \leq 7 \).
D. The series converges absolutely for all \( x \) in the interval \( (-4, 4) \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The radius of convergence \( R \) defines a symmetric interval \( (-R, R) \) where absolute convergence occurs. Since the series converges at \( x=4 \), we know \( R \geq 4 \). Since it diverges at \( x=-7 \), we know \( R \) cannot be greater than or equal to 7 (otherwise it would converge absolutely at -7). Thus, \( 4 \leq R < 7 \). Option A assumes the endpoints are exactly the convergence boundaries, which is not guaranteed. Option C includes 7, which contradicts the divergence at -7.

Q2. Consider the function defined by the power series \( f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k^2} \). If one were to differentiate this series term-by-term to find \( f'(x) \), how does the interval of convergence for \( f'(x) \) compare to that of \( f(x) \)?

A. They are identical, including behavior at both endpoints.
B. They share the same radius, but \( f'(x) \) may diverge at an endpoint where \( f(x) \) converges. ✅
C. The radius of convergence for \( f'(x) \) is strictly smaller than that of \( f(x) \).
D. The radius of convergence for \( f'(x) \) is strictly larger because differentiation smooths out singularities.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Differentiation of a power series preserves the radius of convergence \( R \), but endpoint behavior can change. For \( f(x) \), the series converges at both \( x=1 \) (p-series p=2) and \( x=-1 \) (alternating p-series). The derivative is \( \sum x^{k-1}/k \). At \( x=1 \), this becomes the harmonic series which diverges. At \( x=-1 \), it is the alternating harmonic series which converges. Thus, the interval shrinks from \( [-1, 1] \) to \( [-1, 1) \). This illustrates that while \( R \) is invariant, endpoint inclusion is not.

Q3. A student attempts to find the Maclaurin series for \( g(x) = \ln(1+x^2) \) by integrating the series for \( \frac{2x}{1+x^2} \). They obtain \( \sum (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k+2}}{k+1} \). However, they forgot to evaluate the constant of integration. Why is the resulting series still technically correct for representing \( \ln(1+x^2) \) specifically?

A. Because the constant of integration is always zero for logarithmic functions.
B. Because \( \ln(1+0^2) = 0 \) and the series evaluates to 0 at \( x=0 \), forcing \( C=0 \). ✅
C. Because integration of power series automatically accounts for initial conditions.
D. The student's result is actually incorrect; there should be a non-zero constant added.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When finding a specific function via integration of a series, \( \int f'(x) dx = f(x) + C \). To determine \( C \), one must evaluate at the center of expansion (usually \( x=0 \) for Maclaurin). Since \( \ln(1+0^2) = \ln(1) = 0 \) and the derived power series has no constant term (all terms have powers of \( x \)), substituting \( x=0 \) yields \( 0 = 0 + C \), implying \( C=0 \). This highlights the necessity of checking initial values when reconstructing functions from derivatives via series.

Q4. Given the graph of the partial sums \( S_n(x) \) for a power series centered at 0, where \( S_2(x) \), \( S_4(x) \), and \( S_6(x) \) are plotted. The graphs show that for \( |x| < 1 \), the even partial sums are decreasing and bounded below, while for \( |x| > 1 \), they oscillate wildly with increasing amplitude. What can be deduced about the series?

A. The series represents a function with a vertical asymptote at \( x=1 \).
B. The radius of convergence is \( R=1 \), and the series likely diverges at \( x=1 \). ✅
C. The series converges uniformly on the entire real line.
D. The function represented is discontinuous at \( x=0 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Graphical analysis of partial sums provides visual evidence of convergence. Inside the radius of convergence, partial sums settle toward a limit curve. Outside, they diverge. The transition point where behavior shifts from stable approximation to unbounded oscillation indicates the boundary of the interval of convergence. Here, the shift at \( |x|=1 \) implies \( R=1 \). The wild oscillation at the boundary suggests divergence rather than conditional convergence, as conditional convergence typically shows damped oscillation settling slowly, whereas 'wildly with increasing amplitude' indicates the terms do not approach zero.

Q5. Suppose you have two power series \( A(x) = \sum a_k x^k \) with radius \( R_A = 3 \) and \( B(x) = \sum b_k x^k \) with radius \( R_B = 5 \). You form a new series \( H(x) = A(x) + B(x) \). A colleague claims the radius of convergence for \( H(x) \) must be 5 because it is the larger of the two radii. Evaluate this claim.

A. The claim is correct; the sum inherits the larger domain of validity.
B. The claim is incorrect; the radius is definitely 3.
C. The claim is potentially incorrect; the radius is at least 3, but could be larger if leading terms cancel. ✅
D. The claim is incorrect; the radius is the average of 3 and 5.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The radius of convergence of a sum is at least the minimum of the individual radii, i.e., \( R_H \geq \min(R_A, R_B) = 3 \). Within \( |x|<3 \), both converge, so the sum converges. For \( 3 < |x| < 5 \), \( B(x) \) converges but \( A(x) \) diverges, making the sum diverge. Thus, usually \( R_H = 3 \). However, if \( A(x) \) and \( B(x) \) are constructed such that their singularities at \( |x|=3 \) cancel each other out perfectly (e.g., \( A(x) = \frac{1}{3-x} \) and \( B(x) = -\frac{1}{3-x} + \frac{1}{5-x} \)), the singularity at 3 vanishes, potentially extending \( R_H \) to 5. Therefore, stating it 'must' be 3 or 5 without analyzing coefficients is flawed.

Q6. In modeling a physical system, a function \( y(t) \) is approximated by a power series \( \sum c_k t^k \). Experimental data suggests the model breaks down catastrophically at \( t = 2 \) seconds, despite the mathematical series having a calculated radius of convergence \( R = 4 \). Which explanation best reconciles the mathematical theory with the physical observation?

A. The calculation of \( R=4 \) must be erroneous; recalculate using the ratio test.
B. Physical systems cannot be modeled by power series beyond \( t=1 \).
C. The series may converge mathematically at \( t=2 \), but the higher-order terms become numerically dominant due to roundoff error before convergence sets in. ✅
D. The function \( y(t) \) has a complex singularity at \( t=2 \) that limits the physical validity regardless of real-axis convergence.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This addresses the distinction between theoretical convergence and numerical utility. Even if a series converges at a point within its radius, the rate of convergence might be extremely slow, or intermediate terms might grow very large before decaying. In computational modeling, finite precision arithmetic means that if terms grow large (e.g., \( 10^{16} \)) before cancelling out to a small sum, significant digits are lost, rendering the result useless. This 'numerical breakdown' often precedes the theoretical radius of convergence, especially near singularities or in ill-conditioned expansions.

Q7. Analyze the following reasoning: 'To find the Taylor series for \( f(x) = e^{-x^2} \) about \( x=0 \), I computed \( f(0), f'(0), f''(0) \) directly. Since \( f'(x) = -2xe^{-x^2} \), \( f'(0)=0 \). Continuing this, all odd derivatives are zero. Thus, the series contains only even powers.' Identify the primary inefficiency or potential pitfall in this direct approach compared to substitution.

A. There is no pitfall; direct computation is always superior for verifying coefficients.
B. Computing high-order derivatives of composite functions like \( e^{-x^2} \) becomes algebraically unmanageable quickly, whereas substituting \( -x^2 \) into the known series for \( e^u \) is immediate. ✅
C. Substitution is invalid here because \( -x^2 \) is not a linear transformation.
D. Direct computation fails to capture the factorial denominators correctly.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While direct differentiation works theoretically, the product rule complexity explodes for \( e^{-x^2} \). By the fourth or sixth derivative, the expression involves polynomials multiplied by the exponential, making evaluation at zero prone to algebraic errors. Substitution leverages the known structure of \( e^u = \sum u^k/k! \). Replacing \( u \) with \( -x^2 \) instantly yields \( \sum (-1)^k x^{2k}/k! \). This question tests the strategic selection of methods. The distractor about substitution validity is false; composition with analytic functions preserves analyticity within the domain.

Q8. Consider the power series representation of \( \arctan(x) \) obtained by integrating the geometric series for \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \). The resulting series is \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+1} \). Why is it valid to assert that this series equals \( \arctan(x) \) at the endpoint \( x=1 \), even though the original geometric series diverges at \( x=1 \)?

A. Because the integrated series converges absolutely at \( x=1 \).
B. Because Abel’s Theorem guarantees continuity at the endpoint if the integrated series converges there. ✅
C. Because the derivative of the series at \( x=1 \) matches the derivative of \( \arctan(x) \).
D. It is not valid; the series only represents \( \arctan(x) \) on \( (-1, 1) \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The geometric series \( \sum (-x^2)^k \) converges only for \( |x|<1 \). Integrating term-by-term gives the arctan series, which converges conditionally at \( x=1 \) (alternating harmonic-like). While the original series diverges at the boundary, the integrated series defines a continuous function on \( [0, 1] \). Abel's Theorem states that if a power series converges at an endpoint of its interval of convergence, the function it represents is continuous at that endpoint from within the interval. Since \( \arctan(x) \) is also continuous at \( x=1 \), the equality holds. This connects integration, convergence, and continuity.

Q9. You are given a power series \( \sum c_k x^k \) where \( c_k = \frac{k!}{k^k} \). Without using the Ratio Test, determine the radius of convergence by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of the coefficients using Stirling’s approximation concepts.

A. \( R = 0 \)
B. \( R = 1/e \)
C. \( R = e \) ✅
D. \( R = \infty \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Using the Root Test concept implicitly related to coefficient growth: \( \lim |c_k|^{1/k} \). Applying Stirling’s approximation \( k! \approx \sqrt{2\pi k}(k/e)^k \), we get \( c_k \approx \sqrt{2\pi k} (k/e)^k / k^k = \sqrt{2\pi k} e^{-k} \). Taking the k-th root: \( (\sqrt{2\pi k})^{1/k} (e^{-k})^{1/k} \to 1 \cdot e^{-1} = 1/e \). The radius is the reciprocal of this limit, so \( R = e \). This requires connecting coefficient asymptotics to convergence radii without relying solely on mechanical ratio test application, testing deep understanding of factorial growth rates versus exponential bases.

Q10. A student calculates the Maclaurin series for \( f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x} \) as \( \sum (-1)^k x^k \). They then attempt to find the series for \( g(x) = \frac{1}{(1+x)^2} \) by squaring the series for \( f(x) \) term-by-term (i.e., squaring each coefficient). Explain why this procedure is fundamentally flawed.

A. Squaring a series requires the Cauchy product (convolution), not term-wise squaring. ✅
B. The series for \( f(x) \) does not converge, so operations are invalid.
C. Term-wise squaring is only valid for odd functions.
D. The correct operation is differentiating \( f(x) \), not squaring it.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

A common misconception is treating power series multiplication like vector component-wise multiplication. If \( A(x) = \sum a_n x^n \), then \( A(x)^2 \neq \sum a_n^2 x^n \). Instead, \( A(x)^2 = \sum c_n x^n \) where \( c_n = \sum_{j=0}^n a_j a_{n-j} \). For \( \frac{1}{1+x} \), term-wise squaring gives \( \sum x^k \) (since \( (-1)^{2k}=1 \)), which equals \( \frac{1}{1-x} \), clearly wrong. The correct method is either Cauchy product or recognizing \( \frac{1}{(1+x)^2} = -\frac{d}{dx}(\frac{1}{1+x}) \). This tests understanding of series algebra versus scalar algebra.

Q11. Suppose a function \( f(x) \) is defined by a power series centered at \( x=2 \) with radius of convergence \( R=3 \). If we re-center the series at \( x=0 \) to create a Maclaurin series for the same function, what can be definitively stated about the new radius of convergence \( R_{new} \)?

A. \( R_{new} \) must be exactly 3.
B. \( R_{new} \) must be at least 1, but could be larger. ✅
C. \( R_{new} \) must be exactly 5.
D. \( R_{new} \) depends entirely on the specific coefficients and cannot be determined from \( R \) alone.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The original series converges on \( (2-3, 2+3) = (-1, 5) \). The function \( f(x) \) is analytic on this interval. A Maclaurin series (centered at 0) will converge on the largest symmetric interval \( (-R_{new}, R_{new}) \) contained within the domain of analyticity. Since \( f \) is analytic on \( (-1, 5) \), it is certainly analytic on \( (-1, 1) \). Thus \( R_{new} \geq 1 \). However, if the nearest singularity to 0 is actually at \( x=-1 \), then \( R_{new}=1 \). If the singularity at -1 was removable or the function extends analytically beyond it, \( R_{new} \) could be larger. We cannot assume \( R_{new}=R \) because the center shifted relative to the singularities.

Q12. In error analysis for approximating \( \sin(x) \) using its Maclaurin polynomial \( P_n(x) \), a student uses the Lagrange Error Bound. They argue that since \( |f^{(n+1)}(z)| \leq 1 \) for all \( z \), the error is always bounded by \( \frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \). However, for alternating series like sine, a tighter bound exists. When is the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem preferable to the Lagrange Error Bound?

A. Never; Lagrange is always more precise.
B. When the series satisfies alternating series criteria and \( x \) is within the convergence interval, as it avoids estimating the unknown \( z \). ✅
C. Only when \( n \) is even.
D. When calculating derivatives is computationally expensive.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Lagrange Error Bound requires finding a maximum \( M \) for the \( (n+1) \)-th derivative on an interval containing \( x \) and the center. While \( |\sin^{(n+1)}(z)| \leq 1 \) is a valid global bound, it can be overly conservative for small \( x \) or specific intervals. The Alternating Series Estimation Theorem states the error is bounded by the magnitude of the first neglected term, \( |a_{n+1}| \), provided the series alternates and terms decrease. This bound is exact for the worst-case scenario of the remainder's sign and requires no knowledge of intermediate points \( z \). It is often sharper and simpler for standard trigonometric/exponential series.

Q13. Consider the binomial series expansion for \( (1+x)^m \) where \( m \) is not a positive integer. The series converges for \( |x|<1 \). Analyze the behavior at the endpoint \( x=1 \). Under what condition does the series converge absolutely at \( x=1 \)?

A. For all real \( m \).
B. Only when \( m > 0 \). ✅
C. Only when \( m > -1 \).
D. Never; the binomial series always diverges at endpoints for non-integer \( m \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The convergence of the binomial series at endpoints depends critically on \( m \). At \( x=1 \), the series behaves asymptotically like \( \sum \frac{1}{k^{m+1}} \) (derived via Gamma function properties or Gauss’s test). For absolute convergence, we need the exponent \( m+1 > 1 \), implying \( m > 0 \). If \( -1 < m \leq 0 \), it converges conditionally. If \( m \leq -1 \), it diverges. This nuanced endpoint behavior distinguishes the binomial series from simpler geometric or exponential series and requires understanding asymptotic analysis of generalized binomial coefficients.

Q14. A researcher models population growth using a power series solution to a differential equation. The series solution \( P(t) = \sum a_k t^k \) has a radius of convergence determined by the nearest singularity in the complex plane. If the biological population crashes (goes to infinity) at \( t=5 \), but the series coefficients suggest \( R=10 \), what is the most likely mathematical explanation?

A. The population crash is not a mathematical singularity; the model is simply invalid biologically beyond \( t=5 \). ✅
B. The singularity at \( t=5 \) is removable in the complex extension of the function.
C. The coefficients were calculated incorrectly.
D. The radius of convergence is determined by real-axis behavior only.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Mathematical models often have domains of validity restricted by physical constraints rather than analytical ones. A power series solution to a DE might be analytic everywhere (entire function, \( R=\infty \)) or have singularities far from the region of interest. However, the physical quantity being modeled (population) might blow up or become negative due to model limitations (e.g., logistic vs exponential assumptions) before any mathematical singularity is reached. The radius of convergence describes where the *series* sums to the *function*, but the *function* itself might cease to represent reality. This distinguishes mathematical existence from physical applicability.

Q15. You are asked to approximate \( \int_0^{0.5} e^{-x^2} dx \) to four decimal places. You decide to use the Maclaurin series for \( e^{-x^2} \). After integrating term-by-term, you obtain an alternating series. How do you rigorously determine the number of terms needed without trial-and-error?

A. Set the general term \( \frac{(0.5)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)n!} < 0.00005 \) and solve for \( n \). ✅
B. Use the Lagrange Error Bound on the integral directly.
C. Integrate until the calculator display stops changing.
D. Use the Ratio Test on the integrated series to find \( n \).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since the integrated series \( \sum (-1)^n \frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)n!} \) evaluated at \( x=0.5 \) is an alternating series with decreasing terms, the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem applies. The error after \( N \) terms is less than the absolute value of the \( (N+1) \)-th term. To guarantee 4 decimal place accuracy (error \( < 0.5 \times 10^{-4} \)), one solves the inequality involving the next term. This is a direct application of error bounds for numerical integration via series, superior to trial-and-error or inappropriate tests like the Ratio Test (which determines convergence, not partial sum error).

Q16. Which of the following functions cannot be represented by a Maclaurin series on any open interval containing 0, despite being continuous at 0?

A. \( f(x) = |x| \) ✅
B. \( f(x) = e^{-1/x^2} \) for \( x \neq 0 \), \( f(0)=0 \)
C. \( f(x) = x \sin(1/x) \) for \( x \neq 0 \), \( f(0)=0 \)
D. \( f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x} \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

A necessary condition for a function to be represented by a power series (analytic) at a point is that it must be infinitely differentiable at that point. \( |x| \) is continuous at 0 but not differentiable there (corner). Thus, no Maclaurin series exists. Note: \( e^{-1/x^2} \) IS infinitely differentiable and HAS a Maclaurin series (all zeros), but the series does not converge TO the function (it's not analytic). The question asks what *cannot be represented*, implying the series representation itself doesn't exist or fails to represent the function. \( |x| \) fails the basic differentiability prerequisite. \( \sqrt[3]{x} \) has infinite derivative at 0, also failing. Between A and D, A is the classic example of non-differentiability preventing series formation.

Q17. Given the power series identity \( \frac{1}{1-x} = \sum x^k \), a student derives \( \frac{1}{(1-x)^3} \) by differentiating twice. They obtain \( \sum k(k-1)x^{k-2} \). Upon checking at \( x=0 \), the left side is 1, but the right side appears undefined or zero depending on indexing. What is the critical error in their manipulation?

A. Differentiation increases the power of the denominator, not decreases it.
B. They failed to adjust the summation index after differentiation, losing the constant factors. ✅
C. The formula for the second derivative of a geometric series involves factorials that were omitted.
D. There is no error; the series is correct but starts at \( k=2 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When differentiating \( \sum_{k=0}^\infty x^k \), the first derivative is \( \sum_{k=1}^\infty k x^{k-1} \). The second derivative is \( \sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1) x^{k-2} \). At \( x=0 \), only the \( k=2 \) term survives: \( 2(1)(0)^0 = 2 \). But \( \frac{d^2}{dx^2}(1-x)^{-1} = 2(1-x)^{-3} \). At \( x=0 \), this is 2. So \( \frac{1}{(1-x)^3} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)x^{k-2} \). The student missed the factor of \( 1/2! \) arising from the chain rule/derivative scaling. Proper re-indexing to \( n=k-2 \) yields \( \sum \frac{(n+2)(n+1)}{2} x^n \), which correctly gives 1 at \( x=0 \).

Q18. Compare the efficiency of computing \( \cos(0.1) \) using its Maclaurin series versus using the identity \( \cos(x) = 1 - 2\sin^2(x/2) \) combined with the sine series. Assuming both achieve the same accuracy, which statement best characterizes the computational trade-off?

A. The direct cosine series is always superior because it has fewer terms.
B. The sine-based identity reduces the argument size, accelerating convergence despite extra operations. ✅
C. Both methods require identical numbers of multiplications.
D. The sine-based identity introduces catastrophic cancellation errors.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For small \( x \), \( \cos(x) \approx 1 - x^2/2 \). Direct series converges rapidly. Using \( 1 - 2\sin^2(x/2) \) involves computing \( \sin(0.05) \), which converges even faster due to the smaller argument (error scales with \( x^{2n+1} \)). Although there are extra arithmetic operations (squaring, multiplying by 2, subtracting from 1), the reduction in required series terms for high precision can outweigh this. However, for very small \( x \), \( 1 - \text{small} \) risks loss of significance (catastrophic cancellation). Thus, while theoretically faster in convergence, numerically it requires care. Option B captures the convergence benefit, which is the primary analytical advantage.

Q19. A power series \( \sum c_k x^k \) has radius of convergence \( R=2 \). Define a new series \( \sum c_k x^{2k} \). What is the set of \( x \) values for which this new series converges?

A. \( (-2, 2) \)
B. \( (-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}) \) ✅
C. \( (-4, 4) \)
D. \( (-1, 1) \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Let \( y = x^2 \). The series becomes \( \sum c_k y^k \), which converges for \( |y| < 2 \). Substituting back, \( |x^2| < 2 \implies x^2 < 2 \implies |x| < \sqrt{2} \). Thus the interval is \( (-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}) \). This tests understanding of variable substitution in power series. Students often mistakenly think replacing \( x \) with \( x^2 \) squares the radius or leaves it unchanged. The correct transformation involves solving the inequality imposed by the original radius on the new variable expression.

Q20. In the context of generating functions, the sequence \( a_n = n^2 \) has generating function \( A(x) = \sum n^2 x^n \). To find a closed form, one operates on \( \sum x^n = \frac{1}{1-x} \). Which sequence of operations correctly yields \( A(x) \)?

B. Differentiate twice, multiply by \( x^2 \).

Q21. Consider the function \( f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k!} \). A student claims that since the ratio test gives limit 0 for all \( x \), the series converges uniformly on \( (-\infty, \infty) \). Is this claim correct?

A. Yes, infinite radius implies uniform convergence everywhere.
B. No, uniform convergence holds only on compact subsets, not the entire real line. ✅
C. Yes, because the terms decay factorially.
D. No, the series diverges for large \( x \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Pointwise convergence on \( \mathbb{R} \) does not imply uniform convergence on \( \mathbb{R} \). For \( e^x \), the remainder \( R_n(x) \) grows without bound as \( x \to \infty \) for any fixed \( n \). Uniform convergence requires \( \sup_{x \in D} |R_n(x)| \to 0 \). On \( \mathbb{R} \), this supremum is infinite. However, on any bounded interval \( [-M, M] \), the Weierstrass M-test confirms uniform convergence. This distinction is crucial in analysis: power series are uniformly convergent on compact subsets of their interval of convergence, but not necessarily on the entire open interval if it is unbounded.

Q22. You are analyzing the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k x^k}{k} \). You recognize this as \( -\ln(1+x) \). If you integrate this series term-by-term from 0 to 1, you get \( \sum \frac{(-1)^k}{k(k+1)} \). Does this resulting numerical series converge to \( \int_0^1 -\ln(1+x) dx \)?

A. No, because the original series only converges conditionally at \( x=1 \).
B. Yes, because the integrated series converges absolutely and the function is continuous. ✅
C. No, integration requires absolute convergence of the original series.
D. Yes, but only if interpreted as an improper integral.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Although \( \sum \frac{(-1)^k x^k}{k} \) converges only conditionally at \( x=1 \), term-by-term integration over \( [0,1] \) is valid. The resulting series \( \sum \frac{(-1)^k}{k(k+1)} \) converges absolutely (terms ~ \( 1/k^2 \)). More importantly, since the original series converges at the endpoint \( x=1 \) and represents a continuous function \( -\ln(1+x) \) on \( [0,1] \), the integral of the series equals the series of the integrals. This is a consequence of Abel's theorem and properties of uniformly convergent sequences on compact sets where endpoint convergence holds.

Q23. A physics problem yields the series \( S = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \binom{2n}{n} x^n \). Using the Ratio Test, one finds \( R=1/4 \). At the boundary \( x=1/4 \), the terms behave asymptotically as \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \). Based on this asymptotic behavior, what is the convergence status at \( x=1/4 \)?

A. Converges absolutely.
B. Converges conditionally.
C. Diverges. ✅
D. Oscillates finitely.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

At \( x=1/4 \), the general term is \( \binom{2n}{n} (1/4)^n \). Using Stirling’s approximation, \( \binom{2n}{n} \sim \frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \). Multiplying by \( (1/4)^n \) gives terms asymptotic to \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}} \). This is a p-series with \( p=1/2 \). Since \( p \leq 1 \), the series diverges. This problem requires combining radius of convergence calculation with asymptotic analysis of binomial coefficients at the boundary, going beyond standard textbook examples where endpoints are usually simple geometric or alternating harmonic series.

Q25. In numerical analysis, evaluating \( \frac{e^x - 1}{x} \) near \( x=0 \) causes division by zero errors in floating-point arithmetic. How does the power series representation resolve this issue computationally?

A. It doesn't; series evaluation is slower and less accurate.
B. By replacing the expression with \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{(k+1)!} \), which is well-defined and stable at \( x=0 \). ✅
C. By using L'Hopital's rule inside the computer code.
D. By shifting the center of expansion to \( x=1 \).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Direct evaluation suffers from catastrophic cancellation (\( e^x \approx 1 \)) and division by small numbers. The series \( \frac{e^x-1}{x} = \frac{(1+x+x^2/2!+\dots)-1}{x} = 1 + \frac{x}{2!} + \frac{x^2}{3!} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{x^k}{(k+1)!} \) removes the singularity analytically. At \( x=0 \), it evaluates exactly to 1. For small \( x \), it avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers. This demonstrates the practical utility of power series in algorithm design for robust numerical computation near singular points.

Q27. A student tries to find the Taylor series for \( \tan(x) \) at \( x=0 \) by dividing the series for \( \sin(x) \) by \( \cos(x) \). Is this worry justified?

A. Yes, higher-order terms in the divisor affect lower-order terms in the quotient.
B. No, the coefficient of \( x^n \) in the quotient depends only on terms up to degree \( n \) in the dividend and divisor. ✅
C. Yes, division of series is never exact.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Therefore, truncating the calculation after obtaining the desired degree is perfectly valid and exact for those coefficients. This property makes series division a practical tool for finding initial terms without needing infinite precision.

Q30. A power series \( \sum c_k x^k \) has coefficients satisfying \( c_{k+1}/c_k \to L \) as \( k \to \infty \). If \( L=0 \), what is the radius of convergence? Additionally, if \( c_k = 1/k! \), verify this limit.

Q35. In quantum mechanics, perturbation theory often yields asymptotic series rather than convergent power series. If a series \( \sum a_n \lambda^n \) has zero radius of convergence but provides accurate physical predictions for small \( \lambda \), how should one interpret the partial sums?

A. They are meaningless mathematically and should be discarded.
B. They approximate the true value up to an optimal order, after which error increases. ✅
C. They converge to the true value in the limit \( n \to \infty \).
D. They represent a different physical system entirely.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Asymptotic series diverge for any fixed \( \lambda \neq 0 \) as \( n \to \infty \). However, for small \( \lambda \), terms initially decrease, reach a minimum, then increase. Truncating at the smallest term yields an approximation with error proportional to that term (often exponentially small). Adding more terms worsens accuracy. This contrasts sharply with convergent series where more terms always improve accuracy. Understanding asymptotic vs. convergent series is vital in advanced applied mathematics and physics, where formal power series often lack convergence but retain utility.
