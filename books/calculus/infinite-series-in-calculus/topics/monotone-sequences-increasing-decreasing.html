What is Monotone sequences increasing decreasing?

A sequence is monotone if it is either increasing (each term is ≤ the next, an​≤an+1​) or decreasing (each term is ≥ the next, an​≥an+1​); for example, an​=n is increasing, while an​=n1​ is decreasing, and monotone sequences are easier to analyze for convergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Monotonicity of a sequence an​=f(n) cannot be determined solely by inspecting the numerator or denominator in isolation. Even if the numerator grows quadratically, the denominator also increases, potentially causing the overall fraction to decrease initially before increasing. A rigorous analysis requires examining an+1​−an​ or f&#039;(x) for the complete function. The quadratic numerator has a minimum at n=5, but the rational function’s behavior is more complex due to the linear denominator.

This avoids pitfalls of asymptotic approximations and provides a self-contained algebraic proof suitable for Olympiad-level reasoning.

Q6. Suppose {an​} is defined recursively by a1​=2, an+1​=2+an​​. A student argues the sequence is increasing because \sqrt{2+x} &gt; x for all x &gt; 0 . What is the error in this justification?

D.The base case a1​=2 makes the sequence constant.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The claim that \sqrt{2+x} &gt; x for all x &gt; 0 is false; solving \sqrt{2+x} &gt; x gives x &lt; 2 (since squaring yields x^2 - x - 2 &lt; 0 ). At x=2, equality holds, and for x &gt; 2 , the inequality reverses. Since a1​=2, we get a2​=4​=2, so the sequence is actually constant, not increasing. The student’s universal quantifier was incorrect, highlighting the danger of unverified functional inequalities in recursive definitions.

Q10. Suppose f(x) is differentiable for x≥1 and f&#039;(x) &gt; 0 for all x &gt; 1 , but f&#039;(n) = 0 for every positive integer n. Can the sequence an​=f(n) be strictly increasing?

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Even though f&#039;(n) = 0 at integers, the integral of f&#039; over [n, n+1] remains positive since f&#039; &gt; 0 on (n, n+1). By the Fundamental Theorem, a_{n+1} - a_n = \int_n^{n+1} f&#039;(x) dx &gt; 0 , so the sequence is strictly increasing. Derivative values at isolated points don’t control discrete differences; only the integral matters. This counters the misconception that vanishing derivative at sample points precludes sequence growth, emphasizing the distinction between pointwise and aggregate behavior.

Q23. Which statement correctly distinguishes monotonicity from boundedness in convergence criteria?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The Monotone Convergence Theorem states that a monotone sequence converges if and only if it is bounded. In contrast, boundedness alone doesn’t ensure convergence (e.g., (−1)n). This clarifies why both hypotheses are necessary in MCT and prevents conflating necessary vs sufficient conditions.
