Q1. A student claims that the sequence defined by \( a_n = \frac{n^2 - 10n + 25}{n+1} \) is strictly increasing for all \( n \geq 1 \) because the numerator is a quadratic with a positive leading coefficient. Which of the following best identifies the flaw in this reasoning?

🔹 A. The student failed to check the derivative of the corresponding function.
🔹 B. The student ignored that monotonicity depends on the entire expression, not just the numerator's shape.
🔹 C. The student should have used the ratio test instead of analyzing the numerator.
🔹 D. The sequence is actually strictly decreasing, so the student’s conclusion is backwards.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Monotonicity of a sequence \( a_n = f(n) \) cannot be determined solely by inspecting the numerator or denominator in isolation. Even if the numerator grows quadratically, the denominator also increases, potentially causing the overall fraction to decrease initially before increasing. A rigorous analysis requires examining \( a_{n+1} - a_n \) or \( f'(x) \) for the complete function. The quadratic numerator has a minimum at \( n=5 \), but the rational function’s behavior is more complex due to the linear denominator.

Q2. Consider the sequence \( a_n = \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \). Without computing numerical values, which method most rigorously establishes its monotonicity for all \( n \geq 1 \)?

🔹 A. Compute \( a_{n+1}/a_n \) and apply Bernoulli’s inequality to show it exceeds 1.
🔹 B. Take the natural log and differentiate \( f(x) = x \ln(1 + 1/x) \) to show \( f'(x) > 0 \).
🔹 C. Use induction to prove \( a_{n+1} > a_n \) directly from the binomial expansion.
🔹 D. Apply the AM-GM inequality to the \( n+1 \) terms in the product form of \( a_{n+1} \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

While multiple methods can work, the AM-GM approach elegantly proves strict increase without calculus or advanced inequalities. Applying AM-GM to the \( n+1 \) numbers \( 1, 1+\frac{1}{n}, \dots, 1+\frac{1}{n} \) (with one 1 and n copies of \( 1+1/n \)) yields \( a_{n+1}^{1/(n+1)} > a_n^{1/n} \), implying \( a_{n+1} > a_n \). This avoids pitfalls of asymptotic approximations and provides a self-contained algebraic proof suitable for Olympiad-level reasoning.

Q3. A population model generates a sequence \( P_n \) where \( P_{n+1} = rP_n(1 - P_n/K) \) with \( r = 2.5 \) and \( K = 1000 \). If \( P_1 = 800 \), what can be concluded about the long-term monotonicity of \( \{P_n\} \)?

🔹 A. The sequence is eventually strictly decreasing toward equilibrium.
🔹 B. The sequence is eventually strictly increasing toward equilibrium.
🔹 C. The sequence oscillates indefinitely and is never eventually monotone.
🔹 D. The sequence converges monotonically after finitely many terms.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

For logistic maps with \( r > 2 \), the fixed point becomes unstable and periodic or chaotic behavior emerges. At \( r = 2.5 \), the system exhibits stable 2-cycle oscillations around the equilibrium \( K(1 - 1/r) = 600 \). Starting at \( P_1 = 800 \), the sequence will alternate above and below 600 without settling into monotonic behavior. Thus, it is never eventually monotone, illustrating how nonlinear recurrence relations can violate intuitive expectations from continuous models.

Q4. Given the graph of a differentiable function \( f(x) \) that is positive, decreasing, and concave up for \( x \geq 1 \), and defining \( a_n = f(n) \), which statement about \( \{a_n\} \) must be true?

🔹 A. \( \{a_n\} \) is strictly decreasing and the differences \( a_n - a_{n+1} \) are strictly decreasing.
🔹 B. \( \{a_n\} \) is strictly decreasing and the differences \( a_n - a_{n+1} \) are strictly increasing.
🔹 C. \( \{a_n\} \) is strictly increasing and concave up.
🔹 D. \( \{a_n\} \) is strictly decreasing but nothing can be said about the differences.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since \( f \) is decreasing, \( a_n > a_{n+1} \), so the sequence is strictly decreasing. Concavity up means \( f' \) is increasing (less negative), so the slope becomes less steep. Thus, the drop \( f(n) - f(n+1) \approx -f'(c) \) for \( c \in (n, n+1) \) decreases as \( n \) increases because \( f' \) is rising toward zero. Therefore, the successive differences \( a_n - a_{n+1} \) themselves form a strictly decreasing positive sequence, reflecting diminishing decrements.

Q5. Which of the following sequences is monotone but NOT eventually strictly monotone?

🔹 A. \( a_n = (-1)^n / n \)
🔹 B. \( a_n = 1 + 1/n \) for odd \( n \), \( a_n = 1 \) for even \( n \)
🔹 C. \( a_n = n / (n+1) \)
🔹 D. \( a_n = \sin(n)/n \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A sequence is eventually strictly monotone only if, after some index, every consecutive pair satisfies strict inequality. Option B equals 1 at every even index and exceeds 1 at odd indices, so \( a_{2k} = a_{2k+2} = 1 \), violating strictness infinitely often. Yet it is nonincreasing overall since odd terms exceed 1 and even terms equal 1, making it monotone (nonincreasing) but never strictly so beyond any point. Other options either fail monotonicity entirely or become strictly monotone.

Q6. Suppose \( \{a_n\} \) is defined recursively by \( a_1 = 2 \), \( a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \). A student argues the sequence is increasing because \( \sqrt{2+x} > x \) for all \( x > 0 \). What is the error in this justification?

🔹 A. The inequality \( \sqrt{2+x} > x \) does not hold for all \( x > 0 \); it fails when \( x > 2 \).
🔹 B. The student should have checked boundedness first.
🔹 C. The recursion guarantees convergence, not monotonicity.
🔹 D. The base case \( a_1 = 2 \) makes the sequence constant.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The claim that \( \sqrt{2+x} > x \) for all \( x > 0 \) is false; solving \( \sqrt{2+x} > x \) gives \( x < 2 \) (since squaring yields \( x^2 - x - 2 < 0 \)). At \( x = 2 \), equality holds, and for \( x > 2 \), the inequality reverses. Since \( a_1 = 2 \), we get \( a_2 = \sqrt{4} = 2 \), so the sequence is actually constant, not increasing. The student’s universal quantifier was incorrect, highlighting the danger of unverified functional inequalities in recursive definitions.

Q7. Let \( a_n = \int_0^1 x^n e^x \, dx \). Without evaluating the integral, determine the monotonicity of \( \{a_n\} \).

🔹 A. Strictly increasing because \( e^x > 0 \).
🔹 B. Strictly decreasing because \( x^{n+1} < x^n \) on \( (0,1) \).
🔹 C. Not monotone due to oscillation of \( e^x \).
🔹 D. Constant because the interval is fixed.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

On the open interval \( (0,1) \), we have \( 0 < x < 1 \), so \( x^{n+1} < x^n \). Multiplying both sides by the positive function \( e^x \) preserves the inequality: \( x^{n+1}e^x < x^n e^x \). Integrating over [0,1] maintains strict inequality since the integrands differ on a set of positive measure. Hence \( a_{n+1} < a_n \) for all \( n \), proving strict decrease. This application leverages pointwise dominance and integral monotonicity without computation.

Q8. If a sequence \( \{a_n\} \) satisfies \( a_{n+2} > a_n \) for all \( n \geq 1 \), which conclusion is necessarily valid?

🔹 A. The sequence is strictly increasing.
🔹 B. The subsequences \( \{a_{2k}\} \) and \( \{a_{2k-1}\} \) are each strictly increasing.

🔹 D. The sequence is eventually monotone.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The condition \( a_{n+2} > a_n \) implies that every other term increases, so both the even-indexed and odd-indexed subsequences are strictly increasing. However, the full sequence need not be monotone; for example, \( a_n = n + (-1)^n \) satisfies \( a_{n+2} > a_n \) but alternates in local behavior. It may still converge if bounded, or diverge, but no global monotonicity or divergence is guaranteed. Only the subsequence structure is enforced by the given recurrence inequality.

Q9. Which scenario best illustrates a sequence that is bounded and monotone yet whose limit is difficult to find analytically?

🔹 A. \( a_n = 1 - 1/n \)
🔹 B. \( a_n = \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k^2 \)

The sequence \( a_{n+1} = \cos(a_n) \) with \( a_1 = 1 \) is bounded in [0,1] and eventually monotone (it alternates then settles), converging to the unique fixed point of \( \cos x = x \). However, this Dottie number has no closed-form expression in elementary functions, unlike the other options which converge to known constants (1, \( \pi^2/6 \), e). This exemplifies how monotone convergence guarantees existence without providing computability, a key conceptual distinction in analysis.

Q10. Suppose \( f(x) \) is differentiable for \( x \geq 1 \) and \( f'(x) > 0 \) for all \( x > 1 \), but \( f'(n) = 0 \) for every positive integer \( n \). Can the sequence \( a_n = f(n) \) be strictly increasing?

🔹 A. Yes, because \( f'(x) > 0 \) almost everywhere ensures growth.
🔹 B. No, because \( a_{n+1} - a_n = \int_n^{n+1} f'(x) dx > 0 \) despite isolated zeros.
🔹 C. No, because \( f'(n) = 0 \) implies local maxima at integers.
🔹 D. Yes, but only if \( f''(n) > 0 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Even though \( f'(n) = 0 \) at integers, the integral of \( f' \) over [n, n+1] remains positive since \( f' > 0 \) on (n, n+1). By the Fundamental Theorem, \( a_{n+1} - a_n = \int_n^{n+1} f'(x) dx > 0 \), so the sequence is strictly increasing. Derivative values at isolated points don’t control discrete differences; only the integral matters. This counters the misconception that vanishing derivative at sample points precludes sequence growth, emphasizing the distinction between pointwise and aggregate behavior.

Q11. In modeling compound interest with variable rates, suppose account balance follows \( B_{n+1} = B_n (1 + r_n) \) where \( r_n = 0.05 + 0.01 \sin(n) \). Is \( \{B_n\} \) monotone?

🔹 A. Yes, strictly increasing because \( r_n > 0 \) always.

🔹 C. Yes, nondecreasing but not strictly.
🔹 D. Cannot determine without initial balance.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since \( |\sin(n)| \leq 1 \), we have \( r_n \geq 0.05 - 0.01 = 0.04 > 0 \) for all n. Thus \( 1 + r_n > 1 \), and with positive initial balance \( B_1 > 0 \), each multiplication increases the balance: \( B_{n+1} > B_n \). Despite rate fluctuations, positivity of \( r_n \) ensures strict monotonic increase. This applies financial intuition within mathematical rigor, showing that bounded oscillation in growth rate doesn’t violate monotonicity if the rate stays positive.

The sequence \( a_n = 1 + \sin(\ln n)/n \) is positive, and \( a_{n+1}/a_n \to 1 \) since the perturbation decays. However, \( \sin(\ln n) \) oscillates infinitely often with decreasing amplitude but non-monotonic envelope, causing \( a_n \) to increase and decrease indefinitely. Q13. Given two sequences where \( \{a_n\} \) is increasing and \( \{b_n\} \) is decreasing, with \( a_n < b_n \) for all n, what can be definitively concluded?

🔹 A. Both sequences converge.
🔹 B. \( \{a_n\} \) converges and \( \{b_n\} \) diverges.
🔹 C. \( \{b_n - a_n\} \) is decreasing and nonnegative.
🔹 D. \( \{a_n + b_n\} \) is monotone.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Define \( c_n = b_n - a_n \). Since \( b_n \) decreases and \( a_n \) increases, \( c_{n+1} = b_{n+1} - a_{n+1} < b_n - a_n = c_n \), so \( \{c_n\} \) is strictly decreasing. Also \( c_n > 0 \) by hypothesis. Thus \( \{c_n\} \) is a positive decreasing sequence. Convergence of individual sequences isn’t guaranteed without boundedness, and sum/difference monotonicity doesn’t extend to \( a_n + b_n \). Only the gap’s monotonicity follows directly from the given conditions.

Q14. When applying the derivative test to \( a_n = f(n) \), a student finds \( f'(x) > 0 \) for \( x > 10 \) but \( f'(x) < 0 \) for \( 1 < x < 10 \). They conclude \( \{a_n\} \) is eventually strictly increasing. Is this valid?

🔹 A. Yes, because behavior for \( x > 10 \) determines eventual monotonicity.
🔹 B. No, because \( f'(x) \) could be negative at some integers beyond 10.
🔹 C. Yes, provided \( f \) is continuous.
🔹 D. No, because the sequence might oscillate near x=10.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Eventual monotonicity depends only on tail behavior. Since \( f'(x) > 0 \) for all \( x > 10 \), then for all integers \( n \geq 11 \), \( f \) is increasing on [n, n+1], so \( a_{n+1} = f(n+1) > f(n) = a_n \). Thus \( \{a_n\} \) is strictly increasing for \( n \geq 11 \), satisfying eventual strict increase. Early behavior (n < 11) is irrelevant to eventual properties. The continuity ensures no pathological jumps, but even without it, positivity of derivative on an interval implies increase on that interval.

Q15. Which modification to the harmonic series partial sums \( H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k \) produces a sequence that is both bounded and monotone?

🔹 A. \( H_n - \ln n \)
🔹 B. \( H_n / n \)

The sequence \( a_n = H_n - \ln n \) is well-known to be strictly decreasing and bounded below (converging to Euler’s constant γ). Monotonicity follows from integral comparison: \( \int_n^{n+1} 1/x dx < 1/n \), implying \( \ln(n+1) - \ln n < 1/n \), so \( a_{n+1} < a_n \). Boundedness comes from \( H_n < 1 + \ln n \). Other options either diverge, oscillate, or fail monotonicity. This connects classic results to monotone-bounded principles.

Q16. A recursive sequence satisfies \( a_{n+1} = a_n + \frac{1}{n^2}(1 - a_n) \) with \( a_1 = 0 \). Without solving, prove it is strictly increasing and bounded above by 1.

🔹 A. By induction: assume \( 0 \leq a_n < 1 \), then \( a_{n+1} - a_n > 0 \) and \( a_{n+1} < 1 \).
🔹 B. Because \( 1/n^2 \) is summable, the sequence converges absolutely.
🔹 C. The function \( f(x) = x + (1-x)/n^2 \) has derivative < 1.
🔹 D. It is a contraction mapping on [0,1].

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Base case: \( a_1 = 0 \in [0,1) \). Assume \( 0 \leq a_n < 1 \). Then \( 1 - a_n > 0 \), so \( a_{n+1} - a_n = (1 - a_n)/n^2 > 0 \), proving strict increase. Also \( a_{n+1} = a_n + (1 - a_n)/n^2 < a_n + (1 - a_n) = 1 \), so bounded above by 1. Induction completes the proof. This demonstrates how recursive structure with positive feedback toward a bound enforces monotonicity and boundedness simultaneously, a common technique in dynamical systems.

Q17. If \( \{a_n\} \) is monotone and the subsequence \( \{a_{n_k}\} \) with \( n_k = k^2 \) converges to L, what follows about \( \{a_n\} \)?

🔹 A. It converges to L.
🔹 B. It converges only if it is bounded.
🔹 C. It may diverge if monotonicity direction opposes subsequence limit.
🔹 D. Nothing can be concluded without knowing if it’s increasing or decreasing.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For a monotone sequence, convergence of any subsequence implies convergence of the whole sequence to the same limit. If increasing and a subsequence → L, then all terms beyond some point are ≤ L (if subseq terms approach L from below) or ≥ L, but monotonicity forces squeezing. Specifically, if increasing and \( a_{k^2} \to L \), then for any n, choose k with \( k^2 > n \), so \( a_n \leq a_{k^2} \to L \), and since increasing, \( a_n \) bounded above by L, hence converges to sup ≤ L; but subsequence hits arbitrarily close to L, so sup = L. Similar for decreasing. Thus full sequence → L.

Q21. Let \( a_n = \frac{\ln n}{n} \). For which n is the sequence strictly decreasing?

🔹 A. All \( n \geq 1 \)
🔹 B. All \( n \geq 3 \)
🔹 C. All \( n \geq e \)
🔹 D. All \( n \geq 2 \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Define \( f(x) = \ln x / x \). Then \( f'(x) = (1 - \ln x)/x^2 \), which is negative when \( \ln x > 1 \), i.e., \( x > e \approx 2.718 \). Thus for integers \( n \geq 3 \), \( f'(x) < 0 \) on [n, n+1], so \( a_{n+1} < a_n \). At n=2: \( a_2 = \ln2/2 ≈ 0.346 \), \( a_3 = \ln3/3 ≈ 0.366 > a_2 \), so not decreasing at n=2. Hence strictly decreasing only for \( n \geq 3 \). This combines calculus with discrete indexing, noting threshold at e.

Q22. In error analysis of iterative root-finding, suppose errors satisfy \( |e_{n+1}| \leq k |e_n|^2 \) with \( 0 < k < 1 \) and \( |e_1| < 1 \). Is \( \{|e_n|\} \) monotone?

🔹 B. Not necessarily; it depends on initial error magnitude.
🔹 C. Yes, nonincreasing but may plateau.
🔹 D. No, because squaring can increase small errors.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Given \( |e_1| < 1 \) and \( k < 1 \), we have \( |e_2| \leq k |e_1|^2 < |e_1|^2 < |e_1| \). By induction, if \( |e_n| < 1 \), then \( |e_{n+1}| \leq k |e_n|^2 < |e_n| \). Thus strictly decreasing. Quadratic convergence with contraction factor ensures monotonic error reduction once inside basin of attraction. This links numerical analysis to monotone sequences, showing how convergence order implies monotonicity under suitable initialization.

Q23. Which statement correctly distinguishes monotonicity from boundedness in convergence criteria?

🔹 A. Boundedness implies convergence for all sequences; monotonicity does not.
🔹 B. Monotone sequences converge iff bounded; bounded sequences may oscillate.
🔹 C. Both properties are equivalent for convergence.
🔹 D. Monotonicity guarantees divergence if unbounded; boundedness guarantees convergence.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Monotone Convergence Theorem states that a monotone sequence converges if and only if it is bounded. In contrast, boundedness alone doesn’t ensure convergence (e.g., \( (-1)^n \)). This distinction is fundamental: monotonicity provides directional control, while boundedness provides range control. Together they suffice; separately, only monotone+bnd ⇒ conv. This clarifies why both hypotheses are necessary in MCT and prevents conflating necessary vs sufficient conditions.

Q24. Given \( a_n = \sum_{k=n}^{2n} \frac{1}{k} \), determine monotonicity by comparing \( a_n \) and \( a_{n+1} \).

🔹 A. Strictly increasing because more terms are added.
🔹 B. Strictly decreasing because terms shrink faster than count grows.
🔹 C. Not monotone due to harmonic series divergence.
🔹 D. Constant asymptotically.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Write \( a_{n+1} - a_n = \sum_{k=n+1}^{2n+2} \frac{1}{k} - \sum_{k=n}^{2n} \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{2n+1} + \frac{1}{2n+2} - \frac{1}{n} \). Simplify: \( \frac{1}{2n+1} + \frac{1}{2n+2} < \frac{1}{2n} + \frac{1}{2n} = \frac{1}{n} \), so difference < 0. Thus strictly decreasing. This requires careful index manipulation and inequality estimation, showcasing advanced application of series comparison within sequence monotonicity analysis.

This applies domain-specific knowledge of population models to monotonicity, linking biology to analysis.

Q28. Consider \( a_n = n^{1/n} \). A student claims it’s decreasing for all n≥1 because exponent 1/n decreases. Why is this incomplete?

🔹 A. They didn’t consider that base n increases simultaneously.
🔹 B. They should have used logarithms.
🔹 C. The sequence actually increases initially.
🔹 D. All of the above.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The student isolates exponent decay but ignores competing base growth. Taking logs: \( \ln a_n = (\ln n)/n \), whose derivative sign determines monotonicity. As shown earlier, this increases for n<e and decreases for n>e. Thus sequence increases from n=1 to n=3 then decreases. All listed critiques are valid: simultaneous variation, log transformation necessity, and initial increase. Comprehensive error analysis requires acknowledging all facets.

Q29. In optimization, gradient descent generates \( x_{n+1} = x_n - \alpha \nabla f(x_n) \). If f is convex and α small, \( \{f(x_n)\} \) is monotone decreasing. What if f is non-convex?

🔹 A. \( \{f(x_n)\} \) may lose monotonicity due to local minima/saddles.
🔹 B. \( \{f(x_n)\} \) remains monotone if α is adaptive.
🔹 C. \( \{x_n\} \) stays monotone even if f(x_n) doesn’t.
🔹 D. Monotonicity is irrelevant in non-convex settings.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Non-convex landscapes contain regions where gradient ascent occurs temporarily during escape from saddles or transitions between basins, causing \( f(x_{n+1}) > f(x_n) \). Thus objective value sequence loses monotonicity. This connects optimization practice to sequence theory, showing how problem structure dictates monotonicity—a crucial consideration in algorithm design and convergence proofs.

Q30. Let \( a_n = \prod_{k=1}^n (1 + 1/k^2) \). Prove it is strictly increasing and bounded.

🔹 A. Increasing because each factor >1; bounded because \( \sum 1/k^2 \) converges.
🔹 B. Increasing trivially; bounded by e^{π²/6} via exp(log product).
🔹 C. Both follow from comparison to exponential of harmonic series.
🔹 D. Boundedness requires integral test; increase is obvious.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Each factor \( 1 + 1/k^2 > 1 \), so product strictly increases. Taking logs: \( \ln a_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \ln(1 + 1/k^2) < \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k^2 < \pi^2/6 \). Thus \( a_n < e^{\pi^2/6} \), proving boundedness. This uses inequality \( \ln(1+x) < x \) and known p-series sum, combining infinite products, series, and monotonicity in a sophisticated argument typical of advanced analysis.

Q31. Which condition ensures that a recursively defined sequence \( a_{n+1} = g(a_n) \) is monotone?

🔹 A. g is increasing and \( a_2 \geq a_1 \).
🔹 B. g is continuous.
🔹 C. g has a fixed point.
🔹 D. g is differentiable.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

If g is increasing and \( a_2 = g(a_1) \geq a_1 \), then by induction \( a_{n+1} = g(a_n) \geq g(a_{n-1}) = a_n \), so increasing. Similarly for decreasing. Continuity, fixed points, or differentiability alone don’t guarantee monotonicity without ordering conditions. This synthesizes recursion, function properties, and induction—a mixed-concept insight vital for dynamical systems analysis.
