What is Eventually properties of sequences?

A sequence has an 'eventually' property if it holds for all sufficiently large \( n \) (beyond some index \( N \)), like 'eventually increasing' means \( a_{n+1} \ge a_n \) for all \( n \ge N \), which is enough for convergence tests since the first few terms don't affect the limit.

Q1. A sequence is defined by \( a_n = \frac{n^2 - 10n + 25}{n - 5} \) for \( n \neq 5 \) and \( a_5 = 100 \). Which statement best describes the monotonicity of this sequence?

A.The sequence is strictly increasing for all \( n \geq 1 \)
B.The sequence is eventually strictly increasing ✅
C.The sequence is neither increasing nor decreasing due to the value at \( n=5 \)
D.The sequence is eventually decreasing
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Although the specific value \( a_5 = 100 \) creates a significant disruption in the pattern, the definition of properties holding eventually allows us to ignore finitely many initial terms. For \( n > 5 \), the expression simplifies to \( n-5 \), which is clearly strictly increasing. Therefore, despite the anomaly at \( n=5 \), the sequence possesses the property of being eventually strictly increasing because the tail behaves monotonically.

The student's error lies in assuming that global regularity is required for convergence, whereas only the asymptotic behavior determines the existence of a limit.

Q10. If a sequence \( \{a_n\} \) is known to be eventually increasing and bounded above by \( M=10 \), but the first 50 terms exceed 10, what is the most precise statement about its limit \( L \)?

C.\( L = 10 \) exactly
D.No limit exists because the sequence violates the upper bound condition
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The Completeness Axiom and the Monotone Convergence Theorem rely on the behavior of the sequence's tail. If the sequence is eventually increasing, there exists some index \( N \) after which \( a_n \leq M \). The limit \( L \) is the least upper bound of the set \( \{a_n : n \geq N\} \). Since all terms in this tail are \( \leq 10 \), the supremum must be \( \leq 10 \). The fact that earlier terms exceeded 10 is irrelevant to the limit's value, as limits describe asymptotic behavior, not transient excursions.

Q13. A sequence satisfies \( a_n > 0 \) for all \( n \) and \( \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = 0.5 \). A student claims the sequence is eventually strictly decreasing. Is this claim justified?

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The definition of a limit states that if \( \lim r_n = L \), then for any \( \epsilon > 0 \), eventually \( |r_n - L| < \epsilon \). Choosing \( \epsilon \) such that \( L + \epsilon < 1 \) (possible since \( L=0.5 \)), we guarantee that eventually \( r_n < 1 \). Since terms are positive, \( a_{n+1}/a_n < 1 \) implies \( a_{n+1} < a_n \). Thus, the sequence is indeed eventually strictly decreasing. This connects the analytic definition of limits directly to the discrete property of monotonicity, validating the student's claim rigorously.

Q21. In modeling chemical concentration, \( C_n \) follows a complex recurrence. Analysis shows \( C_{n+1} - C_n = \frac{(-1)^n}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2} \). Determine if the concentration is eventually monotone.

Example 3: \( a_n = n + (-1)^n/n, b_n = -n \) (adjusting to maintain monotonicity individually is tricky, but simpler: \( a_n=n, b_n=-n+1/n \) for \( n \geq 1 \); \( b \) is eventually decreasing? Derivative of \( -x+1/x \) is \( -1-1/x^2 < 0 \). Sum = \( 1/n \), decreasing). Since outcomes vary, no universal eventual monotonicity exists for the sum. This highlights that eventual monotonicity is not closed under addition.

Q29. Which of the following sequences is NOT eventually monotone?

A.\( a_n = \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} \) ✅
B.\( a_n = n + \sin(n) \)
C.\( a_n = \ln(n!) - n \ln n \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Option A: Signs alternate forever. Values jump between positive and negative. Never monotone. Option B: Derivative \( 1 + \cos x \geq 0 \). Non-decreasing everywhere. Option C: Related to Stirling; eventually increasing/decreasing? Actually \( \ln(n!) \approx n \ln n - n \). Difference approaches constant. Detailed analysis shows it's monotone. Q30. In a proof showing a sequence is eventually strictly decreasing, why is it acceptable to assume \( n \) is sufficiently large without specifying \( N \) explicitly?

A.Because \( N \) is always 1
B.Because the existence of such \( N \) is guaranteed by the limit properties, even if not computed ✅
C.Because proofs don't require rigor
D.Because all sequences become monotone after \( n=100 \)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: In many analytical proofs, establishing that \( \lim r_n = L < 1 \) suffices to claim eventual strict decrease. The epsilon-delta definition guarantees some \( N \) exists where the condition holds. Explicitly finding \( N \) is often algebraically tedious and unnecessary for proving existence/convergence. The logical structure relies on the existential quantifier in the limit definition. This distinguishes constructive algorithms (needing explicit \( N \)) from existence proofs (relying on guaranteed \( N \)). Understanding this abstraction is key to higher-level analysis.

Q31. A recursive sequence \( x_{n+1} = \frac{x_n^2 + 3}{4} \) with \( x_1 = 0.5 \) is observed to increase. To prove it is eventually strictly increasing, what additional condition must be verified beyond the base case?

A.That \( x_n < 1 \) for all relevant \( n \)
B.That \( x_n > 3 \) for all relevant \( n \)
C.That the function \( f(x) = (x^2+3)/4 \) satisfies \( f(x) > x \) on the interval containing the sequence's range ✅
D.That the sequence is bounded
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Monotonicity of recursive sequences \( x_{n+1}=f(x_n) \) depends on whether \( f(x) > x \) (increasing) or \( f(x) < x \) (decreasing) in the region where the sequence lives. Solving \( (x^2+3)/4 > x \Rightarrow x^2 - 4x + 3 > 0 \Rightarrow (x-1)(x-3) > 0 \). This holds for \( x < 1 \) or \( x > 3 \). Since \( x_1=0.5 \) and fixed point is 1, the sequence stays in \( [0.5, 1) \). In this interval, \( f(x) > x \), guaranteeing strict increase. Verifying this functional inequality on the invariant interval is the rigorous step linking recursion to eventual monotonicity.
