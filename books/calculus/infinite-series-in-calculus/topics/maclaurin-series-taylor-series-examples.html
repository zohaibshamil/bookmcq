What is Maclaurin series Taylor series examples?

A Taylor series is an infinite sum \( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!} (x-a)^k \); examples include \( e^x = \sum \frac{x^k}{k!} \) (Maclaurin), \( \sin x = \sum (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} \), and \( \cos x = \sum (-1)^k \frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!} \), all valid for all real \( x \).

Q1. A student attempts to find the Maclaurin series for \( f(x) = \ln(1+x^2) \) by computing derivatives at zero. After finding \( f(0)=0, f'(0)=0, f''(0)=2 \), they conclude the series begins \( 0 + 0x + x^2 + \dots \). Which statement best critiques this approach compared to using substitution into a known series?

A. The derivative method is always superior because it guarantees convergence.
B. The derivative method is correct but inefficient; substitution yields \( x^2 - x^4/2 + x^6/3 \dots \) instantly and avoids higher-order derivative errors. ✅
C. The student's result is wrong because \( f''(0) \) should be 1.
D. Substitution cannot be used here because \( \ln(1+u) \) only converges for \( u<1 \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While the derivative method is theoretically valid, computing higher derivatives of composite functions like \( \ln(1+x^2) \) is prone to algebraic error and computationally expensive. Substituting \( u=x^2 \) into the known expansion for \( \ln(1+u) \) is a higher-order strategy that leverages existing knowledge. This question tests the conceptual understanding of when to apply direct definition versus algebraic manipulation of known series, emphasizing efficiency and error reduction in mathematical modeling.

Q2. Consider the power series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(x-3)^k}{k} \). If this series is differentiated term-by-term to create a new series representing \( f'(x) \), how does the interval of convergence change?

A. The radius of convergence increases to infinity.
B. The radius of convergence remains 1, but the interval changes from \( [2, 4) \) to \( (2, 4) \). ✅
C. The radius of convergence remains 1, and the interval remains \( [2, 4) \).
D. The radius of convergence decreases because differentiation makes terms larger.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Differentiation of a power series preserves the radius of convergence \( R \), but endpoint behavior can change. The original series converges conditionally at \( x=2 \) (alternating harmonic) and diverges at \( x=4 \) (harmonic). The differentiated series \( \sum (x-3)^{k-1} \) is geometric with ratio \( |x-3| \); it diverges at both endpoints \( x=2 \) and \( x=4 \). Thus, the open interval \( (2,4) \) remains, but conditional convergence at the boundary is lost. This tests deep understanding of endpoint analysis versus radius preservation.

Q3. In modeling relativistic kinetic energy, \( K = m_0 c^2 [ (1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2} - 1 ] \). Using the binomial series, which term represents the first correction to the classical Newtonian kinetic energy \( \frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2 \)?

A. \( \frac{1}{8} m_0 v^4 / c^2 \)
B. \( \frac{3}{8} m_0 v^4 / c^2 \) ✅
C. \( \frac{1}{2} m_0 v^2 \)
D. \( -\frac{1}{4} m_0 v^4 / c^2 \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Expanding \( (1-u)^{-1/2} \) where \( u=v^2/c^2 \) gives \( 1 + \frac{1}{2}u + \frac{3}{8}u^2 + \dots \). Subtracting 1 and multiplying by \( m_0 c^2 \) yields \( \frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2 + \frac{3}{8}m_0 v^4/c^2 + \dots \). The first term is Newtonian KE. The second term is the leading-order relativistic correction. This requires applying binomial series to a physics model and interpreting the physical significance of specific terms in an asymptotic expansion, bridging pure math and applied science.

Q4. A student claims that since the Maclaurin series for \( e^{-x^2} \) is \( \sum (-1)^k x^{2k}/k! \), integrating from 0 to 1 gives an alternating series whose error is bounded by the next term. They approximate \( \int_0^1 e^{-x^2} dx \approx 1 - 1/3 + 1/10 \) and claim error \( < 1/42 \). What is the flaw in this reasoning?

A. There is no flaw; the alternating series estimation theorem applies directly.
B. The integrated series is not alternating.
C. The bound \( 1/42 \) is correct but the approximation value is calculated wrongly.
D. The Alternating Series Estimation Theorem requires terms to decrease monotonically in magnitude, which must be verified for the integrated series at the specific limit. ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

While the integrated series \( \sum (-1)^k / [(2k+1)k!] \) is indeed alternating, students often blindly apply the error bound without verifying the decreasing condition \( |a_{n+1}| \leq |a_n| \). In this case it holds, but the *reasoning* is incomplete without verification. More subtly, if the upper limit were variable or large, monotonicity might fail initially. This question targets the misconception that 'alternating' automatically implies 'estimable by next term' without checking all hypotheses, promoting rigorous error analysis over rote procedure.

Q5. Given graphs of \( y=f(x) \) and three polynomials \( P_1, P_2, P_3 \) near \( x=a \), where \( P_1 \) is tangent, \( P_2 \) matches concavity, and \( P_3 \) matches third derivative. If \( f(x) \) has an inflection point at \( x=a \), which polynomial will visually appear most distinct from the others near \( a \)?

A. \( P_1 \) because linear approximations fail at inflection points.
B. \( P_2 \) because it forces a local extremum where none exists. ✅
C. \( P_3 \) because cubic terms dominate near zero.
D. All polynomials look identical near \( a \).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

At an inflection point, \( f''(a)=0 \). A quadratic approximation \( P_2(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+0 \) reduces to the linear tangent, matching \( P_1 \). However, if a student incorrectly computes \( P_2 \) assuming non-zero curvature or misinterprets the graph, discrepancies arise. But theoretically, \( P_1 \) and \( P_2 \) coincide. The key insight is recognizing that at inflection points, even-degree Taylor polynomials may degenerate. This tests graphical interpretation of derivative conditions and understanding how polynomial degree relates to local geometry, specifically identifying when higher-order terms vanish.

Q6. Suppose \( f(x) = \int_0^x \sin(t^2) dt \). Without evaluating the integral, determine the coefficient of \( x^7 \) in the Maclaurin series for \( f(x) \).

A. \( -1/42 \) ✅
B. \( 1/42 \)
C. \( -1/7 \)
D. \( 0 \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

First expand \( \sin(u) = u - u^3/6 + \dots \) with \( u=t^2 \): \( \sin(t^2) = t^2 - t^6/6 + \dots \). Integrating term-by-term: \( f(x) = x^3/3 - x^7/(6 \cdot 7) + \dots = x^3/3 - x^7/42 + \dots \). The coefficient is \( -1/42 \). This combines substitution, series expansion, and integration. It avoids brute-force differentiation of an integral function, testing strategic problem-solving and synthesis of multiple calculus operations within the power series framework.

Q7. Let \( f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2} & x \neq 0 \\ 0 & x=0 \end{cases} \). All derivatives at zero are zero. What does this imply about representing \( f \) as a power series centered at 0?

A. The Maclaurin series converges to \( f(x) \) for all \( x \).
B. The Maclaurin series converges only at \( x=0 \), despite \( f \) being smooth everywhere.
C. The Maclaurin series converges to 0 for all \( x \), but equals \( f(x) \) only at \( x=0 \). ✅
D. No Maclaurin series exists because derivatives don't exist.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This classic counterexample shows that \( C^\infty \) does not imply analyticity. The Taylor series is identically zero (since all \( f^{(n)}(0)=0 \)), so it converges everywhere to the zero function. But \( f(x) > 0 \) for \( x \neq 0 \), so the series equals \( f \) only at the center. This challenges the assumption that infinite differentiability guarantees power series representation, highlighting the distinction between smooth and analytic functions—a profound conceptual point often missed in computational courses.

Q9. A pendulum's period involves \( \int_0^{\pi/2} (1-k^2 \sin^2 \phi)^{-1/2} d\phi \). For small \( k \), why is truncating after the \( k^2 \) term sufficient for engineering precision, whereas for \( k \approx 0.7 \) many more terms are needed?

A. Because \( k^2 \) is always negligible.
B. Because the binomial coefficients grow factorially, making high-order terms dominant for large \( k \).
C. Because the series is asymptotic, not convergent.
D. Because for small \( k \), higher powers decay rapidly; for \( k \approx 0.7 \), \( k^{2n} \) decays slowly and coefficients amplify error. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The binomial expansion \( (1-u)^{-1/2} = \sum c_n u^n \) has positive increasing coefficients \( c_n \). When \( u=k^2 \sin^2 \phi \ll 1 \), terms decay fast. When \( k \approx 0.7 \), \( u \) approaches 0.5, and combined with growing \( c_n \), convergence slows dramatically. This requires analyzing rate of convergence dependent on parameter magnitude, connecting series theory to practical numerical modeling trade-offs in physics.

Q13. To evaluate \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2} \), which method provides the most insight into the rate of approach?

Q14. If \( \sum c_k (x-a)^k \) converges at \( x=b \) where \( b>a \), what can be definitively concluded about convergence at \( x=a-(b-a) \)?

A. It converges absolutely.
B. It converges, possibly conditionally. ✅
C. It diverges.
D. Nothing can be concluded without knowing coefficients.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Convergence at \( x=b \) implies radius \( R \geq b-a \). The symmetric point \( x=a-(b-a) \) is at distance \( b-a \) left of center. Within open interval \( (a-R, a+R) \), convergence is absolute. At boundary points distance R away, convergence may be conditional or absolute. Since \( b \) is a boundary candidate, the mirror point is also boundary. We know it converges (by symmetry of radius), but not necessarily absolutely. Tests precise understanding of interval structure versus absolute/conditional distinction at endpoints.

Tests transfer of abstract convergence concepts to engineering contexts.

This probes deep algebraic structure underlying special functions: exponential generating functions encode additive structure via factorial denominators. Challenges students to derive functional equations from series definitions rather than memorizing identities.

Q24. Prove that if \( \sum a_n x^n \) has radius R and \( \limsup |a_n|^{1/n} = L \), then \( R=1/L \). Now suppose \( a_n = 2^n \) for even n, \( 3^n \) for odd n. Find R.

Even subsequence gives 2, odd gives 3; supremum dominates. Tests mastery of Cauchy-Hadamard theorem with non-monotonic coefficients, challenging assumption that coefficient patterns are regular.

Contrasts with differentiation, which amplifies terms. Tests understanding of how calculus operations alter series convergence characteristics at critical points.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Start with \( \sum x^n = 1/(1-x) \). Differentiate: \( \sum n x^{n-1} = 1/(1-x)^2 \). Multiply by x: \( \sum n x^n = x/(1-x)^2 \). Set x=1/2: \( (1/2)/(1/2)^2 = 2 \). Efficiently leverages calculus on generating functions. Tests strategic manipulation of series to evaluate numeric sums, connecting discrete summation to continuous function operations.

Q29. A population model uses \( P(t) = P_0 e^{rt} \). For small r, linear approximation \( P_0(1+rt) \) suffices. If r doubles, why can't we just double the correction term?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Reinforces that integration preserves alternating/decreasing structure for well-behaved integrands, validating error estimation.

Q32. Suppose \( f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n \) converges for \( |x|<R \) and \( f(x) = 0 \) for all \( x \in (-R,R) \). Prove \( a_n=0 \) for all n. Now, if \( f(x)=0 \) only on \( (-R/2, R/2) \), does conclusion hold?

Q33. Why is the Taylor series for \( |x| \) about \( x=1 \) valid only for \( x>0 \), despite \( |x| \) being defined for all x?

Q34. In finance, continuous compounding uses \( e^r \). Discrete compounding uses \( (1+r/n)^n \). Show via series that difference is \( O(r^2/n) \) for small r, large n.

A. Expand both as series in r; leading difference term involves \( r^2/n \). ✅
B. They are identical; no difference.
C. Difference is exponential in n.
D. Use L'Hôpital on difference.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

\( e^r = 1+r+r^2/2+\dots \). \( (1+r/n)^n = \exp(n \ln(1+r/n)) = \exp(r - r^2/(2n)+\dots) = e^r \cdot e^{-r^2/(2n)+\dots} \approx e^r (1 - r^2/(2n)) \). Difference \( \approx e^r \cdot r^2/(2n) \). Series expansion quantifies discretization error. Tests asymptotic analysis combining multiple expansions to extract leading error term, vital for numerical method validation.

Q35. Given \( f(x) = \int_0^x t \sin(t^3) dt \). Find \( f^{(7)}(0) \) without differentiating 7 times.
