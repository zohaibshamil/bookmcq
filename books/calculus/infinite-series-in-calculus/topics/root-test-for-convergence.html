Q1. A student applies the Root Test to the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{k}{2k+1} \right)^k \) and calculates \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{a_k} = \frac{1}{2} \). They conclude the series converges. However, another student claims the limit is actually 1 because \( \frac{k}{2k+1} \to \frac{1}{2} \) and raising to power \( 1/k \) makes it go to 1. Which analysis correctly identifies the flaw in the second student's reasoning?

🔹 A. The second student is correct because any sequence approaching a constant raised to \( 1/k \) approaches 1.
🔹 B. The second student confused the limit of the base with the limit of the entire expression; since the base approaches \( 1/2 \), the \( k \)-th root also approaches \( 1/2 \), not 1.
🔹 C. The Root Test cannot be applied here because the terms are not strictly positive for all \( k \).
🔹 D. Both students are incorrect; the limit does not exist because the expression oscillates.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The second student commits a fundamental error in limit evaluation by misapplying exponent rules. While it is true that \( c^{1/k} \to 1 \) for a fixed constant \( c > 0 \), here the base itself is a sequence \( b_k = \frac{k}{2k+1} \) that depends on \( k \). The correct evaluation recognizes that \( \sqrt[k]{(b_k)^k} = b_k \), so the limit is simply \( \lim_{k \to \infty} b_k = 1/2 \). This distinction between a constant base and a variable base raised to a variable power is crucial for correctly applying the Root Test and avoiding false conclusions about convergence.

Q2. Consider the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k \) where \( a_k = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{3^k} & \text{if } k \text{ is even} \\ \frac{1}{2^k} & \text{if } k \text{ is odd} \end{cases} \). Why is the Root Test more appropriate than the Ratio Test for determining convergence of this specific series?

🔹 D. The Ratio Test would incorrectly suggest divergence because the ratio alternates between values greater than and less than 1.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question targets the conceptual advantage of the Root Test over the Ratio Test for series with oscillating or piecewise-defined terms. For this series, the ratio \( a_{k+1}/a_k \) alternates between \( \frac{1/2^{k+1}}{1/3^k} \) and \( \frac{1/3^{k+1}}{1/2^k} \), causing the limit to not exist. However, the Root Test examines \( \sqrt[k]{a_k} \), which equals \( 1/3 \) for even \( k \) and \( 1/2 \) for odd \( k \). Since both subsequences converge to values less than 1, and specifically \( \limsup \sqrt[k]{a_k} = 1/2 < 1 \), the Root Test definitively establishes convergence. This highlights the Root Test's robustness via limit superior when standard limits fail.

🔹 B. The maximum value of \( 3 + \sin(k) \) is 4, so the limsup is \( 4/5 \), ensuring convergence.
🔹 C. The average value of \( \sin(k) \) is 0, so the limit is \( 3/5 \).
🔹 D. Because \( \sin(k) \) is dense in [-1,1], the limsup is determined by the supremum of the base, which is \( 4/5 \), confirming convergence.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

This scenario-based question requires understanding how the Root Test handles bounded oscillations within an exponential term. Although \( \sin(k) \) never settles to a single value, the Root Test relies on the limit superior. Since \( -1 \leq \sin(k) \leq 1 \), we have \( 2 \leq 3+\sin(k) \leq 4 \). Thus \( \sqrt[k]{A_k} = \frac{3+\sin(k)}{5} \). The limsup is determined by the largest accumulation point of this sequence, which is \( 4/5 \). Because \( 4/5 < 1 \), the series converges absolutely. Students must recognize that density or oscillation doesn't prevent application of the Root Test; rather, the limsup captures the worst-case growth rate, making it ideal for such physical models with bounded perturbations.

Q6. For the series \( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^2 4^k} \), a student attempts the Root Test and gets stuck evaluating \( \lim_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{\frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^2}} \). Which alternative strategy best resolves this impasse while staying within the spirit of root-based analysis?

🔹 A. Switch immediately to the Integral Test since factorials suggest continuous extension.
🔹 B. Use Stirling’s approximation \( n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n \) to simplify the \( k \)-th root expression asymptotically.
🔹 C. Apply the Ratio Test instead, as it handles factorials more naturally through cancellation.
🔹 D. Conclude the test is inconclusive because factorials grow too fast for root extraction.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This challenging question addresses a common computational barrier in Root Test applications involving factorials. While the Ratio Test is often preferred for factorials due to telescoping, the question specifically asks for a root-based resolution. Stirling’s formula provides the asymptotic equivalence \( n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n \), allowing substitution into the radical: \( \sqrt[k]{\frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^2}} \sim \sqrt[k]{\frac{\sqrt{4\pi k}(2k/e)^{2k}}{2\pi k (k/e)^{2k}}} = \sqrt[k]{\frac{4^k}{\sqrt{\pi k}}} \to 4 \). Combined with the \( 4^k \) denominator, \( \rho = 4/4 = 1 \), rendering the test inconclusive—but the method itself is valid. This demonstrates advanced technique selection and asymptotic reasoning, showing that the Root Test can handle factorials when equipped with proper approximations, even if ultimately inconclusive.

Thus, the Root Test’s reliance on \( \limsup \) rather than strict limits makes it applicable to a broader class of series. This theoretical strength justifies its inclusion despite computational difficulty. Understanding this hierarchy helps students choose tests strategically based on term structure rather than habit.

This Olympiad-style problem demands sophisticated asymptotic analysis within the Root Test framework. This exemplifies advanced problem-solving where Root Test initiates but doesn’t conclude analysis, pushing students beyond mechanical application toward nuanced asymptotic reasoning essential in research-level mathematics.

This reflects mature problem-solving: recognize tool limitations and pivot efficiently. Students learn that inconclusive results aren’t failures but signals to deploy complementary techniques, optimizing analytical workflow.

Physical normalization would fix \( C \), but mathematically, convergence holds universally. This counters intuition that large \( C \) might cause divergence; the \( k \) in denominator dominates exponentially. Students see how mathematical tools provide rigorous guarantees beyond physical heuristics, reinforcing abstraction’s power in scientific modeling.

Q18. A recursive sequence defines \( a_1 = 1 \), \( a_{k+1} = a_k \cdot \left( \frac{k}{k+1} \right)^k \). To analyze \( \sum a_k \), express \( a_k \) in closed form and apply Root Test. What closed-form expression facilitates this?

📖 Explanation:

This multi-step reasoning problem requires unwinding recursion before Root Test application. Then \( \sqrt[k]{a_k} \) becomes tractable via Stirling or known limits. This synthesis of recurrence solving and series testing exemplifies advanced problem decomposition. Students practice converting implicit definitions to explicit forms amenable to standard tests, bridging discrete dynamics and infinite series analysis.
