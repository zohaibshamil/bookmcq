What is Evaluating Definite Integrals by Substitution?

Definition:
To evaluate \( \int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x) \, dx \) by substitution, let \( u=g(x) \). Change limits: \( u(a) \) and \( u(b) \). Then compute \( \int_{u(a)}^{u(b)} f(u) \, du \). This avoids converting back to x.

Example:
\( \int_0^1 2x e^{x^2} \, dx \). Let \( u=x^2 \). Limits: \( 0 \to 0, 1 \to 1 \). \( du=2x dx \). Integral: \( \int_0^1 e^u du = e^1 - e^0 = e - 1 \).

Reason:
Changing limits simplifies the process by keeping the calculation in the u-domain, reducing algebraic errors associated with substituting back to the original variable.

Q1. Evaluate \(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin^3 x \cos x \, dx\).

A. 01-Apr ✅
B. 01-Feb
C. 03-Apr
D. 1

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = \sin x\), so \(du = \cos x dx\). When \(x=0, u=0\); when \(x=\pi/2, u=1\). The integral becomes \(\int_{0}^{1} u^3 du = [u^4/4]_{0}^{1} = 1/4\). This is a direct Easy of the substitution method for definite integrals, where changing the limits of integration is crucial to avoid back-substitution.

Q2. Which substitution would NOT simplify the evaluation of \(\int_{1}^{e} \frac{\ln x}{x} dx\)?

A. \(u = \ln x\) ✅
B. \(u = 1/x\)
C. \(u = (\ln x)^2/2\)
D. \(u = e^x\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The substitution \(u = e^x\) would make the integral more complex because \(du = e^x dx\) and \(x = \ln u\), leading to \(\int \frac{\ln(\ln u)}{u} du\). The other substitutions simplify the integrand. \(u = \ln x\) gives \(du = dx/x\). \(u = 1/x\) gives \(du = -dx/x^2\), leading to \(-\int u \ln(1/u) du\). \(u = (\ln x)^2/2\) is an antiderivative form.

Q3. A student evaluating \(\int_{0}^{4} x\sqrt{9-x^2} dx\) uses \(u = 9-x^2\). What is the correct transformed integral?

A. \(\int_{9}^{-7} \sqrt{u} du\) ✅
B. \(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{9}^{-7} \sqrt{u} du\)
C. \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{9}^{25} \sqrt{u} du\)
D. \(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{9}^{25} \sqrt{u} du\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

With \(u=9-x^2\), \(du=-2x dx\), so \(x dx = -du/2\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=9\), \(x=4 \to u=-7\). The integral becomes \(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{9}^{-7} \sqrt{u} du\). The student might forget the negative sign or incorrectly transform the limits, leading to common errors like option C which has incorrect limits and sign.

Q4. Given that \(\int_{0}^{2} f(x) dx = 5\) and \(f\) is continuous, what is \(\int_{0}^{1} f(2x) dx\)?

A. 2.5 ✅
B. 5
C. 10
D. Cannot be determined

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = 2x\), then \(du = 2dx\), so \(dx = du/2\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=0\), \(x=1 \to u=2\). The integral becomes \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2} f(u) du = \frac{1}{2}(5) = 2.5\). This tests the understanding that substitution changes the limits and scales the integral, which is a common Easy of the method.

Q5. Which of the following integrals requires a substitution beyond a simple linear function?

A. \(\int_{0}^{1} (3x+2)^4 dx\) ✅
B. \(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \cos(2x) dx\)
C. \(\int_{0}^{1} x e^{x^2} dx\)
D. \(\int_{0}^{2} \sin(4x-1) dx\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The integral \(\int_{0}^{1} x e^{x^2} dx\) requires a non-linear substitution \(u=x^2\). The others only require linear substitutions like \(u=3x+2\), \(u=2x\), or \(u=4x-1\). This question distinguishes between recognizing the need for a linear vs. a more complex substitution, testing Medium of the method.

Q6. Evaluate \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \sin^2 x \cos x \, dx\).

A. 0 ✅
B. 02-Mar
C. 01-Mar
D. 04-Mar

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = \sin x\), \(du = \cos x dx\). Limits: \(x=-\pi/2 \to u=-1\), \(x=\pi/2 \to u=1\). The integral becomes \(\int_{-1}^{1} u^2 du = [u^3/3]_{-1}^{1} = 1/3 - (-1/3) = 2/3\). Since the integrand is even, the result is twice the integral from 0 to 1, which is \(2(1/3)=2/3\).

Q7. What is the most common error when applying substitution to definite integrals?

A. Choosing the wrong \(u\) ✅
B. Forgetting to change the limits of integration
C. Not multiplying by the derivative
D. Not finding the antiderivative

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The most frequent error is forgetting to change the limits of integration from \(x\)-values to corresponding \(u\)-values. Students often compute the antiderivative in terms of \(u\), evaluate at the original \(x\)-limits, or forget to back-substitute for the final evaluation. This error leads to incorrect numerical answers and is a common misconception.

Q8. If \(f\) is continuous and \(\int_{1}^{4} f(u) du = 7\), then \(\int_{0}^{1} x f(x^2+1) dx = ?\)

A. 07-Feb ✅
B. 14
C. 07-Apr
D. Cannot be determined

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = x^2+1\), \(du = 2x dx\), so \(x dx = du/2\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=1 \to u=2\). The integral becomes \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2} f(u) du\). We are given \(\int_{1}^{4} f(u) du = 7\), but the limit is 2, not 4, so we cannot determine the exact value. Wait, the question says \(\int_{1}^{4} f(u) du = 7\). The new limits are 1 to 2, not 1 to 4. So the value is \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2} f(u) du\), which is not determined by the given information. Correct answer: Cannot be determined.

Q9. The substitution \(u = x^2\) transforms \(\int_{0}^{2} x^3 \sqrt{x^2+1} dx\) into which integral?

A. \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{4} u \sqrt{u+1} du\) ✅
B. \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{4} u^2 \sqrt{u+1} du\)
C. \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2} u \sqrt{u+1} du\)
D. \(\int_{0}^{4} u \sqrt{u+1} du\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u=x^2\), then \(du=2x dx\), so \(x dx = du/2\). The integrand \(x^3\sqrt{x^2+1} dx = x^2 \sqrt{x^2+1} (x dx) = u \sqrt{u+1} (du/2)\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=0\), \(x=2 \to u=4\). So the integral is \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{4} u \sqrt{u+1} du\). The challenge is correctly identifying \(x^3 dx\) as \(x^2 (x dx)\).

Q10. Which of the following is the correct evaluation of \(\int_{0}^{1} \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} dx\) using substitution?

A. \([-\sqrt{1-x^2}]_{0}^{1}\) ✅
B. \([-\sqrt{1-x^2}]_{0}^{1}\) and equals 1
C. \([-\sqrt{1-x^2}]_{0}^{1}\) and equals -1
D. \([-\sqrt{1-x^2}]_{1}^{0}\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u=1-x^2\), \(du=-2x dx\), so \(x dx = -du/2\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=1 \to u=0\). The integral becomes \(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{0} u^{-1/2} du = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} u^{-1/2} du = [\sqrt{u}]_{0}^{1} = 1\). Or directly, the antiderivative is \(-\sqrt{1-x^2}\), evaluated from 0 to 1 gives \(-0 - (-1) = 1\). Option B correctly states the antiderivative and the value.

Q11. If \(F(x)\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x)\), what is \(\int_{a}^{b} x f(x^2) dx\) in terms of \(F\)?

A. \(\frac{1}{2}[F(b^2)-F(a^2)]\) ✅
B. \(F(b^2)-F(a^2)\)
C. \(\frac{1}{2}[F(b)-F(a)]\)
D. \(F(b)-F(a)\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u=x^2\), \(du=2x dx\), so \(x dx = du/2\). Limits: \(x=a \to u=a^2\), \(x=b \to u=b^2\). The integral becomes \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{a^2}^{b^2} f(u) du = \frac{1}{2}[F(b^2)-F(a^2)]\). This demonstrates the chain rule in reverse and the change of limits, requiring an understanding that the argument of \(F\) must match the new limits of integration.

Q12. Evaluate \(\int_{0}^{\pi/4} \tan x \sec^2 x dx\).

A. 01-Feb ✅
B. 1
C. \(\sqrt{2}-1\)
D. 01-Apr

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = \tan x\), then \(du = \sec^2 x dx\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=0\), \(x=\pi/4 \to u=1\). The integral becomes \(\int_{0}^{1} u du = [u^2/2]_{0}^{1} = 1/2\). This is a straightforward Easy where the derivative of the chosen \(u\) is present in the integrand, making the substitution obvious and the integration simple.

Q13. A student evaluates \(\int_{1}^{2} \frac{dx}{x \ln x}\) and gets \(\ln(\ln 2)\). What is the student's mistake?

A. They used \(u = \ln x\) correctly ✅
B. They forgot the constant of integration
C. They evaluated at wrong limits
D. They made no mistake; the answer is correct

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = \ln x\), \(du = dx/x\). Limits: \(x=1 \to u=0\), \(x=2 \to u=\ln 2\). The integral becomes \(\int_{0}^{\ln 2} \frac{du}{u} = [\ln |u|]_{0}^{\ln 2}\). However, \(\ln(0)\) is undefined, so the integral is improper. The correct evaluation would be \(\lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \int_{\epsilon}^{\ln 2} \frac{du}{u} = \infty\). The student likely ignored the singularity at \(x=1\). This is a common error in analyzing the domain of the integrand.

Q14. Which of the following is NOT a valid step in evaluating \(\int_{0}^{1} x(1-x)^3 dx\) using substitution?

A. \(u = 1-x\), \(du = -dx\) ✅
B. \(x = 1-u\)
C. Limits: 0 to 1 become 1 to 0
D. \(\int_{0}^{1} x(1-x)^3 dx = -\int_{1}^{0} (1-u)u^3 du\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

All steps are correct. Let \(u=1-x\), \(du=-dx\), \(x=1-u\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=1 \to u=0\). So \(\int_{0}^{1} x(1-x)^3 dx = \int_{1}^{0} (1-u)u^3 (-du) = -\int_{1}^{0} (1-u)u^3 du = \int_{0}^{1} (1-u)u^3 du\). This is correct. The question asks which is NOT a valid step, but all are valid, implying the student must recognize the equivalence.

Q15. If \(\int_{0}^{1} f(x) dx = 3\), what is \(\int_{0}^{1} f(1-x) dx\)?

A. 3
B. -3
C. 1
D. Cannot be determined ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = 1-x\), \(du = -dx\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=1 \to u=0\). The integral becomes \(\int_{1}^{0} f(u) (-du) = \int_{0}^{1} f(u) du = 3\). This shows that the integral is invariant under the substitution \(x \to 1-x\). It tests the understanding that the value of the integral does not depend on the variable name and how substitution affects the limits.

Q16. Evaluate \(\int_{-1}^{1} x^3 \sqrt{1-x^2} dx\).

A. 0 ✅
B. 01-Feb
C. 02-Mar
D. 1

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The function \(f(x) = x^3 \sqrt{1-x^2}\) is odd because \(f(-x) = -x^3 \sqrt{1-x^2} = -f(x)\). The interval \([-1,1]\) is symmetric about the origin. The definite integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is 0. This can also be checked by substitution \(u=1-x^2\) or \(x = \sin \theta\), but the symmetry argument is most efficient. This is a classic Easy reasoning question.

Q17. The integral \(\int_{0}^{\pi} \sin x \cos(\cos x) dx\) equals:

A. 0 ✅
B. 2\(\sin(1)\)
C. \(\sin(2)\)
D. \(\sin(1)\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = \cos x\), \(du = -\sin x dx\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=\pi \to u=-1\). The integral becomes \(-\int_{1}^{-1} \cos u du = \int_{-1}^{1} \cos u du = [\sin u]_{-1}^{1} = \sin(1) - \sin(-1) = 2\sin(1)\). Wait, the correct answer is \(2\sin(1)\). But option B is \(2\sin(1)\). Let's check: \(-\int_{1}^{-1} \cos u du = \int_{-1}^{1} \cos u du = [\sin u]_{-1}^{1} = \sin 1 - (-\sin 1) = 2\sin 1\). So the correct answer is B.

Q18. A student uses \(u = x+1\) to evaluate \(\int_{0}^{2} x\sqrt{x+1} dx\). What is the transformed integral?

A. \(\int_{1}^{3} (u-1)\sqrt{u} du\) ✅
B. \(\int_{0}^{2} (u-1)\sqrt{u} du\)
C. \(\int_{1}^{3} u\sqrt{u-1} du\)
D. \(\int_{1}^{3} \sqrt{u} du\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

With \(u=x+1\), \(du=dx\), and \(x=u-1\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=2 \to u=3\). The integral becomes \(\int_{1}^{3} (u-1)\sqrt{u} du\). This is a correct Easy where the student must express \(x\) in terms of \(u\) and change the limits. Option A is correct. Common errors include not changing limits (B) or incorrectly expressing \(x\) (C).

Q19. Which of the following is the correct antiderivative for \(\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-9x^2}}\)?

A. \(\frac{1}{3}\sin^{-1}(3x) + C\) ✅
B. \(\sin^{-1}(3x) + C\)
C. \(\frac{1}{3}\sin^{-1}(x) + C\)
D. \(\frac{1}{9}\sin^{-1}(3x) + C\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = 3x\), \(du = 3 dx\), so \(dx = du/3\). Then \(\int \frac{du/3}{\sqrt{1-u^2}} = \frac{1}{3}\int \frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^2}} = \frac{1}{3}\sin^{-1}(u) + C = \frac{1}{3}\sin^{-1}(3x) + C\). This requires recognizing the standard integral \(\int \frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^2}} = \sin^{-1} u + C\) and correctly handling the coefficient of \(x\).

Q20. Evaluate \(\int_{0}^{1} \frac{e^x}{e^x+1} dx\).

A. \(\ln(2)\) ✅
B. \(\ln(\frac{e+1}{2})\)
C. \(1 - \ln 2\)
D. \(\ln(e+1)\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = e^x+1\), \(du = e^x dx\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=2\), \(x=1 \to u=e+1\). The integral becomes \(\int_{2}^{e+1} \frac{du}{u} = [\ln u]_{2}^{e+1} = \ln(e+1) - \ln 2 = \ln(\frac{e+1}{2})\). This tests the substitution \(u\) and the properties of logarithms. Option B is correct. A common error is to incorrectly evaluate \(\ln(e+1)\) as \(1+\ln 1\), leading to option A or C.

Q21. What is the average value of \(f(x) = x e^{x^2}\) on the interval [0, 1]?

A. \(\frac{e-1}{2}\) ✅
B. \(e-1\)
C. \(\frac{e}{2}\)
D. \(\frac{e-1}{4}\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The average value is \(\frac{1}{1-0}\int_{0}^{1} x e^{x^2} dx\). Let \(u=x^2\), \(du=2x dx\), so \(x dx = du/2\). Limits: 0 to 1 become 0 to 1. The integral becomes \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} e^u du = \frac{1}{2}(e-1)\). The average value is \(\frac{e-1}{2}\). This combines the concept of average value with the substitution method, making it a Easy problem.

Q22. If \(\int_{0}^{1} f(x) dx = 4\), then \(\int_{0}^{1} x f(x^2) dx\) is:

A. 1
B. 2
C. 4 ✅
D. 8

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = x^2\), \(du = 2x dx\), so \(x dx = du/2\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=0\), \(x=1 \to u=1\). The integral becomes \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} f(u) du = \frac{1}{2}(4) = 2\). This is a straightforward Easy of substitution, but the trick is recognizing that the limits remain 0 to 1 and that the integral of \(f(u)\) from 0 to 1 is given. This is a classic problem that tests the understanding that the variable of integration is a dummy variable.

Q23. Which of the following substitutions is most appropriate for \(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin^4 x \cos^5 x dx\)?

A. \(u = \sin x\) ✅
B. \(u = \cos x\)
C. \(u = \tan x\)
D. \(u = \sin^2 x\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Both \(u = \sin x\) and \(u = \cos x\) would work, but \(u = \sin x\) is more direct because \(du = \cos x dx\), and the remaining \(\cos^4 x\) can be expressed as \((1-\sin^2 x)^2 = (1-u^2)^2\). If \(u = \cos x\), \(du = -\sin x dx\), and the remaining \(\sin^3 x\) is not a simple power of \(u\) without introducing a square root. \(u = \sin x\) leads to \(\int u^4 (1-u^2)^2 du\), which is a polynomial.

Q24. Evaluate \(\int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^3}{\sqrt{x^4+1}} dx\).

A. \(\sqrt{2}-1\) ✅
B. \(\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2}\)
C. \(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\)
D. \(\frac{1}{2}\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u = x^4+1\), \(du = 4x^3 dx\), so \(x^3 dx = du/4\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=1\), \(x=1 \to u=2\). The integral becomes \(\frac{1}{4}\int_{1}^{2} u^{-1/2} du = \frac{1}{4}[2u^{1/2}]_{1}^{2} = \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{2}-1)\). This is a non-linear substitution where the derivative of \(u\) is exactly present in the integrand. Option B is correct. Option A is missing the factor of 1/2.

Q25. A student claims that \(\int_{0}^{2} x\sqrt{4-x^2} dx = \frac{8}{3}\) by using \(u=4-x^2\). The student's answer is:

A. Correct ✅
B. Incorrect; the correct value is \(-\frac{8}{3}\)
C. Incorrect; the correct value is \(\frac{4}{3}\)
D. Incorrect; the correct value is 0

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Let \(u=4-x^2\), \(du=-2x dx\), so \(x dx = -du/2\). Limits: \(x=0 \to u=4\), \(x=2 \to u=0\). The integral becomes \(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{4}^{0} \sqrt{u} du = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{4} u^{1/2} du = \frac{1}{2}[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}]_{0}^{4} = \frac{1}{3}(8) = \frac{8}{3}\). The student's answer is correct. This question tests the student's ability to verify an answer and identify that the negative sign and changing limits are handled correctly.
