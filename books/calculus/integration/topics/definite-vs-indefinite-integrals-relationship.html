Q1. A student states that since \( \int x^2 \, dx = \frac{x^3}{3} + C \), the value of \( \int_{0}^{2} x^2 \, dx \) must be \( \frac{8}{3} + C \). Why is this incorrect?

🔹 A. The constant of integration cancels out when evaluating a definite integral, so the final result cannot contain \(C\).
🔹 B. The student forgot to multiply by the lower limit of integration when applying the Fundamental Theorem.
🔹 C. The student used an antiderivative that is invalid for the interval [0, 2].
🔹 D. The student's indefinite integral formula is algebraically incorrect, as \(C\) is a function of \(x\).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The student's reasoning contains a critical misconception about the role of the constant of integration. When applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the constant \(C\) from the antiderivative \(F(x)+C\) is subtracted out: \((F(b)+C)-(F(a)+C) = F(b)-F(a)\). Therefore, the value of a definite integral is a fixed number (like \(8/3\)) and cannot contain an arbitrary constant \(C\), as this would imply the area under a curve is not a unique value, which is geometrically impossible.

Q2. Given that \( \frac{d}{dx}[x \sin x] = x \cos x + \sin x \), which of the following correctly evaluates \( \int_{0}^{\pi} x \cos x \, dx \)?

🔹 A. \( \pi \sin \pi - 0 \)
🔹 B. \( \pi \sin \pi - 0 - \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin x \, dx \)
🔹 C. \( 0 - \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin x \, dx \)
🔹 D. \( -2 \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

This question tests the concept of reversing the product rule (integration by parts) within the context of definite integrals. Recognizing \(x \cos x\) as part of the derivative of \(x \sin x\) is key. The derivative is \(d(x \sin x) = x \cos x + \sin x\). Therefore, \( \int x \cos x \, dx = x \sin x - \int \sin x \, dx\). Evaluating this definite integral from 0 to \(\pi\) gives \([x \sin x]_0^\pi - [-\cos x]_0^\pi = (0 - 0) - (-1 - (-1))? Wait, careful: \( [x \sin x]_0^\pi = 0\). Then \(- \int_0^\pi \sin x \, dx = - [-\cos x]_0^\pi = -(-1 - (-1)?)\) Let's re-evaluate: \(-\int_0^\pi \sin x dx = -[-\cos x]_0^\pi = -((-1) - (-1)?)\) Actually, \( -\cos(\pi) = -(-1) = 1\), and \(-\cos(0) = -1\). So \(-\cos x]_0^\pi = 1 - (-1) = 2\). Then the second part is \(-[-\cos x]_0^\pi = -2\). Thus, the total is 0 - 2 = -2.

Q3. What is the primary difference between the indefinite integral \( \int f(x) \, dx \) and the definite integral \( \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \)?

🔹 A. The indefinite integral is a family of functions, while the definite integral is a number.
🔹 B. The indefinite integral is defined for all \(x\), while the definite integral is only defined for \(x\) between \(a\) and \(b\).
🔹 C. The definite integral always yields a positive value, while the indefinite integral can be negative.
🔹 D. The indefinite integral includes the differential \(dx\), but the definite integral does not.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question targets the core conceptual difference between the two types of integrals. An indefinite integral represents a set of all antiderivatives of a function, differing by a constant (a family of functions). A definite integral, on the other hand, represents the net signed area under a curve over a specific interval, which results in a numerical value. This distinction is fundamental to understanding the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which connects these two seemingly different concepts.

Q4. A particle moves with velocity \(v(t) = 3t^2 - 2\). Which integral directly calculates the displacement of the particle from \(t=1\) to \(t=3\)?

🔹 A. \( \int (3t^2 - 2) \, dt \)
🔹 B. \( \int_{1}^{3} (3t^2 - 2) \, dt \)
🔹 C. \( 3 \int_{1}^{3} t^2 \, dt - 2 \)
🔹 D. \( \int_{1}^{3} (t^3 - 2t) \, dt \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question connects the concept of integration to a physical Easy in rectilinear motion. The displacement of a particle over a time interval \([t_1, t_2]\) is defined as the change in its position \(s(t_2) - s(t_1)\). Since velocity is the derivative of position, \(v(t) = s'(t)\), the displacement is given by the definite integral of the velocity function over that time interval. The correct answer is the definite integral of \(v(t)\) from 1 to 3. Option A is an indefinite integral, which gives a family of position functions, not a specific displacement. The displacement is a specific number, making the definite integral the correct choice.

Q5. A student evaluates \( \int_{0}^{1} (2x+1)^2 \, dx \) by first finding \( \int (2x+1)^2 \, dx = \frac{(2x+1)^3}{6} \). They then plug in the limits to get \( \frac{(2(1)+1)^3}{6} - \frac{(2(0)+1)^3}{6} = \frac{27}{6} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{26}{6} \). Is this correct?

🔹 A. Yes, because the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus allows the use of any antiderivative.
🔹 B. No, because the substitution method is required for definite integrals with composite functions.
🔹 C. Yes, but only if a \(u\)-substitution is performed on the limits of integration as well.
🔹 D. No, because the antiderivative of \((2x+1)^2\) is \(\frac{(2x+1)^3}{3}\), not \(\frac{(2x+1)^3}{6}\).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The student's approach is perfectly correct. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus states that if \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\), then \( \int_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a)\). The student correctly found an antiderivative of \((2x+1)^2\) by using the chain rule in reverse (or a \(u\)-substitution). The derivative of \(\frac{(2x+1)^3}{6}\) is \(\frac{3(2x+1)^2 \cdot 2}{6} = (2x+1)^2\), so it is a valid antiderivative. The constant of integration is omitted, which is standard when evaluating definite integrals. The other options represent common errors such as forgetting the chain rule (Option D) or believing a substitution is mandatory (Option B).

Q9. Consider two functions: \(F(x) = \frac{x^2}{2} + 1\) and \(G(x) = \frac{x^2}{2} - 5\). How does the value of \( \int_{1}^{3} x \, dx \) compare to \(F(3) - F(1)\) and \(G(3) - G(1)\)?

🔹 B. It is equal to \(F(3) - F(1)\) but not \(G(3) - G(1)\).

Q10. When using a \(u\)-substitution to evaluate a definite integral, a student converts \( \int_{0}^{2} x(x^2+1)^3 \, dx \) to \( \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2} u^3 \, du \). What is the primary error in this conversion?

🔹 A. The \(1/2\) factor is incorrect; it should be \(1/3\).
🔹 B. The lower and upper limits of integration have not been changed to correspond to the \(u\)-variable.
🔹 C. The \(u\)-substitution is invalid because \(du\) must equal \(x \, dx\).
🔹 D. The problem is better solved by expanding the polynomial.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The core error is a fundamental one in the substitution method for definite integrals. When the substitution \(u = x^2 + 1\) is made, \(du = 2x \, dx\), so the integrand \(x(x^2+1)^3 dx\) becomes \(\frac{1}{2} u^3 du\). However, the limits of integration \(x=0\) and \(x=2\) must also be converted to their corresponding \(u\)-values: \(u = 0^2+1 = 1\) and \(u = 2^2+1 = 5\). The integral should be \(\frac{1}{2} \int_{1}^{5} u^3 \, du\). Forgetting to change the limits is a common and serious mistake. If this is done, the student would be integrating the function over a different interval, leading to an incorrect numerical answer.

Q11. The graphs of \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = \int_{0}^{x} f(t) \, dt\) are shown. Which statement must be true based on the graphs?

Option A is a common misconception, as the integral function can be increasing even if \(f(x)\) is not always positive (it could be zero or positive on average). Q17. A function \(F(x)\) is defined by \(F(x) = \int_{0}^{x} t^2 \, dt\). What is the value of \(F'(x)\), and how does it relate to the integrand?

This demonstrates the inverse relationship: differentiation undoes the integration. Option B is the antiderivative itself, not its derivative. Option C is a common error, treating the upper limit as a constant. Option D is incorrect as the derivative of the definite integral is the integrand as a function of \(x\), not the indefinite integral.

Q19. Consider the functions \(f(x) = \cos x\), \(F(x) = \sin x\), and \(G(x) = \sin x + 100\). When using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to evaluate \( \int_{0}^{\pi} f(x) \, dx\), choosing \(F\) over \(G\) will:

🔹 D. Yield a different, but mathematically valid, result.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

This question reinforces the fact that the constant of integration is arbitrary for indefinite integrals and completely irrelevant for definite integrals. Both \(F\) and \(G\) are valid antiderivatives because \(F'(x) = \cos x\) and \(G'(x) = \cos x\). Evaluating \(F\): \(F(\pi)-F(0) = \sin \pi - \sin 0 = 0 - 0 = 0\). This demonstrates that the fundamental theorem is independent of the choice of the particular antiderivative, making the constant of integration a purely formal addition for indefinite integrals.

🔹 B. \(F(x) = \int_{1}^{x} \frac{1}{t} dt + C\); this is valid because the constant \(C\) must be determined.

🔹 D. \(F(x) = \ln x + 3\); this is the most direct and correct expression.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question bridges the concepts of integration, initial-value problems, and functions defined by integrals. The differential equation \(F'(x) = 1/x\) has the solution \(F(x) = \ln x + C\). The initial condition \(F(1)=3\) gives \(C=3\). The integral representation \(3 + \int_{1}^{x} \frac{1}{t} dt\) is the solution to the initial value problem by definition. It is valid and a fundamental way to express the solution of an ODE. Option B is an indefinite integral representation but is not a single function. Option C is the elementary function solution, which is also correct, making this a question about equivalency of representations, though A is the most direct Easy of the FTC's definition of a function as an integral. The question's focus is on the FTC's ability to define functions, making A the best answer.

Q23. A student is asked to find the area under \(y = x^3\) from \(x = 1\) to \(x = 2\). They use the antiderivative \(F(x) = \frac{x^4}{4} + 10\). Their final answer is \(F(2)-F(1) = (4+10) - (0.25+10) = 3.75\). Which statement is most accurate?

🔹 A. The student's answer is correct, despite using an unusual antiderivative.
🔹 B. The student's answer is wrong because they should have used \(F(x) = \frac{x^4}{4}\).
🔹 C. The student's answer is wrong because they did not include the constant of integration in the final answer.
🔹 D. The student's answer is correct, but they should have added the constant to the final result.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question tests the misconception that the specific choice of antiderivative matters. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus states any antiderivative works. \(F(x) = x^4/4 + 10\) is a valid antiderivative because its derivative is \(x^3\). The calculation is correct: \(F(2)-F(1) = (4+10) - (1/4+10) = 14 - 10.25 = 3.75\). The +10 constant cancels out. The correct value of the definite integral is \(x^4/4\big|_1^2 = 4 - 1/4 = 3.75\). The student's answer is numerically identical to the correct one. This is a classic trap for students who mistakenly believe they must use the simplest antiderivative. The other options incorrectly suggest that the student's approach is flawed.

Q24. A particle moves along a line with velocity \(v(t) = 6t^2\). Its position function is \(s(t) = 2t^3 + C\). If \(s(0) = 5\), then its displacement from \(t=1\) to \(t=3\) is:

🔹 A. \( \int_{1}^{3} 6t^2 \, dt = 52 \)
🔹 B. \( \int (6t^2) \, dt = 2t^3 \)
🔹 C. \( s(3) - s(1) = (54+5) - (2+5) = 52 \)
🔹 D. Both A and C are correct and equivalent.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

This question expertly combines several related concepts: the relationship between position and velocity (\(v(t) = s'(t)\)), the concept of a family of antiderivatives (\(s(t) = 2t^3 + C\)), the use of an initial condition to solve for \(C\) (\(s(0)=5 \Rightarrow C=5\)), and the computation of displacement from the definite integral of velocity. Option A directly calculates displacement as the definite integral of velocity: \(\int_1^3 6t^2 dt = 2t^3\big|_1^3 = 54 - 2 = 52\). Option C calculates displacement from the position function: \(s(3)-s(1) = (54+5) - (2+5) = 52\). Both methods are mathematically equivalent and yield the same result. Option B gives the indefinite integral, which is the position function without the constant of integration, not the displacement. This question effectively synthesizes multiple concepts from differential and integral calculus.

Q25. The Fresnel sine function is \(S(x) = \int_{0}^{x} \sin \left( \frac{\pi t^2}{2} \right) dt\). Which of the following correctly describes the relationship between \(S(x)\) and its integrand?

🔹 A. \(S'(x) = \sin \left( \frac{\pi x^2}{2} \right)\), showing that \(S\) is an antiderivative of the integrand.
🔹 B. \(S(x)\) is the derivative of \(\sin \left( \frac{\pi x^2}{2} \right)\).
🔹 C. The derivative of \(S(x)\) is \(S(x)\), making it an exponential function.
🔹 D. \(S(x)\) is a constant function because its derivative is zero.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This question introduces a nonelementary function defined by an integral (a Fresnel function). It tests the student's ability to recognize and apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2, to unfamiliar functions. The theorem states that the derivative of a definite integral with a variable upper limit is simply the integrand evaluated at that upper limit. Therefore, \(S'(x) = \sin(\pi x^2/2)\). This means \(S(x)\) is an antiderivative of the integrand. This is a powerful result, even if the integral cannot be expressed with elementary functions. It shows how the FTC can be used to analyze functions defined by integrals, such as their extrema and inflection points. Option B incorrectly reverses the relationship. Option C is false; this is not an exponential function. Option D is false as the integrand is not zero.
