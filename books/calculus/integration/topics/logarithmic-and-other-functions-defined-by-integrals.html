What is Logarithmic and Other Functions Defined by Integrals?

Definition:
The natural logarithm is defined as \( \ln(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} \, dt \) for \( x>0 \). This integral definition establishes ln(x) as the area under \( 1/t \), from which its properties like \( \ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b \) are derived.

Example:
Derive \( \frac{d}{dx}\ln(x) \). By FTC Part 1, derivative of \( \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} dt \) is \( \frac{1}{x} \). This confirms the standard derivative rule for natural logs.

Reason:
Defining functions via integrals provides a rigorous foundation for transcendental functions, linking algebraic properties to geometric area interpretations.

Q1. Using the integral definition \( \ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} dt \), which of the following is the correct geometric interpretation of \( \ln(1/2) \)?

A. The area under \( y=1/t \) from \( t=1/2 \) to \( t=1 \)
B. The negative of the area under \( y=1/t \) from \( t=1/2 \) to \( t=1 \) ✅
C. The area under \( y=1/t \) from \( t=1 \) to \( t=2 \)
D. The area under \( y=1/t \) from \( t=0 \) to \( t=1/2 \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \( 1/2 < 1 \), the integral \( \int_1^{1/2} 1/t \, dt \) is negative. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives \( \ln(1/2) = - \int_{1/2}^1 1/t \, dt \), which is the negative of the area under the curve from \( 1/2 \) to \( 1 \). Option A confuses the sign, Option C confuses the interval, and Option D is incorrect because the integral starts at 1, not 0.

Q2. Given the definition \( \ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} dt \), a student concludes that \( \ln x \) is always positive for \( x > 0 \). What is the error in this reasoning?

A. The student incorrectly assumes the integrand is always positive, which it is, but the integral is positive only for \( x > 1 \).
B. The student incorrectly assumes the integrand is positive, which is false for \( x<1 \).
C. The student incorrectly assumes the integral's sign depends solely on the integrand's sign, ignoring the limits of integration. ✅
D. The student incorrectly assumes \( \ln x \) is defined for all real numbers.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The sign of a definite integral depends on both the integrand and the limits. While \( 1/t \) is positive for \( t>0 \), the integral from 1 to x is negative when \( 0 < x < 1 \) because the upper limit is less than the lower limit. Option C correctly identifies the core error: ignoring the effect of the limits on the integral's sign. Option B is false because the integrand is indeed positive, and Option A is partially true but misses the key point about integration limits.

Q3. A particle's velocity is given by \( v(t) = \frac{1}{t} \). If its position at \( t=1 \) is \( s(1)=3 \), what is its position function \( s(t) \) for \( t>0 \)?

A. \( s(t) = \ln t \)
B. \( s(t) = \ln t + 3 \) ✅
C. \( s(t) = \ln t - 3 \)
D. \( s(t) = \ln|t| + 3 \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since velocity is the derivative of position, \( s'(t) = v(t) = 1/t \). Integrating gives \( s(t) = \ln t + C \) for \( t>0 \). Using the initial condition \( s(1)=3 \), we get \( 3 = \ln 1 + C \), so \( C = 3 \). Thus, \( s(t) = \ln t + 3 \). Option C incorrectly subtracts the constant, and Option A forgets the initial condition. Option D uses the absolute value, which is unnecessary for \( t>0 \).

Q4. The Fresnel sine function is defined as \( S(x) = \int_0^x \sin(\pi t^2/2) dt \). Which of the following is the best interpretation of \( S(2) \)?

A. The area under \( y = \sin(\pi t^2/2) \) from \( t=0 \) to \( t=2 \)
B. The net signed area between \( y = \sin(\pi t^2/2) \) and the t-axis from \( t=0 \) to \( t=2 \) ✅
C. The derivative of \( \sin(\pi x^2/2) \) at \( x=2 \)
D. The value of \( \sin(\pi x^2/2) \) at \( x=2 \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

By definition, a definite integral from 0 to 2 represents the net signed area. Since \( \sin(\pi t^2/2) \) can be negative, the integral is not simply the 'area' (which is always positive). Option A is incorrect because it ignores the sign of the function. Option C and D confuse the function with its integral and derivative, respectively, as per the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Q5. A student attempts to find \( \frac{d}{dx} \int_0^{x^2} \frac{1}{1+t^2} dt \) and writes \( \frac{1}{1+x^4} \). Which crucial step did they miss?

A. They forgot to apply the chain rule and multiply by the derivative of the upper limit, \( 2x \). ✅
B. They used the wrong antiderivative for \( 1/(1+t^2) \).
C. They incorrectly treated \( x^2 \) as a constant.
D. They forgot to evaluate the integrand at the lower limit.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus Part 2 states: \( \frac{d}{dx} \int_a^{g(x)} f(t) dt = f(g(x)) g'(x) \). The student correctly substituted the upper limit into the integrand but forgot to multiply by the derivative of the upper limit, which is \( 2x \). The correct answer is \( \frac{2x}{1+x^4} \). Option B is false because the antiderivative is not needed here. Option D is irrelevant as the lower limit is constant.

Q6. Consider the graph of \( y = f(t) \) which is positive and increasing for \( t>0 \). Define \( F(x) = \int_1^x f(t) dt \). Which statement about \( F(x) \) is most accurate for \( x>1 \)?

A. \( F(x) \) is decreasing and concave up.
B. \( F(x) \) is increasing and concave up. ✅
C. \( F(x) \) is increasing and concave down.
D. \( F(x) \) is decreasing and concave down.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, \( F'(x) = f(x) \). Since \( f(x) \) is positive for \( x>1 \), \( F'(x) > 0 \), so \( F(x) \) is increasing. Since \( f(x) \) is increasing, \( F''(x) = f'(x) > 0 \), so \( F(x) \) is concave up. Option A reverses the monotonicity. Option C incorrectly states concave down. Option D is incorrect for both properties.

Q7. A common mistake when differentiating \( F(x) = \int_{x}^{1} \frac{1}{t} dt \) is to write \( 1/x \). What is the correct derivative?

A. \( 1/x \)
B. \( -1/x \) ✅
C. \( x \)
D. \( -x \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Using the property \( \int_x^1 f(t)dt = - \int_1^x f(t)dt \), we have \( F(x) = - \int_1^x 1/t \, dt \). Differentiating gives \( F'(x) = -1/x \). The common mistake is forgetting the negative sign that arises from swapping the limits of integration. Option A is the derivative of \( \int_1^x 1/t \, dt \), not \( \int_x^1 1/t \, dt \). Options C and D are incorrect and likely arise from an improper Easy of the power rule.

Q8. Using the integral definition of \( \ln x \), which of the following correctly proves the property \( \ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b \)?

A. By showing \( \int_1^{ab} 1/t \, dt = \int_1^a 1/t \, dt + \int_a^{ab} 1/t \, dt \) and using substitution \( t=au \). ✅
B. By differentiating \( \ln(ab) - \ln a \).
C. By using the definition \( \ln(ab) = \int_1^{ab} 1/t \, dt = \int_1^a 1/t \, dt + \int_1^b 1/t \, dt \).
D. By showing the function \( f(x) = \ln(ax) - \ln a \) has derivative 0.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The standard proof involves splitting the integral \( \int_1^{ab} 1/t \, dt = \int_1^a 1/t \, dt + \int_a^{ab} 1/t \, dt \). For the second integral, substituting \( t=au \), \( dt = a \, du \), and changing limits from \( t=a \) to \( u=1 \) and \( t=ab \) to \( u=b \), gives \( \int_1^b 1/u \, du \). Thus, \( \ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b \). Option B is a correct approach but less direct. Option C ignores the substitution needed for the second integral. Option D is a different, albeit correct, proof.

Q9. The error function is defined as \( \text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^x e^{-t^2} dt \). What is the limit \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \text{erf}(x) \)?

A. \( 0 \)
B. \( 1 \) ✅
C. \( \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \)
D. \( \infty \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The error function is used in probability and statistics. As \( x \to \infty \), the integral \( \int_0^\infty e^{-t^2} dt = \sqrt{\pi}/2 \), which is a known result (Gaussian integral). Thus, \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} = 1 \). Option A and C are incorrect values; Option D is a common misconception for functions defined by integrals.

Q10. A model for the spread of a disease predicts \( r(t) = \frac{1}{t+1} \) individuals per day. If there are 0 infected people at \( t=0 \), find the function \( P(t) \) for the number infected after \( t \) days.

A. \( P(t) = \ln(t+1) \) ✅
B. \( P(t) = \ln|t+1| + C \)
C. \( P(t) = \ln(t+1) + 1 \)
D. \( P(t) = \frac{1}{(t+1)^2} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The rate of change of the infected population is \( P'(t) = r(t) \). Thus, \( P(t) = \int \frac{1}{t+1} dt = \ln(t+1) + C \). Since \( P(0)=0 \), we get \( 0 = \ln(1) + C \), so \( C=0 \). Thus, \( P(t) = \ln(t+1) \). Option B is an indefinite integral without applying the initial condition. Option C has an incorrect constant. Option D is the derivative of \( -1/(t+1) \), which is not the integral.

Q11. Given the function \( F(x) = \int_{0}^{x^2} \sin(t^2) dt \), what is \( F''(0) \)?

A. \( 0 \) ✅
B. \( 2 \)
C. \( 4 \)
D. Undefined

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

First, \( F'(x) = 2x \sin(x^4) \) by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and the chain rule. Then, \( F''(x) = 2 \sin(x^4) + 2x \cdot \cos(x^4) \cdot 4x^3 = 2 \sin(x^4) + 8x^4 \cos(x^4) \). Evaluating at \( x=0 \) gives \( F''(0) = 2 \sin(0) + 0 = 0 \). Options B and C are incorrect and might arise from misapplying the chain rule or differentiating the product incorrectly. Option D is a common trap when functions are defined by integrals, but here the derivative exists and is well-defined.

Q12. Which of the following is a crucial difference between a function defined by an integral and one expressed in terms of elementary functions?

A. Integrals always produce functions that are more complex.
B. Functions defined by integrals may not have derivatives.
C. An integral representation is a complete and precise definition of a function's value for each point in its domain, whereas an elementary expression is just a closed-form formula. ✅
D. There is no difference; both are equally easy to compute numerically.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

A function defined by an integral, like \( \text{erf}(x) \), is precisely defined for every \( x \) by the value of the definite integral. This is a rigorous mathematical definition. While it might lack a simple closed-form, it is not less 'complete.' Option A is subjective. Option B is false, as continuous integrands guarantee differentiability. Option D is false, as functions like \( \text{erf}(x) \) are computationally intensive to evaluate, requiring numerical methods.

Q13. A student uses the definition \( \ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} dt \) to prove \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \ln x = \infty \). Which argument is logically sound?

A. Since \( \ln x \) is the area under \( y=1/t \) from 1 to x, and the region is unbounded, the area must be infinite.
B. For any integer n, if \( x > 2^n \), then \( \ln x > \ln 2^n = n \ln 2 \). Since \( \ln 2 > 0 \), this can be made arbitrarily large. ✅
C. The function \( 1/t \) is positive and decreasing, so its integral from 1 to infinity must diverge.
D. Since the derivative of \( \ln x \) is \( 1/x \) which approaches 0, the function must approach infinity.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Option B uses the formal definition of a limit to infinity. It establishes that for any large number \( M \), we can choose n such that \( n \ln 2 > M \), and then for any \( x > 2^n \), \( \ln x > M \). This is a rigorous proof. Option A is an intuitive but not formal argument. Option C is a known theorem but not a proof from the definition. Option D is a logically flawed argument; a derivative approaching zero does not imply the function approaches infinity.

Q14. A function \( F \) is defined by \( F(x) = \int_{1}^{x} \frac{\sin t}{t} dt \). Which of the following statements about the derivative \( F'(x) \) is true?

A. \( F'(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x} \), and this is valid for all real \( x \).
B. \( F'(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x} \), but this is only valid for \( x \neq 0 \). ✅
C. The derivative exists only for \( x > 0 \).
D. The derivative cannot be found because the antiderivative of \( \sin t / t \) is not an elementary function.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus states that \( F'(x) = \sin x / x \). However, this function is undefined at \( x=0 \) because of division by zero. The integral is defined and differentiable for all x, but the derivative formula is only valid where the integrand is continuous, which excludes \( x=0 \). Option A incorrectly states it's valid for all x. Option C is false; \( \sin t / t \) is continuous for all \( t \neq 0 \), so the integral is defined on all intervals excluding zero. Option D is a common misconception; the derivative exists, even if the antiderivative isn't elementary.

Q15. Using the integral definition of \( \ln x \), a student wants to compare \( \ln 4 \) and \( \ln 8 \). Which of the following is the most efficient method without directly evaluating the integrals?

A. Since \( 8 = 2 \cdot 4 \), we use \( \ln 8 = \ln(2 \cdot 4) = \ln 2 + \ln 4 \), so \( \ln 8 > \ln 4 \).
B. Since \( 8 > 4 \), and \( 1/t \) is positive for \( t>0 \), the integral from 1 to 8 is greater than the integral from 1 to 4.
C. The derivative \( \frac{d}{dx} \ln x = 1/x \) is positive, so \( \ln x \) is increasing, hence \( \ln 8 > \ln 4 \). ✅
D. Graph the function \( y=1/t \) and observe the area from 1 to 8 is greater than from 1 to 4.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Option C is the most elegant and theoretically sound proof. The derivative of \( \ln x \) is \( 1/x \), which is positive for \( x>0 \), proving \( \ln x \) is strictly increasing. Therefore, for \( 8>4 \), \( \ln 8 > \ln 4 \). Option A is circular, as it requires knowing \( \ln 2 > 0 \). Option B relies on the monotonicity of \( 1/t \), which is true but less direct. Option D is a geometric argument, not a rigorous algebraic one.

Q16. The Fresnel cosine function \( C(x) = \int_0^x \cos(\pi t^2/2) dt \) has a relative maximum when \( C'(x)=0 \) and \( C''(x)<0 \). For what positive value of x does this occur?

A. \( x = \sqrt{2} \)
B. \( x = 1 \) ✅
C. \( x = 2 \)
D. \( x = \sqrt{3} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

We have \( C'(x) = \cos(\pi x^2/2) \). Setting \( C'(x)=0 \) gives \( \pi x^2/2 = \pi/2 + n\pi \), so \( x^2 = 1 + 2n \). For \( n=0 \), \( x=1 \). For \( x=1 \), \( C''(x) = -\pi x \sin(\pi x^2/2) \). At \( x=1 \), \( C''(1) = -\pi \sin(\pi/2) = -\pi < 0 \), which indicates a relative maximum. Option A is incorrect because \( x=\sqrt{2} \) would be a minimum. Option C and D are also incorrect as they are not critical points.

Q17. A common mistake when working with \( \ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} dt \) is to say \( \ln(-2) = \int_1^{-2} \frac{1}{t} dt \). Why is this mathematically invalid?

A. Because the function \( 1/t \) is not integrable on any interval.
B. Because the definite integral is only defined for continuous functions on closed intervals, and \( 1/t \) is not continuous on the interval from 1 to -2.
C. Because the natural logarithm is only defined for positive arguments, so \( \ln(-2) \) is not a real number, and the integral on that interval is not a real number. ✅
D. Because the limits of integration must be in increasing order.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The integral \( \int_1^{-2} \frac{1}{t} dt \) is improper because the function is discontinuous at \( t=0 \), which lies between the limits. It does not converge to a real number. Option A is incorrect because \( 1/t \) is integrable on any interval that does not contain 0. Option B is partially true but misses the key point that the integral diverges. Option D is false; limits can be swapped, but the integral would be \( \int_1^{-2} \), which is improper.

A. \( dy/dx = 1/x, y(1)=0 \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

Formula (11) assumes the integrand \( f(t) \) is continuous on an open interval containing the path from \( x_0 \) to \( x \). The function \( f(x)=|x| \) is continuous everywhere, so the formula applies. Actually, all these functions are continuous on appropriate intervals. The question tests understanding that the formula applies to all continuous f, including those without elementary antiderivatives. Q19. Consider the function \( F(x) = \int_{1}^{x} \frac{1}{t} dt \). A student states \( F'(x) = \frac{1}{x} \) and \( F''(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2} \). Which statement is true regarding the concavity of \( F(x) \)?

A. \( F(x) \) is concave up for all \( x > 0 \).
B. \( F(x) \) is concave down for all \( x > 0 \). ✅
C. \( F(x) \) has an inflection point at \( x=1 \).
D. \( F(x) \) is not concave anywhere.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The second derivative of \( \ln x \) is \( -1/x^2 \), which is negative for all \( x > 0 \). A negative second derivative means the function is concave down. Option A is incorrect; it would require a positive second derivative. Option C is false because \( F''(x) \) never changes sign. Option D is false because the function is clearly concave down.

Q20. A particle moves along the \( s \)-axis with acceleration \( a(t) = \frac{1}{t} \) for \( t>0 \). Its initial velocity is \( v(1)=2 \). What is its velocity function \( v(t) \)?

A. \( \ln t + 2 \) ✅
B. \( \ln t + 1 \)
C. \( \ln|t| + 2 \)
D. \( \ln t + C \), where C is any constant.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity: \( v'(t) = a(t) = 1/t \). Integrating gives \( v(t) = \ln t + C \). Using the initial condition \( v(1)=2 \), we get \( 2 = \ln 1 + C \), so \( C=2 \). Thus, \( v(t) = \ln t + 2 \). Option C unnecessarily uses the absolute value, which is valid but not simpler. Option B has an incorrect constant. Option D is the general solution without applying the initial condition.

Q21. A student differentiates \( F(x) = \int_{0}^{x} \frac{\sin t}{t} dt \) and correctly gets \( F'(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x} \). For their next problem, they differentiate \( G(x) = \int_{0}^{x^2} \frac{\sin t}{t} dt \) and write \( G'(x) = \frac{\sin x^2}{x^2} \). What is the best critique of their solution?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The student correctly applied the chain rule in the first part but forgot it in the second. The correct derivative is \( G'(x) = \frac{\sin x^2}{x^2} \cdot 2x = \frac{2 \sin x^2}{x} \). Option B is incorrect; they did substitute \( x^2 \), but didn't multiply by its derivative. Option C is false; while the integrand has a removable discontinuity at 0, the integral is differentiable. Option D is false.

Q23. Given the graph of a positive, continuous function \( f(t) \), define \( F(x) = \int_{1}^{x} f(t) dt \). If \( F(3) = 5 \) and \( F(5) = 9 \), what is the average value of \( f(t) \) on the interval [3,5]?

A. 2 ✅
B. 4
C. \( \frac{7}{4} \)
D. \( \frac{14}{5} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The average value of \( f(t) \) on [3,5] is \( \frac{1}{5-3} \int_3^5 f(t) dt \). Using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, \( \int_3^5 f(t) dt = F(5) - F(3) = 9 - 5 = 4 \). Thus, the average value is \( \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 = 2 \). Option B is the integral itself, not the average. Option C and D are arbitrary values derived from incorrect sums.

Q24. A student uses a calculator to approximate \( \ln 2 \) and gets 0.693. Another student uses the midpoint rule with \( n=10 \) to approximate \( \int_1^2 1/t \, dt \) and gets 0.693. Which statement is most accurate?

A. The calculator is correct, but the midpoint rule approximation is incorrect because it only approximates the area.
B. The midpoint rule gives the exact value of \( \ln 2 \) because \( n=10 \) is sufficiently large.

D. The midpoint rule with \( n=10 \) is more accurate than the calculator because calculators have rounding errors.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Both methods are numerical approximations of the true value of \( \ln 2 \). A calculator typically uses more sophisticated algorithms and can compute with high precision, while the midpoint rule with \( n=10 \) is a relatively coarse approximation. Option A is false because both are approximations. Option B is false; it's an approximation, not exact. Option D is generally false; calculator approximations are usually very accurate.
