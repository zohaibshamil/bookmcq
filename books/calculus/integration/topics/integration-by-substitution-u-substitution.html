What is Integration by Substitution u-Substitution?

Definition:
U-substitution reverses the chain rule for integration by changing variables. If \( u = g(x) \), then \( du = g'(x)dx \), transforming \( \int f(g(x))g'(x) \, dx \) into \( \int f(u) \, du \), simplifying composite functions into basic forms.

Example:
Integrate \( \int 2x(x^2+1)^3 \, dx \). Let \( u = x^2+1 \), \( du = 2x \, dx \). Integral becomes \( \int u^3 \, du = \frac{u^4}{4} + C = \frac{(x^2+1)^4}{4} + C \).

Reason:
This technique is essential for integrating composite functions where the inner function's derivative is present, expanding the range of integrable functions beyond simple polynomials.

Setting \( u = x^2 \) simplifies the integral to a basic cosine integral. Other options incorrectly identify \( u \) or the resulting integrand.

Q3. A student makes the substitution \( u = x^2 \) for the integral \( \int x \sin(x^2) \, dx \). They obtain \( \frac{1}{2} \int \sin(u) \, du \). The student then replaces \( u \) with \( x^2 \) to get \( \frac{1}{2} \cos(x^2) + C \). What is the error in the student's work?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: The student correctly performed the u-substitution: \( u = x^2, du = 2x dx \), so the integral becomes \( \frac{1}{2} \int \sin(u) du \). The error is in the final integration. The antiderivative of \( \sin(u) \) is \( -\cos(u) \), not \( \cos(u) \). Therefore, the correct final answer is \( -\frac{1}{2} \cos(x^2) + C \). This question tests the ability to identify common sign errors in integration.

Q15. A student evaluates \( \int_{0}^{1} x \sqrt{1 - x^2} \, dx \) using \( u = 1 - x^2 \). The student correctly calculates the antiderivative as \( -\frac{1}{3} (1-x^2)^{3/2} \). However, they get a final answer of 0. What is the likely mistake?

A.The student used the wrong derivative for \( u \).
B.The student evaluated the antiderivative at the wrong limits.
C.The student forgot to change the limits of integration and evaluated at \( x=0 \) and \( x=1 \). ✅
D.The student incorrectly integrated the \( u \) power.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: This is a classic error. If a student uses u-substitution but does not change the limits of integration, they will evaluate the antiderivative in terms of \( u \) at the original \( x \) limits, which is incorrect. Let's check: \( u = 1 - x^2 \), when \( x=0 \), \( u=1 \), and when \( x=1 \), \( u=0 \). The correct integral is \( \int_{1}^{0} -\frac{1}{2} \sqrt{u} du = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} u^{1/2} du = \frac{1}{3} \). If the limits are not changed, evaluating at \( x=0 \) and \( x=1 \) gives \( -\frac{1}{3}(1-1)^{3/2} + \frac{1}{3}(1-0)^{3/2} = 1/3 \), which is actually the correct answer. The student's answer of 0 suggests a different error, like mis-evaluating the antiderivative. Let's refine the question. A student correctly finds the antiderivative to be \( -\frac{1}{3}(1-x^2)^{3/2} \) and evaluates it from 0 to 1, getting \( -\frac{1}{3}(0)^{3/2} - (-\frac{1}{3}(1)^{3/2}) = 1/3 \). If they got 0, they might have evaluated \( 1-x^2 \) at both limits as 0, which means they didn't understand the substitution. Let's reword to focus on the limits. The student's mistake is evaluating the antiderivative at the original \( x \) limits after substituting, but then incorrectly substituting the \( x \) values back into the \( u \)-expression. The core error is not correctly applying the changed limits.

Q20. Two students evaluate \( \int \sin(x) \cos(x) dx \). Student A lets \( u = \sin(x) \), getting \( \frac{1}{2} \sin^2(x) + C \). Student B lets \( u = \cos(x) \), getting \( -\frac{1}{2} \cos^2(x) + C \). Are these answers equivalent?

The difference between the two answers is the constant \( \frac{1}{2} \), which is absorbed into the constant of integration \( C \). This question tests the understanding that antiderivatives are unique only up to an additive constant.

D.\( \frac{1}{x} + C \)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: This is a classic integral. With \( u = \ln(x) \), \( du = \frac{1}{x} dx \), the integral becomes \( \int \frac{1}{u} du = \ln|u| + C = \ln|\ln(x)| + C \). This is a fundamental example of the \( \int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} dx = \ln|f(x)| + C \) pattern. The other options are incorrect Easys of the power rule or other integration rules.

Q28. Which of the following integrals requires a substitution involving completing the square before u-substitution?

A.\( \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} dx \)
B.\( \int \frac{1}{x^2+2x+5} dx \) ✅
C.\( \int \sin(x) \cos(x) dx \)
D.\( \int \frac{e^x}{1+e^{2x}} dx \)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The integral in option B involves a quadratic in the denominator. Completing the square gives \( x^2+2x+5 = (x+1)^2+4 \). Then a substitution like \( u = x+1 \) (or a trigonometric substitution) can be used. The other options are standard u-substitution problems: A uses \( u = x \), C uses \( u = \sin(x) \), and D uses \( u = e^x \). This question tests the skill of algebraically manipulating the integrand before applying u-substitution.

Q29. Explain why the substitution \( u = x^2 \) is not a good choice for the integral \( \int \sin(x^2) dx \).

A.Because the derivative of \( x^2 \), which is \( 2x \), is not present in the integrand. ✅
B.Because \( \sin(x^2) \) is not a function of \( u \).
C.Because the integral of \( \sin(u) \) is not defined.
D.Because the limits of integration cannot be changed.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: For u-substitution to work, the derivative of the substituted variable must be present in the integrand. In \( \int \sin(x^2) dx \), if we let \( u = x^2 \), then \( du = 2x dx \). The \( 2x \) term is not present, and we cannot create it without complicating the integral (e.g., \( x = \sqrt{u} \)). This question tests the ability to analyze why a substitution fails, which is an important troubleshooting skill.

Q30. Evaluate \( \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin^3(x) \cos(x) dx \).

A.\( \frac{1}{4} \) ✅
B.\( \frac{1}{3} \)
C.\( \frac{1}{2} \)
D.\( 1 \)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Let \( u = \sin(x) \), so \( du = \cos(x) dx \). The new limits are \( u(0)=0 \) and \( u(\pi/2)=1 \). The integral becomes \( \int_{0}^{1} u^3 du = \left[ \frac{u^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{1} = \frac{1}{4} \). This tests the ability to apply u-substitution to a definite integral with trigonometric functions and correctly change the limits. It's a direct Easy, but a common source of errors.

Q31. A student uses a CAS to evaluate an integral and gets the answer \( \frac{1}{2} \sin^2(x) - \frac{1}{4} \cos^2(2x) + C \). They suspect it's equivalent to \( \frac{1}{2} \sin^2(x) + C \). Which of the following trigonometric identities would confirm their suspicion?

A.\( \cos^2(2x) = 1 - \sin^2(2x) \) ✅
B.\( \sin^2(x) = \frac{1 - \cos(2x)}{2} \)
C.\( \cos^2(2x) = \frac{1 + \cos(4x)}{2} \)
D.None of the above; the two expressions are not equivalent.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: This is an advanced question testing the ability to manipulate trigonometric expressions. The term \( \cos^2(2x) \) can be expressed using \( \sin^2(2x) \). However, the key is to see if \( \frac{1}{4} \cos^2(2x) \) can be absorbed into the constant of integration. Since \( \cos^2(2x) \) is not constant, the two expressions are generally not equivalent. The only way for the expressions to be equivalent is if \( \frac{1}{4} \cos^2(2x) \) were constant, which it is not. Therefore, the CAS answer is not equivalent to \( \frac{1}{2} \sin^2(x) + C \). The correct answer is D.
