What is First order linear differential equations?

Definition:
First-order linear ODEs have form \( \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x) \), solvable using integrating factor \( \mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)dx} \) to transform into exact derivative form.

Example:
For \( y' + 2y = 4 \), integrating factor \( \mu = e^{2x} \). Multiply: \( e^{2x}y' + 2e^{2x}y = 4e^{2x} \), giving \( y = 2 + Ce^{-2x} \).

Reason:
Linear equations appear frequently in applications; the integrating factor method provides systematic solution approach for this important class.

Q1. A student attempts to solve the differential equation \( y' + 2xy = x \) by separating variables, rewriting it as \( \frac{dy}{dx} = x(1-2y) \). Which of the following best analyzes the validity of this approach?

A.The method is valid because all first-order equations can be separated.
B.The method is invalid because the term \( 2xy \) prevents separation; an integrating factor is required. ✅
C.The method is valid but only yields a particular solution, not the general solution.
D.The method is invalid because the integrating factor must be \( e^{x^2} \), making separation impossible.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: This question targets error analysis regarding the distinction between separable and linear equations. The equation \( y' + 2xy = x \) cannot be written in the form \( g(y)dy = f(x)dx \) because the right-hand side \( x - 2xy \) does not factor cleanly into a product of a function of x and a function of y. Students often mistakenly attempt separation on linear equations. Recognizing that the presence of both \( y \) and \( xy \) terms necessitates the integrating factor method \( \mu = e^{\int 2x dx} \) is crucial for conceptual understanding of differential equation classification.

Q2. Consider the initial value problem \( y' + p(x)y = q(x) \) where \( p(x) \) has a discontinuity at \( x=0 \). If the initial condition is given at \( x_0 = -1 \), what is the maximal interval of existence for the unique solution?

A.The entire real line \( (-\infty, \infty) \)
B.The interval \( (-\infty, 0) \) ✅
C.The interval \( (0, \infty) \)
D.The interval \( [-1, \infty) \)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: This question tests the theoretical underpinnings of the Existence and Uniqueness Theorem for linear equations without explicitly naming it. The theorem guarantees a unique solution only on an open interval containing the initial point where both coefficient functions are continuous. Since \( p(x) \) is discontinuous at 0 and the initial point is negative, the solution cannot cross the singularity at \( x=0 \). Therefore, the domain is restricted to the negative reals. This challenges students to think beyond algebraic manipulation and consider the analytic constraints imposed by the coefficients' domains.

If a student incorrectly assumes volume changes or treats constants as variables, the exponent in the integrating factor changes fundamentally. This alters the homogeneous solution's decay rate. Understanding that the integrating factor encapsulates the system's natural response (flushing rate) helps students realize why misidentifying \( V \) leads to physically impossible predictions about concentration equilibrium.

This foundational step is essential for all subsequent linear equation applications.

Q6. Two students solve \( y' + y = e^{-x} \). Student A gets \( y = xe^{-x} + Ce^{-x} \). Student B gets \( y = e^{-x} + Ce^{-x} \). Without re-solving, how can you immediately identify Student B’s error?

📖 Explanation: This error analysis question focuses on a common integration pitfall. The integrating factor is \( e^x \). Multiplying gives \( (ye^x)' = e^{-x} \cdot e^x = 1 \). This specific mistake occurs when the source term is the reciprocal of the integrating factor. Recognizing this pattern allows for quick verification of solutions and highlights the importance of simplifying the product \( \mu q(x) \) before integrating.

Understanding this prevents confusion about 'missing' constants and clarifies that the IF is a tool, not a unique function. It reinforces the multiplicative nature of the method versus additive constants in integration.
