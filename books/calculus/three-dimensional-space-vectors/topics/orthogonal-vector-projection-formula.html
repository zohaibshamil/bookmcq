📝 Orthogonal Vector projection formula

📖 From Calculus • 12. Three Dimensional Space: Vectors • 27 questions available

What is Orthogonal Vector projection formula?

Definition:
The orthogonal projection of \( \vec{v} \) onto nonzero \( \vec{u} \) is \( \text{proj}_{\vec{u}}\vec{v} = \left( \frac{\vec{v} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}} \right) \vec{u} \), yielding the component of \( \vec{v} \) parallel to \( \vec{u} \).

Example:
Projecting \( \vec{v} = \langle 5,1 \rangle \) onto \( \vec{u} = \langle 2,1 \rangle \) gives scalar factor \( \frac{10+1}{4+1} = \frac{11}{5} \), so projection is \( \frac{11}{5}\langle 2,1 \rangle = \langle \frac{22}{5}, \frac{11}{5} \rangle \).

Reason:
Projection extracts relevant directional information, foundational for least-squares approximation, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, and computing work done along constrained paths.

Q1. A student claims that the orthogonal projection of vector \( \mathbf{u} \) onto \( \mathbf{v} \) is always shorter than \( \mathbf{u} \). Under which specific condition does this claim definitively fail, revealing a conceptual gap regarding vector magnitude?

A. When \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) are orthogonal.
B. When \( \mathbf{v} \) is a unit vector and parallel to \( \mathbf{u} \). ✅
C. When the angle between them is obtuse.
D. When \( \mathbf{u} \) is the zero vector.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The projection magnitude equals \( \|\mathbf{u}\| |\cos \theta| \). If vectors are parallel and \( \mathbf{v} \) is a unit vector, the projection length equals \( \|\mathbf{u}\| \), not strictly shorter. This tests understanding that projection preserves magnitude only in specific alignment cases, challenging the misconception that projection always reduces size.

Q2. In a 3D navigation system, a drone's velocity vector \( \mathbf{v} \) must be decomposed into components parallel and perpendicular to a wind vector \( \mathbf{w} \). If the calculated parallel component has a negative scalar coefficient relative to \( \mathbf{w} \), what is the physical interpretation of this result?

A. The drone is moving directly against the wind direction. ✅
B. The calculation contains an error as magnitudes cannot be negative.
C. The orthogonal component was computed incorrectly.
D. The wind vector was not normalized properly.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

A negative scalar in the projection formula \( \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|^2} \mathbf{w} \) indicates the angle between velocity and wind is obtuse. Physically, this means the drone has a velocity component opposing the wind flow, which is crucial for fuel consumption modeling and trajectory correction in real-world scenarios.

This question targets the common misconception that a zero output implies a zero input, reinforcing that geometric relationship (perpendicularity) dictates projection behavior more than magnitude alone in vector analysis.

Since \( \mathbf{w} \perp \mathbf{v} \), adding \( t\mathbf{w} \) contributes nothing to the projection onto \( \mathbf{v} \). The projection depends solely on \( \mathbf{u} \)'s component along \( \mathbf{v} \). Thus, the norm remains invariant. This tests deep understanding of linearity and orthogonality's effect on functional dependence in vector spaces.

This question assesses whether students understand the geometric nature of projection versus algebraic manipulation, preventing unnecessary normalization steps in applied problems.

Q8. If \( \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \text{proj}_{\mathbf{u}} \mathbf{v} \) for non-zero vectors \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \), which relationship must necessarily hold true between these vectors?

Unless the dot product is zero (orthogonal case excluded here as trivial), this equality forces \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) to be scalar multiples. This Olympiad-style problem requires algebraic manipulation combined with geometric insight about symmetry in projection operations.
