What is Unit vectors definition and examples?

Definition:
A unit vector has magnitude exactly 1 and indicates pure direction; standard basis vectors are \( \hat{i} = \langle 1,0,0 \rangle \), \( \hat{j} = \langle 0,1,0 \rangle \), \( \hat{k} = \langle 0,0,1 \rangle \), and any unit vector satisfies \( \|\hat{u}\| = 1 \).

Example:
The direction of \( \vec{v} = \langle 3,4 \rangle \) is given by unit vector \( \hat{u} = \frac{\vec{v}}{\|\vec{v}\|} = \langle \frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5} \rangle \).

Reason:
Unit vectors decouple direction from magnitude, simplifying projections, defining coordinate frames, and normalizing data in machine learning and physics.

Q1. A particle moves along a path defined by \( \vec{r}(t) = \langle t^2, \sin t, e^t \rangle \). At \( t=0 \), which expression correctly represents the unit tangent vector \( \vec{T}(0) \)?

A.\( \langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle \)
B.\( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle \) ✅
C.\( \langle 0, \cos 0, e^0 \rangle \)
D.\( \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle \)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Students must compute the derivative \( \vec{r}'(t) \), evaluate at \( t=0 \) to get \( \langle 0,1,1 \rangle \), and then normalize. Option A misses normalization, C is the unnormalized velocity, and D uses an incorrect magnitude of \( \sqrt{3} \) instead of \( \sqrt{2} \).

Q2. If \( \vec{u} \) and \( \vec{v} \) are non-parallel unit vectors, under what condition is \( \vec{u} + \vec{v} \) also a unit vector?

A.When the angle between them is \( 60^\circ \)
B.When the angle between them is \( 120^\circ \) ✅
C.When they are orthogonal
D.Never, the sum of two unit vectors cannot be a unit vector
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Setting \( |\vec{u}+\vec{v}|^2 = 1 \) leads to \( 1 + 1 + 2\cos\theta = 1 \), so \( \cos\theta = -1/2 \). This requires deep synthesis of dot product properties and unit vector definitions rather than simple recall, testing algebraic manipulation within geometric constraints.

Q3. A student claims that normalizing \( \vec{v} = \langle 3, -4, 0 \rangle \) yields \( \langle 0.6, -0.8, 0 \rangle \). Another student argues it should be \( \langle 3/7, -4/7, 0 \rangle \). Which analysis identifies the core error?

A.The first student used the correct magnitude but wrong sign convention.
B.The second student incorrectly used the L1 norm (sum of absolute values) instead of the Euclidean norm. ✅
C.Both are incorrect because the z-component must be normalized to 1.
D.The first student forgot to divide by the magnitude entirely.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The second student divided by \( |3|+|-4|+0 = 7 \), confusing the taxicab metric with the standard Euclidean norm \( \sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2}=5 \). Recognizing this specific misconception about norm definitions is crucial for understanding why unit vectors require the square root of summed squares.

Q4. Given a scalar field \( f(x,y,z) \), the gradient \( \nabla f \) points in the direction of steepest ascent. If \( \nabla f(P) = \langle 2, -2, 1 \rangle \), what is the rate of change of \( f \) at \( P \) in the direction of the unit vector \( \vec{u} = \langle \frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3} \rangle \)?

A.\( \frac{4}{3} \) ✅
B.\( \frac{8}{3} \)
C.\( \sqrt{9} \)
D.\( \frac{2}{3} \)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: This requires computing the directional derivative \( \nabla f \cdot \vec{u} \). The calculation is \( 2(2/3) + (-2)(1/3) + 1(2/3) = 4/3 - 2/3 + 2/3 = 4/3 \). Students must verify \( \vec{u} \) is indeed a unit vector before applying the formula, adding a verification step.

Q5. In a physics simulation, force vectors are often normalized to separate direction from magnitude. If a computational algorithm returns \( \vec{d} = \langle 0.577, 0.577, 0.577 \rangle \) as a direction vector, what is the most likely source of numerical imprecision?

A.The vector was computed using single-precision floating point arithmetic for \( 1/\sqrt{3} \). ✅
B.The original vector had unequal components that were rounded independently.
C.The normalization function failed to account for the z-component.
D.The vector represents a projection onto a plane rather than a true 3D direction.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The exact value is \( 1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.57735... \). Truncation to three decimals creates a vector with magnitude \( \sqrt{3(0.577)^2} \approx 0.999 \neq 1 \). Understanding that digital representations of irrational unit vectors inherently carry precision errors is vital for scientific computing and error propagation analysis.

Q6. Which of the following statements about the set of all unit vectors in \( \mathbb{R}^3 \) is fundamentally true regarding their algebraic structure?

A.They form a subspace of \( \mathbb{R}^3 \) because they are closed under addition.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A subspace must contain the zero vector and be closed under linear combinations. Unit vectors have fixed magnitude 1, so scaling by 2 or adding two unit vectors typically results in a non-unit vector. This tests abstract structural understanding beyond mere computation of individual vectors.

Q7. Consider two unit vectors \( \vec{a} \) and \( \vec{b} \) where \( \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 \). A third vector \( \vec{c} \) is defined as \( \vec{c} = \alpha \vec{a} + \beta \vec{b} \). For \( \vec{c} \) to be a unit vector, what constraint must \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) satisfy?

A.\( \alpha + \beta = 1 \)
B.\( \alpha^2 + \beta^2 = 1 \) ✅
C.\( \alpha = \beta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \)
D.\( |\alpha| + |\beta| = 1 \)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Since \( \vec{a} \) and \( \vec{b} \) are orthonormal, \( |\vec{c}|^2 = (\alpha \vec{a} + \beta \vec{b}) \cdot (\alpha \vec{a} + \beta \vec{b}) = \alpha^2 + \beta^2 \). Setting this equal to 1 gives the unit circle constraint. This connects linear combination coefficients directly to geometric normalization conditions in orthonormal bases.

Q8. A graph displays the magnitude of \( \vec{v}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle \) versus time. Without calculating, how can one determine if the normalized vector \( \hat{v}(t) \) changes direction at a constant rate?

A.Check if the magnitude graph is a horizontal line.
B.Check if the derivative of the magnitude graph is constant.
C.The normalized vector never changes direction at a constant rate for this function. ✅
D.Analyze the curvature of the magnitude graph; constant rate implies linear magnitude growth.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The z-component grows linearly while x,y rotate. The changing magnitude means normalization introduces a time-dependent scaling factor that couples with rotation. Even though the xy-projection rotates uniformly, the 3D direction change rate varies because the vertical component's relative influence increases. Graphs of magnitude alone cannot confirm uniform directional change.

Q9. When converting spherical coordinates \( (\rho, \theta, \phi) \) to Cartesian unit vectors, why is \( \hat{\rho} \) dependent on both \( \theta \) and \( \phi \), whereas \( \hat{k} \) in Cartesian is constant?

A.Spherical unit vectors are defined locally at each point and rotate with position; Cartesian basis vectors are global and fixed. ✅
B.\( \hat{\rho} \) depends only on \( \phi \); the dependence on \( \theta \) is a common textbook error.
C.Cartesian vectors are actually position-dependent but we approximate them as constant near the origin.
D.Spherical coordinates are curvilinear, making all basis vectors variable, but \( \hat{\rho} \) specifically encodes radial distance which varies.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Curvilinear coordinate systems have basis vectors that change orientation depending on location. \( \hat{\rho} \) always points radially outward from the origin, so its Cartesian components involve \( \sin\phi\cos\theta \), etc. This contrasts with the invariant Cartesian frame, highlighting the fundamental difference between local and global bases.

Q10. A navigation system computes heading using \( \vec{h} = \frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} \). During a maneuver, \( \vec{v} \) passes through zero momentarily. What is the mathematically rigorous way to handle the unit vector definition at this singularity?

A.Define \( \vec{h} = \vec{0} \) when \( |\vec{v}| = 0 \).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Division by zero makes the unit vector undefined. While numerical codes might default to zero or extrapolate, mathematically the direction is indeterminate without additional constraints. This tests understanding of domain restrictions and the distinction between computational hacks and rigorous mathematical definitions in vector calculus.

Q11. If \( \vec{u} \) is a unit vector and \( c \) is a scalar, which statement correctly describes \( |c\vec{u}| \)?

A.It always equals \( c \).
B.It equals \( |c| \) because \( |\vec{u}| = 1 \). ✅
C.It equals \( c^2 \) due to the dot product definition.
D.It depends on the angle between \( c \) and \( \vec{u} \).
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: By definition, \( |c\vec{u}| = |c||\vec{u}| \). Since \( \vec{u} \) is a unit vector, \( |\vec{u}| = 1 \), simplifying to \( |c| \). This foundational property distinguishes scalar multiplication effects on magnitude versus direction and is prerequisite for more complex normalization problems involving scaled vectors.

Q12. Two forces \( \vec{F}_1 \) and \( \vec{F}_2 \) act on a body. Their resultant \( \vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 \) has magnitude 10 N. If \( \hat{R} = \langle 0.6, 0.8, 0 \rangle \), what can be definitively concluded about \( \vec{F}_1 \) and \( \vec{F}_2 \)?

A.Both forces must lie in the xy-plane.
B.At least one force has a z-component of zero.
C.The vector sum of their z-components is zero, but individually they may have non-zero z-components. ✅
D.Both forces are unit vectors scaled by factors summing to 10.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Since \( \hat{R} \) has zero z-component, \( R_z = 0 \), implying \( F_{1z} + F_{2z} = 0 \). Individual z-components could be non-zero but cancel. This avoids the common misconception that resultant direction dictates individual vector orientations, emphasizing superposition principles over simplistic component matching.

Q13. In computer graphics, surface normals are stored as unit vectors. Why is storing unnormalized normals problematic for Phong shading calculations?

A.Unnormalized normals cause incorrect lighting intensity because the dot product with light direction scales with normal magnitude. ✅
B.Graphics hardware cannot process vectors with magnitude greater than 1.
C.Phong shading requires normals to be orthogonal to the view vector, which only unit vectors guarantee.
D.Normalization is computationally expensive, so engines skip it and accept visual artifacts.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The diffuse term \( \max(0, \hat{n} \cdot \hat{l}) \) assumes both vectors are unit length. If \( \vec{n} \) isn't normalized, the dot product includes \( |\vec{n}| \), artificially brightening or darkening surfaces based on arbitrary normal scaling. This links mathematical normalization directly to perceptual rendering correctness.

Q14. Given \( \vec{a} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle \) and \( \vec{b} = \langle -2, 1, 0 \rangle \), a student computes the unit vector perpendicular to both as \( \frac{\vec{a} \times \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|} \). What is wrong with this approach?

A.The cross product already yields a unit vector; no division is needed.
B.The denominator should be \( |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| \), not the product of individual magnitudes. ✅
C.The cross product order should be reversed to get the correct orientation.
D.Nothing is wrong; this is equivalent to the standard formula.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: While \( |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta \), dividing by \( |\vec{a}||\vec{b}| \) only works if \( \sin\theta = 1 \). Generally, you must divide by the actual magnitude of the cross product result. This exposes confusion between the cross product magnitude formula and the normalization requirement.

Q15. A drone’s orientation is represented by three mutually orthogonal unit vectors \( \hat{i}', \hat{j}', \hat{k}' \). If sensor noise corrupts \( \hat{k}' \) such that it is no longer exactly orthogonal to \( \hat{i}' \), what is the best method to restore a valid orthonormal basis?

A.Renormalize \( \hat{k}' \) independently and hope orthogonality is approximately preserved.
B.Recompute \( \hat{k}' = \hat{i}' \times \hat{j}' \) using the still-valid vectors, ensuring perfect orthogonality. ✅
C.Average the corrupted \( \hat{k}' \) with \( \hat{i}' \times \hat{j}' \) to balance noise and constraint.
D.Discard all three vectors and recalibrate sensors from scratch.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: In an orthonormal set, any two vectors uniquely determine the third via cross product. Recomputing enforces mathematical consistency despite sensor error. Independent renormalization preserves neither orthogonality nor right-handedness. This applies linear algebra constraints to real-world sensor fusion problems requiring structural integrity over raw data fidelity.

Q16. For the vector field \( \vec{F} = \frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|^3} \), what is the unit vector in the direction of \( \vec{F} \) at any point \( \vec{r} \neq \vec{0} \)?

A.\( \frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|} \) ✅

D.Undefined because the field diverges at the origin.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Despite the \( 1/r^3 \) scaling, the direction of \( \vec{F} \) is purely radial. Factoring out magnitude leaves \( \vec{r}/|\vec{r}| = \hat{r} \). Students often confuse the field’s magnitude decay with its directional property. This separates vector direction from scalar amplitude in inverse-square law contexts.

Q17. When projecting vector \( \vec{v} \) onto unit vector \( \hat{u} \), the formula is \( (\vec{v} \cdot \hat{u})\hat{u} \). Why does this fail if \( \hat{u} \) is replaced by a non-unit vector \( \vec{a} \)?

A.The dot product becomes negative for obtuse angles.
B.The scalar projection \( \vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} \) includes \( |\vec{a}| \), so the result is scaled by \( |\vec{a}|^2 \) instead of representing true projection length. ✅
C.Non-unit vectors cannot define directions in 3D space.
D.The formula still works but requires taking the absolute value of the dot product.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Projection requires isolating the component along a direction. With non-unit \( \vec{a} \), \( \vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} = |\vec{v}||\vec{a}|\cos\theta \), introducing extra magnitude. Correct projection needs division by \( |\vec{a}|^2 \). This highlights why unit vectors simplify formulas by encoding pure direction without scale contamination.

Q18. A student observes that \( \hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k} \) and concludes that for any unit vectors \( \vec{u}, \vec{v} \), \( \vec{u} \times \vec{v} \) must also be a unit vector. Which counterexample most effectively refutes this?

A.Choose \( \vec{u} = \hat{i}, \vec{v} = \hat{i} \); cross product is zero.
B.Choose \( \vec{u} = \hat{i}, \vec{v} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{i}+\hat{j}) \); magnitude is \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \).
C.Both A and B are valid counterexamples. ✅
D.No counterexample exists; the student’s conclusion is correct.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The cross product magnitude equals \( \sin\theta \). Only orthogonal unit vectors yield unit cross products. Parallel vectors give zero; oblique vectors give intermediate magnitudes. Presenting both extreme and intermediate cases comprehensively dismantles the overgeneralization and reinforces the sine dependence in cross product geometry.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Unit vectors isolate directional information, enabling clean separation of magnitude \( \mu \) from angular dependence. The resultant magnitude derivation relies on \( |\hat{u}_1 + \hat{u}_2| = 2\cos(\theta/2) \), which holds only because \( \hat{u}_i \) are normalized. This demonstrates modeling utility in physical sciences.

Apparent discrepancies often resolve upon proper normalization, reinforcing unit vector relevance in graphical analysis.

Both flaws prevent basis formation. This integrates multiple criteria—normalization, orthogonality, and dimensionality—into a single diagnostic evaluation.

Q26. In special relativity, four-velocity is defined as a unit vector in Minkowski spacetime with metric signature (-,+,+,+). How does this differ fundamentally from Euclidean unit vectors?

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Minkowski metric defines unit vectors via \( u_\mu u^\mu = -c^2 \) (or -1 in natural units), contrasting with Euclidean \( +1 \). This pseudo-unit norm reflects spacetime geometry where timelike vectors have negative squared length. Understanding this distinction is essential for relativistic physics beyond classical vector intuition.
