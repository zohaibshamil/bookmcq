📝 Geometric Cross Product Properties

📖 From Calculus • 12. Three Dimensional Space: Vectors • 28 questions available

What is Geometric Cross Product Properties?

Definition:
Magnitude \( \|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\| \) equals area of parallelogram spanned by \( \vec{u},\vec{v} \); direction follows right-hand rule; result is zero iff vectors are parallel; invariant under rotation.

Example:
Vectors \( \langle 2,0,0 \rangle \) and \( \langle 0,3,0 \rangle \) span rectangle of area 6, and cross product \( \langle 0,0,6 \rangle \) points along positive \( z \)-axis per right-hand rule.

Reason:
Area interpretation links vector algebra to geometry, enabling computation of surface elements, flux integrals, and detection of collinearity in computational geometry.

Q1. A student calculates \( \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} \) and obtains a vector with magnitude 12. They then claim that the area of the triangle formed by vectors \( \mathbf{a} \) and \( \mathbf{b} \) originating from the same point is also 12. What is the fundamental error in this reasoning?

A. The student confused the cross product magnitude with the dot product.
B. The student failed to recognize that the cross product magnitude represents the parallelogram area, requiring division by two for the triangle. ✅
C. The student used the wrong angle between the vectors in the sine function.
D. The cross product yields a scalar area directly, so no geometric adjustment is needed.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The magnitude \( |\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}| \) geometrically defines the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two vectors. A triangle formed by these vectors occupies exactly half of that parallelogram. Therefore, the correct triangular area must be calculated as \( \frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}| \), making the student's answer double the true value.

The direction is determined solely by the right-hand rule applied to the plane containing \( \mathbf{r} \) and \( \mathbf{F} \); since their relative orientation is unchanged, the torque vector's direction remains invariant.

Q26. Application: To find the shortest distance between two skew lines \( \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{p} + t\mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \mathbf{q} + s\mathbf{v} \), we use \( d = |(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}) \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v})| / |\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}| \). What geometric role does \( \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \) play?

A. It represents the direction of the common perpendicular and its magnitude scales the projection appropriately. ✅
B. It gives the area between the lines directly.
C. It is merely a computational convenience with no geometric meaning.
D. It defines the plane containing both lines.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Vector \( \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \) is orthogonal to both line directions, hence parallel to the unique common perpendicular. The numerator projects the connector vector \( \mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p} \) onto this normal direction, extracting the perpendicular separation component. Dividing by \( |\mathbf{n}| \) normalizes this projection. Geometrically, this formula computes the height of the parallelepiped formed by \( \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p} \) relative to the base parallelogram spanned by \( \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \).

Q27. Conceptual: Why does \( \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} \) hold geometrically, beyond algebraic derivation from \( \sin(0) = 0 \)?

A. Because a vector cannot be orthogonal to itself except as the zero vector.
B. Because the parallelogram degenerates to a line segment with zero enclosed area.
C. Both A and B are valid complementary geometric interpretations. ✅
D. Because cross product measures difference between vectors.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Algebraically, \( \sin(0)=0 \) gives zero magnitude. Geometrically, this corresponds to two equivalent perspectives: (1) the parallelogram spanned by identical vectors collapses to a line with zero area, and (2) the only vector orthogonal to \( \mathbf{a} \) that also lies in the degenerate plane is the zero vector. These dual interpretations reinforce that the cross product encodes both area measurement and orthogonality constraints simultaneously.

Q28. Mixed Concepts: If \( \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{c} \) and \( \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0 \), express \( |\mathbf{c}| \) purely in terms of dot products of \( \mathbf{a} \) and \( \mathbf{b} \) with themselves. What does this reveal about the relationship between orthogonality and cross product magnitude?

A. \( |\mathbf{c}| = \sqrt{(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b})} \); orthogonality makes the Lagrange identity reduce to pure product of squared norms. ✅
B. \( |\mathbf{c}| = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b} \); magnitudes add under orthogonality.
C. \( |\mathbf{c}| = |\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}| \); zero under orthogonality.
D. Cannot be expressed using only dot products.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Under orthogonality, \( \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0 \), so Lagrange identity \( |\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}|^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2|\mathbf{b}|^2 - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2 \) simplifies to \( |\mathbf{a}|^2|\mathbf{b}|^2 \). Thus \( |\mathbf{c}| = \sqrt{(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a})(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b})} \). This reveals that for orthogonal vectors, cross product magnitude depends solely on individual lengths, decoupled from angular factors. Orthogonality maximizes the cross product for given magnitudes, making it a natural norm product.
