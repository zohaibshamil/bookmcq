What is Work done by force vector?

Definition:
Work \( W \) done by constant force \( \vec{F} \) over displacement \( \vec{d} \) is scalar \( W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = \|\vec{F}\|\|\vec{d}\|\cos\theta \), measuring energy transfer dependent on alignment.

Example:
Pulling a sled with \( \vec{F} = \langle 50, 30 \rangle \) N over \( \vec{d} = \langle 10, 0 \rangle \) m does \( W = 500 + 0 = 500 \) J; vertical component does no work.

Reason:
Dot product formulation captures physical reality that only force component parallel to motion contributes to energy change, central to conservation laws and mechanical efficiency analysis.

Q1. A particle moves along a curved path \( C \) under force \( \vec{F} = \langle y, -x, z^2 \rangle \). If the path is reversed from \( B \to A \) instead of \( A \to B \), how does the work done change?

A. The work remains identical because scalar quantities are path-independent.
B. The work changes sign because the line integral depends on orientation. ✅
C. The work becomes zero because the closed loop integral of any vector field vanishes.
D. The work doubles because the particle traverses the same distance twice.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Work is defined as the line integral \( W = \int_C \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} \). Reversing the orientation of the curve \( C \) negates the differential displacement vector \( d\vec{r} \), which consequently negates the entire integral. This demonstrates that work is not merely a function of endpoints but depends critically on the direction of traversal for non-conservative fields.

Q2. Given a constant force \( \vec{F} = \langle 3, 4, 0 \rangle \) N acting on an object moving from origin to point \( P(6, 8, 0) \) m, a student calculates work as \( |\vec{F}| \times |\vec{d}| = 50 \) J. What is the fundamental error in this reasoning?

A. The student failed to convert units before multiplication.
B. The student assumed the force and displacement were parallel without verifying alignment.
C. The student used the magnitude of displacement instead of the component parallel to force. ✅
D. There is no error; the calculation is correct for constant forces.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The definition of work requires the dot product \( \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} \), not the product of magnitudes. While \( |\vec{F}||\vec{d}| \) equals work only when vectors are perfectly aligned, the general formula is \( |\vec{F}||\vec{d}|\cos\theta \). In this specific case they happen to be parallel, but the *reasoning* cited is flawed because it ignores the angular relationship inherent in the definition of mechanical work.

Q3. A force field \( \vec{F} = \langle 2xy, x^2 + z, y \rangle \) acts on a particle. Without calculating the line integral directly, determine if the work done moving between two fixed points is path-independent.

A. Yes, because the curl of \( \vec{F} \) is identically zero everywhere.
B. No, because the mixed partial derivatives of the components do not satisfy equality conditions. ✅
C. Yes, because the divergence of \( \vec{F} \) is non-zero indicating a source.
D. No, because the field contains polynomial terms of degree greater than one.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Path independence requires the field to be conservative, meaning \( \nabla \times \vec{F} = \vec{0} \). Checking the curl components reveals that \( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(x^2+z) = 0 - 1 = -1 \neq 0 \). Since the curl is non-zero, the field is non-conservative, and work depends on the specific trajectory taken, regardless of the algebraic form of the components.

Q4. An object moves along the helix \( \vec{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle \) for \( 0 \leq t \leq 2\pi \) under gravity \( \vec{F} = \langle 0, 0, -mg \rangle \). Which statement best describes the work done?

A. Work is zero because the horizontal motion is perpendicular to gravity.
B. Work equals \( -2\pi mg \) because only the vertical displacement contributes. ✅
C. Work equals \( -mg \) because the net displacement vector has unit vertical component.
D. Work cannot be determined without knowing the radius of the helix.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Gravity is a conservative force depending solely on vertical displacement \( \Delta z \). The parameterization shows \( z(t) = t \), so \( \Delta z = 2\pi - 0 = 2\pi \). The work is \( \vec{F} \cdot \Delta \vec{r} = -mg(2\pi) \). Horizontal circular motion contributes nothing since \( \vec{F}_{gravity} \perp d\vec{r}_{horizontal} \), simplifying the line integral to a simple potential energy difference calculation.

Q5. Consider the graph of a variable force \( F(x) \) vs position \( x \) where the curve lies entirely below the x-axis from \( x=a \) to \( x=b \). If a particle moves from \( b \) to \( a \), what is the sign of the work done?

A. Negative, because the area under the curve is geometrically negative.
B. Positive, because reversing limits of integration cancels the negative area. ✅
C. Zero, because the force opposes the natural direction of motion.
D. Indeterminate, because the functional form of \( F(x) \) is unknown.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Work is the signed area under the \( F-x \) curve relative to the direction of motion. Moving from \( b \) to \( a \) reverses the integration limits: \( \int_b^a F(x)dx = -\int_a^b F(x)dx \). Since the original area from \( a \) to \( b \) is negative (below axis), the negation makes the work positive. This highlights that physical work sign depends on both force direction and displacement orientation.

Q6. A student claims that if \( \oint_C \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = 0 \) for one specific closed square loop \( C \), then \( \vec{F} \) must be conservative. Evaluate this claim.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A vector field is conservative if and only if the line integral vanishes for *every* closed path in the domain. Finding zero work for a single specific loop is insufficient; the field could be non-conservative elsewhere or have cancellation effects unique to that geometry. Rigorous verification requires checking \( \nabla \times \vec{F} = \vec{0} \) throughout the simply-connected domain or testing arbitrary paths.

Recognizing the gradient structure transforms a calculus problem into simple arithmetic, demonstrating the power of identifying conservative fields in three-dimensional space.

The peer's setup uses \( \vec{F}(t) \) directly and dots with position \( \vec{r}(t) \) instead of velocity \( \vec{r}'(t) \). This error confuses the parametric representation of the field with the kinematic quantity required for work integration.

Q16. A particle moves along \( \vec{r}(t) = \langle \sin t, \cos t, t \rangle \) under \( \vec{F} = \langle y, -x, 1 \rangle \). Without full integration, predict the work from \( t=0 \) to \( t=2\pi \).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Total integrand = 2, integrated over \( 2\pi \) gives \( 4\pi \). Wait—recalculating horizontal: \( \vec{F}_h \cdot \vec{v}_h = \cos t \cdot \cos t + (-\sin t)(-\sin t) = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1 \). Plus vertical 1 gives 2. Integral is \( 4\pi \). So answer should be C. This tests careful decomposition versus intuitive guessing about cancellation.

Q18. Compare work done by \( \vec{F} = \langle x, y, z \rangle \) moving radially outward from origin to sphere surface versus tangentially along the sphere surface.

Tangential motion on sphere: \( \vec{F} \) is radial while \( d\vec{r} \) is tangential, so \( \vec{F} \perp d\vec{r} \) everywhere, yielding zero work. This illustrates how work depends on relative orientation, not just force magnitude or path length, and how central forces do no work during perpendicular displacements despite being nonzero.

Q19. A force \( \vec{F} = \langle 0, 0, kz \rangle \) acts on a mass moving vertically. If the mass oscillates between \( z=0 \) and \( z=h \) repeatedly, what happens to net work per complete cycle?

A. Net work is zero because upward and downward contributions cancel. ✅
B. Net work is positive because force always points upward.
C. Net work is negative because gravity dominates.
D. Net work accumulates linearly with number of cycles.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Upward journey: \( W_{up} = \int_0^h kz \, dz = kh^2/2 \). Downward: \( W_{down} = \int_h^0 kz \, dz = -kh^2/2 \). Sum = 0 per cycle. Despite force being position-dependent and nonzero, the conservative nature ensures cyclic work vanishes. This contrasts with dissipative forces where cyclic work is negative, highlighting how conservativeness manifests in periodic motion regardless of force complexity.

Students frequently apply it to punctured domains, obtaining incorrect zero work when actual circulation is nonzero, missing topological constraints.

Q26. In modeling atmospheric drag \( \vec{F}_d = -k|\vec{v}|\vec{v} \), why is work always negative for any non-trivial trajectory?

A. Because drag coefficient k is defined as negative.
B. Because \( \vec{F}_d \cdot \vec{v} = -k|\vec{v}|^3 < 0 \) for all \( \vec{v} \neq 0 \). ✅
C. Because atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude.
D. Because trajectories in atmosphere are always descending.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Drag opposes velocity by construction: \( \vec{F}_d = -k|\vec{v}|\hat{v} = -k|\vec{v}|\vec{v}/|\vec{v}| \). Dot product: \( \vec{F}_d \cdot \vec{v} = -k|\vec{v}|(\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v})/|\vec{v}| = -k|\vec{v}|^2 \). Wait—actually \( \vec{F}_d = -k|\vec{v}|\vec{v} \) gives \( \vec{F}_d \cdot \vec{v} = -k|\vec{v}|(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}) = -k|\vec{v}|^3 \). Either way, strictly negative for nonzero velocity. This guarantees energy dissipation, modeling irreversibility fundamentally through the mathematical structure of the force law itself.

Q27. A challenging scenario: \( \vec{F} = \langle \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}, 0 \rangle \) on \( \mathbb{R}^3 \setminus z\text{-axis} \). Compute work around unit circle in xy-plane. Despite \( \nabla \times \vec{F} = \vec{0} \) everywhere in domain, why isn't work zero?

C. Circle encloses singularity where field is undefined, violating theorem assumptions.
D. Both B and C are correct explanations. ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Algebraically \( \nabla \times \vec{F} = \vec{0} \) where defined, but domain excludes z-axis, creating a hole. The potential \( \theta = \arctan(y/x) \) is multi-valued, increasing by \( 2\pi \) per revolution. Work = \( \oint d\theta = 2\pi \neq 0 \). This classic example shows curl-free ≠ conservative when topology prevents global potential definition. Students must recognize that vector calculus theorems have topological preconditions beyond local differential conditions, linking analysis to geometry profoundly.
