What is Determinants for cross product?

Definition:
The symbolic 3×3 determinant for cross product is not a true determinant but a formal device encoding cofactor expansion; actual value requires treating first row as basis vectors rather than scalars.

Example:
Evaluating \( \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \) expands as \( \hat{i}(0-6) - \hat{j}(1-0) + \hat{k}(3-0) = \langle -6, -1, 3 \rangle \).

Reason:
Understanding this notation prevents misinterpretation as scalar volume and clarifies why cross product is vector-valued, avoiding errors in torque or area computations.

Q1. A student computes the determinant of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix representing three vectors in space and obtains zero. They conclude the vectors are linearly independent because the system has a trivial solution. What is the fundamental error in this reasoning?

A. Zero determinant implies unique solution, hence independence.
B. A zero determinant indicates the vectors span a volume, confirming independence.
C. The student confused singular matrices with invertible ones; zero determinant means dependent vectors and non-trivial null space. ✅
D. Determinants only apply to square systems, so the conclusion about independence is invalid regardless of value.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The core misconception is equating a zero determinant with independence. In reality, \(\det(A) = 0\) signifies that the column vectors are linearly dependent, the matrix is singular, and the homogeneous system has infinitely many non-trivial solutions. This error analysis requires understanding the geometric and algebraic implications of singularity beyond mere computation.

Q2. Given three points \(A(1,2,3)\), \(B(4,5,6)\), and \(C(7,8,k)\), for what value of \(k\) do these points fail to define a unique plane, and what does this imply about the determinant of vectors \(\vec{AB}\) and \(\vec{AC}\)?

A. \(k=9\); the scalar triple product with any third vector is zero.
B. \(k=6\); the \(2 \times 2\) minor determinant vanishes.
C. \(k=9\); the vectors are collinear, making any \(3 \times 3\) determinant involving them zero. ✅
D. No such \(k\) exists; three distinct points always define a plane.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Three points fail to define a unique plane only if they are collinear. Vectors \(\vec{AB} = \langle 3,3,3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{AC} = \langle 6,6,k-3 \rangle\) must be parallel, requiring \(k-3=6\) so \(k=9\). This makes the vectors linearly dependent, causing any determinant formed with them to vanish, illustrating multi-step geometric-algebraic reasoning.

Starting with \(\det(A)=5\), the first operation keeps it at 5, and the swap changes sign to −5. This tests conceptual understanding of operation effects rather than brute-force calculation.

Thus, torque (a cross product) dotted with the plane’s normal yields zero, integrating geometry, algebra, and physical interpretation.

A. Cramer’s Rule cannot be applied when \(\det(A) = 0\); the system is inconsistent.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis question targets a common misconception. When \(\det(A) = 0\), Cramer’s Rule is invalid. If any \(\det(A_i) \neq 0\), the system is inconsistent (no solution). Infinitely many solutions occur only if all \(\det(A_i) = 0\). Recognizing this distinction requires deep conceptual understanding of linear systems beyond formula application.

Q10. A student claims that if \(\det(A + B) = \det(A) + \det(B)\) for two \(2 \times 2\) matrices, then determinants are linear functions. Provide a counterexample and explain why this property fails generally.

A. Let \(A=B=I\); then \(\det(2I)=4 \neq 2=\det(I)+\det(I)\). Determinants are multilinear in rows, not additive over matrices. ✅
B. Use zero matrices; the equality holds trivially, proving linearity.
C. Determinants are linear only for diagonal matrices; the claim is conditionally true.
D. The property holds for all \(2 \times 2\) matrices; the student is correct.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This error analysis targets the pervasive misconception that determinants are linear. While multilinear in individual rows/columns, \(\det(A+B) \neq \det(A)+\det(B)\) generally. The counterexample \(A=B=I_2\) gives \(\det(2I)=4\) vs. \(1+1=2\). Understanding this distinction is crucial for advanced linear algebra and prevents flawed reasoning in proofs and applications involving matrix sums.

Q11. Given vectors \(\vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with scalar triple product 6, if \(\vec{w}\) is replaced by \(2\vec{w} + \vec{u}\), what is the new scalar triple product, and which determinant property justifies this without recomputation?

A. 12; multilinearity allows factoring scalars and ignoring added parallel components. ✅
B. 6; adding a multiple of one vector to another doesn’t change the triple product.
C. 18; both scaling and addition contribute additively.
D. 0; the new set is linearly dependent.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The scalar triple product equals \(\det[\vec{u}\ \vec{v}\ \vec{w}]\). Replacing \(\vec{w}\) with \(2\vec{w}+\vec{u}\) uses multilinearity: \(\det[\vec{u}\ \vec{v}\ 2\vec{w}+\vec{u}] = 2\det[\vec{u}\ \vec{v}\ \vec{w}] + \det[\vec{u}\ \vec{v}\ \vec{u}]\). The second term is zero (repeated columns), leaving \(2 \times 6 = 12\). This multi-step reasoning applies determinant properties conceptually rather than computationally.

A. The adjugate matrix contains cofactors, which are integer determinants of submatrices when entries are integers. ✅
B. Integer matrices always have integer inverses regardless of determinant.
C. The inverse is computed via row reduction, which preserves integrality when \(\det=1\).
D. Only symmetric integer matrices with unit determinant have integer inverses.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This Olympiad-style question requires synthesizing multiple concepts. Since \(A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \text{adj}(A)\) and \(\det(A)=1\), we get \(A^{-1} = \text{adj}(A)\). Cofactors are determinants of \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) integer submatrices, hence integers. This elegant proof hinges on understanding the adjugate formula and properties of integer arithmetic, representing high-level mathematical reasoning beyond standard curriculum.

Q16. Which of the following best explains why the determinant of a rotation matrix in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is always +1 rather than −1?

A. Rotation matrices are orthogonal with positive orientation preservation; \(\det = \pm 1\), and continuity from identity fixes +1. ✅
B. All orthogonal matrices have determinant +1 by definition.
C. The trace of a rotation matrix determines its determinant via \(\det = \text{tr}/3\).
D. Rotation matrices are symmetric, forcing positive determinant.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This conceptual question probes deeper understanding of matrix groups. Orthogonal matrices satisfy \(Q^T Q = I\), implying \(\det(Q) = \pm 1\). Rotation matrices form the connected component containing \(I\) (which has \(\det=1\)), and since determinant is continuous, it cannot jump to −1 without passing through non-orthogonal matrices. Reflections have \(\det=-1\) but aren’t rotations. This reasoning integrates topology, algebra, and geometry.

Q17. A \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}\) describes planar rotation. Without direct computation, use properties of determinants to find \(\frac{d}{dt}\det(A(t))\) at \(t=0\).

📖 Explanation:

Alternatively, Jacobi’s formula gives \(\frac{d}{dt}\det(A) = \det(A) \cdot \text{tr}(A^{-1} A')\), and \(A^{-1}A'\) is skew-symmetric with zero trace. Both approaches avoid differentiation of trig functions, showcasing advanced determinant properties.
