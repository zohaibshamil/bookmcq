What is Line through point with direction vector?

Definition:
Specifying a point \( P_0 \) and nonzero direction vector \( \vec{d} \) uniquely determines a line in 3D as set \( \{ P_0 + t\vec{d} \mid t \in \mathbb{R} \} \), combining location and orientation.

Example:
Given point \( (0,1,-2) \) and direction \( \langle 3,0,1 \rangle \), the line includes points like \( (3,1,-1) \) at \( t=1 \) and \( (-3,1,-3) \) at \( t=-1 \).

Reason:
This minimal specification avoids redundant constraints and directly supports vector-based algorithms for collision detection, camera positioning, and path interpolation.

Q1. A line \( L \) passes through point \( P(1, 2, 3) \) with direction vector \( \vec{v} = \langle 2, -1, 4 \rangle \). A student claims the symmetric equations are \( \frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y+2}{-1} = \frac{z-3}{4} \). What is the fundamental error in this formulation?

A.The direction numbers were inverted.
B.The sign of the y-coordinate in the numerator is incorrect; it should be \( y-2 \). ✅
C.The denominator for z should be negative.
D.Symmetric equations cannot be formed when a direction component is non-zero.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The symmetric form requires subtracting the coordinate of the given point from the variable. Since the point is \( (1,2,3) \), the y-term must be \( y-2 \), not \( y+2 \). Using \( y+2 \) implies the line passes through \( y=-2 \), which contradicts the given point \( P \). This tests attention to algebraic structure over rote memorization.

Q2. Given line \( L_1: \vec{r} = \langle 1,0,2 \rangle + t\langle 3,1,-1 \rangle \) and point \( Q(4,1,1) \), which condition must be satisfied for \( Q \) to lie on \( L_1 \)?

A.There exists a single scalar \( t \) satisfying all three parametric equations simultaneously. ✅
B.The vector \( \vec{PQ} \) must be perpendicular to the direction vector.
C.The distance from \( Q \) to any point on \( L_1 \) must be zero.
D.The cross product of \( \vec{PQ} \) and the direction vector must equal the direction vector.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: For a point to lie on a line defined parametrically, the same parameter value \( t \) must satisfy the x, y, and z equations concurrently. If different values of \( t \) are required for each coordinate, the point is not on the line. This distinguishes between spatial proximity and actual incidence, addressing a common conceptual gap.

Q3. Two lines are defined as \( L_1: \vec{r}_1 = \langle 0,1,0 \rangle + s\langle 1,2,3 \rangle \) and \( L_2: \vec{r}_2 = \langle 1,3,3 \rangle + t\langle 2,4,6 \rangle \). Which statement best describes their geometric relationship?

A.They intersect at exactly one point.
B.They are skew lines.
C.They are distinct parallel lines. ✅
D.They are coincident (the same line).
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The direction vectors are scalar multiples (\( \langle 2,4,6 \rangle = 2\langle 1,2,3 \rangle \)), indicating parallelism. To distinguish between coincident and distinct parallel lines, check if a point from \( L_2 \) lies on \( L_1 \). Solving \( \langle 1,3,3 \rangle = \langle 0,1,0 \rangle + s\langle 1,2,3 \rangle \) yields inconsistent \( s \) values, proving they are distinct parallel lines rather than identical.

Q4. A drone flies along a path modeled by \( \vec{r}(t) = \langle 5, 10, 20 \rangle + t\langle -1, 2, -3 \rangle \) where \( t \) is time in seconds. At what time does the drone reach an altitude of \( z=8 \), and is this physically meaningful within the model's domain \( t \geq 0 \)?

A.\( t=4 \); yes, it occurs during flight. ✅
B.\( t=-4 \); no, it occurred before launch.
C.\( t=4 \); no, the drone crashes at that altitude.
D.\( t=12 \); yes, but only if direction reverses.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Set \( z(t) = 20 - 3t = 8 \) to find \( t=4 \). Since \( t=4 \geq 0 \), it is within the valid domain. This application question integrates physical constraints with vector parametrization, requiring students to validate mathematical solutions against real-world boundary conditions rather than accepting algebraic results uncritically.

Q5. Consider the line passing through \( A(2, -1, 4) \) with direction \( \vec{d} = \langle a, b, c \rangle \). If the line is perpendicular to the plane \( 3x - y + 2z = 7 \), what constraint does this impose on the direction vector components?

A.\( a=3, b=-1, c=2 \) exactly.
B.\( \langle a,b,c \rangle \) must be orthogonal to \( \langle 3,-1,2 \rangle \).
C.\( \langle a,b,c \rangle \) must be parallel to \( \langle 3,-1,2 \rangle \), so \( a/3 = b/(-1) = c/2 \). ✅
D.No constraint exists; any direction works.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: A line perpendicular to a plane must have its direction vector parallel to the plane’s normal vector \( \langle 3,-1,2 \rangle \). Parallel vectors are scalar multiples, leading to proportional components. Students often confuse perpendicularity of line-to-plane with orthogonality of vectors; this question targets that specific misconception through mixed concept integration.

Q6. A student derives the vector equation of a line through \( P(3,0,-1) \) and \( Q(3,0,5) \) as \( \vec{r} = \langle 3,0,-1 \rangle + t\langle 0,0,0 \rangle \). Why is this representation invalid despite using correct points?

B.The points are identical, so no line exists.
C.The z-coordinates should have been swapped.
D.Vector equations require unit direction vectors.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The direction vector \( \vec{PQ} = \langle 0,0,6 \rangle \), not \( \langle 0,0,0 \rangle \). The student likely subtracted incorrectly or assumed identical coordinates imply zero displacement. A zero direction vector collapses the parametric equation to a single point, violating the definition of a line. This error analysis question reinforces foundational vector arithmetic prerequisites.

Q7. In a 3D graph, line \( L \) appears to pass through the origin and extend diagonally upward. Its projection onto the xy-plane is the line \( y=x \), and onto the xz-plane is \( z=2x \). Which vector equation correctly represents \( L \)?

A.\( \vec{r} = t\langle 1,1,2 \rangle \) ✅
B.\( \vec{r} = t\langle 1,1,1 \rangle \)
C.\( \vec{r} = \langle 0,0,0 \rangle + t\langle 2,2,1 \rangle \)
D.\( \vec{r} = t\langle 1,2,1 \rangle \)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Graph interpretation requires synthesizing projections. The xy-projection \( y=x \) implies equal x and y components. The xz-projection \( z=2x \) implies z is twice x. Thus, direction ratios are \( 1:1:2 \). Option A satisfies both. This tests spatial reasoning beyond symbolic manipulation, aligning with graph-based HOTS requirements.

Q8. Line \( L \) has direction vector \( \vec{v} = \langle 1, -2, 3 \rangle \). Another line \( M \) has direction \( \vec{w} = \langle -2, 4, -6 \rangle \). A student argues they cannot be parallel because \( \vec{w} \neq \vec{v} \). How would you refute this using vector properties?

A.Parallelism requires identical vectors, so the student is correct.
B.Vectors are parallel if one is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other; here \( \vec{w} = -2\vec{v} \). ✅
C.Only unit vectors can be compared for parallelism.
D.Direction vectors must have positive components to indicate parallelism.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Parallel vectors need not be identical; scalar multiplication preserves direction (or reverses it if negative). Since \( \vec{w} = -2\vec{v} \), they are collinear. This direct recall question addresses a pervasive misconception that visual or numerical dissimilarity negates parallelism, reinforcing the formal definition over intuitive judgment.

Q9. A navigation system models two aircraft paths as \( \vec{r}_A = \langle 0,0,10 \rangle + t\langle 1,0,0 \rangle \) and \( \vec{r}_B = \langle 5,0,10 \rangle + s\langle 0,1,0 \rangle \). Do these paths intersect, and if so, do the aircraft collide?

A.Paths intersect at \( (5,0,10) \), and collision occurs if \( t=s=5 \).
B.Paths intersect at \( (5,0,10) \), but collision depends on whether both aircraft occupy that point at the same time parameter. ✅
C.Paths are skew and never meet.
D.Paths are parallel and maintain constant separation.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Spatial intersection occurs when position vectors are equal for some \( t,s \); solving gives \( t=5, s=0 \) at \( (5,0,10) \). However, collision requires simultaneous occupancy, meaning the same time value. Since parameters represent independent times, intersection ≠ collision. This scenario-based question separates geometric coincidence from temporal dynamics, crucial in applied modeling.

📖 Explanation: Option A uses half the original direction vector, which is valid since direction can be scaled. Option B uses a point obtained at \( t=1 \) in the original line and the negative direction vector, which traces the same set of points backward. Both represent identical geometric lines. This tests understanding that lines are sets of points, not tied to specific parametrizations.

Q12. Given point \( P(0,0,0) \) and direction \( \vec{v} = \langle 1,1,1 \rangle \), compare the efficiency of finding the point on the line closest to \( Q(1,0,0) \) using projection versus calculus minimization. Which method is more direct and why?

A.Calculus is better because it avoids vector operations.
B.Projection is more direct: minimize distance via orthogonality condition \( (\vec{PQ} - t\vec{v}) \cdot \vec{v} = 0 \). ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Projection leverages geometric insight: the shortest distance occurs when the connecting vector is orthogonal to the line. This yields a linear equation in \( t \). Calculus requires minimizing a quadratic function, involving derivatives. For lines, projection is inherently simpler. This comparative analysis question evaluates method selection based on structural understanding, not just procedural fluency.

Q13. A line is defined by two points \( A(1,2,3) \) and \( B(4,5,6) \). A third point \( C(7,8,9) \) is claimed to be collinear. Without computing distances, how can you verify collinearity using vectors?

A.Check if \( \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \vec{0} \).
B.Check if \( \vec{AB} \cdot \vec{AC} = |\vec{AB}||\vec{AC}| \).
C.Verify that \( C \) satisfies the symmetric equations derived from \( A \) and \( B \).
D.All of the above are valid methods. ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Cross product zero indicates parallel vectors sharing a point, hence collinearity. Dot product equality implies angle zero, also indicating alignment. Symmetric equations test parametric consistency. All three are mathematically equivalent verification strategies. This mixed-concept question encourages flexible thinking about collinearity beyond a single algorithmic approach.

This application question tests recognition of linearity criteria in dynamic systems, preventing misclassification of trajectories.

Q18. Two lines \( L_1: \vec{r} = \vec{a} + t\vec{u} \) and \( L_2: \vec{r} = \vec{b} + s\vec{v} \) are given. Under what condition are they guaranteed to be coplanar?

If directions are parallel (\( \vec{u}\times\vec{v}=0 \)), they’re automatically coplanar, but the triple product condition covers both parallel and intersecting cases universally. This advanced application integrates multiple vector operations for comprehensive spatial reasoning.

This multi-step problem combines line parametrization with absolute value geometry, requiring careful setup.
