📝 Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinate Systems

📖 From Calculus • 12. Three Dimensional Space: Vectors • 27 questions available

What is Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinate Systems?

Definition:
Cylindrical: \( x=r\cos\theta, y=r\sin\theta, z=z \) with \( r\geq0, \theta\in[0,2\pi) \); Spherical: \( x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, z=\rho\cos\phi \) with \( \rho\geq0, \theta\in[0,2\pi), \phi\in[0,\pi] \).

Example:
Cylinder \( x^2+y^2=4 \) becomes \( r=2 \) in cylindrical; sphere \( x^2+y^2+z^2=9 \) becomes \( \rho=3 \) in spherical.

Reason:
Coordinate transformations reduce complex Cartesian equations to simple constants, dramatically easing integration and revealing inherent symmetries in physical systems.

Q1. A particle moves along a path defined in cylindrical coordinates by \( r = 2\theta \) and \( z = \theta^2 \). If \( \theta \) increases linearly with time, which statement best describes the vertical acceleration component relative to the radial distance?

A. Vertical acceleration is constant regardless of radial position.
B. Vertical acceleration increases quadratically as radial distance increases linearly. ✅
C. Vertical acceleration decreases inversely with radial distance.
D. Vertical acceleration remains zero because \( z \) depends only on angle.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This requires multi-step reasoning linking parametric dependence. Since \( z = \theta^2 \) and \( r = 2\theta \), substituting gives \( z = r^2/4 \). Differentiating twice with respect to time reveals that vertical acceleration scales with both angular velocity squared and radial growth, demonstrating non-linear coupling between coordinates often missed in direct computation.

Q2. When converting the Cartesian equation \( x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4z \) to spherical coordinates, a student obtains \( \rho = 4\sin\phi \). What is the fundamental error in this conversion?

A. The student confused \( \cos\phi \) with \( \sin\phi \) for the z-substitution. ✅
B. The student failed to factor out \( \rho \) before dividing.
C. The student incorrectly assumed \( \rho \neq 0 \) and lost the origin solution.
D. The student used cylindrical radius instead of spherical radius.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

In spherical coordinates, \( z = \rho\cos\phi \), not \( \rho\sin\phi \). The correct substitution yields \( \rho^2 = 4\rho\cos\phi \), simplifying to \( \rho = 4\cos\phi \). This error analysis question targets a pervasive misconception where learners mix up trigonometric components due to over-reliance on memorization without geometric visualization of the polar angle from the positive z-axis.

Q3. Consider two surfaces: Surface A defined by \( \phi = \pi/3 \) in spherical coordinates and Surface B defined by \( z = \sqrt{3}r \) in cylindrical coordinates. How do these surfaces relate geometrically?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Converting \( \phi = \pi/3 \) gives \( z = \rho\cos(\pi/3) = \rho/2 \) and \( r = \rho\sin(\pi/3) = \rho\sqrt{3}/2 \), so \( z/r = 1/\sqrt{3} \) or \( z = r/\sqrt{3} \). However, \( z = \sqrt{3}r \) implies a steeper slope. Re-evaluating shows \( \tan\phi = r/z = \sqrt{3} \), meaning \( z = r/\sqrt{3} \). Thus option A is incorrect; actually \( z=\sqrt{3}r \) corresponds to \( \phi=\pi/6 \). The correct relationship requires careful trigonometric verification, testing conceptual precision beyond formula plugging.

Q21. If a curve in spherical coordinates satisfies \( \rho = \sin\phi \) and \( \theta = \text{constant} \), what is its projection onto the xy-plane?

Q24. Consider the transformation from spherical to cylindrical: \( r = \rho\sin\phi, z = \rho\cos\phi \). If \( \rho = f(\phi) \), under what condition does the resulting cylindrical curve have horizontal tangent?

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

This Olympiad-level problem demands careful chain rule application and sign tracking in parametric derivatives, testing analytical rigor beyond standard conversions.

Q27. Why can’t the entire 3D space be covered smoothly by a single spherical coordinate chart?

A. Because ρ=0 creates a coordinate singularity where angular variables are undefined. ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

At ρ=0, all (θ,φ) map to same point, violating diffeomorphism requirement for smooth charts. This is a topological obstruction: S² cannot be covered by one chart, and radial extension inherits this. While ρ>0 excludes origin, even excluding origin, the angular part still has singularities at poles.
