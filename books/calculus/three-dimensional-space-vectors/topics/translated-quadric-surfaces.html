📝 Translated quadric surfaces

📖 From Calculus • 12. Three Dimensional Space: Vectors • 26 questions available

What is Translated quadric surfaces?

Definition:
Translation replaces \( x,y,z \) with \( (x-h),(y-k),(z-l) \) to shift center/vertex to \( (h,k,l) \), preserving shape but changing position, e.g., \( \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \cdots = 1 \).

Example:
Sphere \( (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + (z-3)^2 = 9 \) is unit sphere scaled by 3 and centered at \( (2,-1,3) \) instead of origin.

Reason:
Real-world objects rarely center at origin; translation adapts standard forms to practical coordinates, essential in CAD, geospatial modeling, and fitting data to theoretical surfaces.

Q1. A student translates the ellipsoid \( \frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 + z^2 = 1 \) by vector \( \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle \) but writes \( \frac{(x+2)^2}{4} + (y-1)^2 + (z+3)^2 = 1 \). Which cognitive error best explains this result?

A. Confusing translation direction with coordinate substitution sign convention ✅
B. Incorrectly applying scaling factors before translation
C. Misidentifying the center of the original surface
D. Applying rotation matrix instead of translation vector

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The student applied the opposite signs in the substitution. Translating by \( \langle h,k,l \rangle \) requires replacing \( x \) with \( x-h \), not \( x+h \). This is a common sign-reversal misconception when students memorize formulas without understanding that the new coordinates must satisfy the original equation relative to the shifted origin.

Q2. If the hyperboloid \( x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1 \) is translated so its center moves to \( (1,2,-3) \), which geometric property remains invariant under this rigid transformation?

A. The orientation of the asymptotic cone
B. The location of the vertices
C. The distance from center to foci along the axis
D. Both A and C ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Translation preserves all intrinsic metric properties including shape, size, and orientation. The asymptotic cone’s orientation and focal distances are invariant because they depend only on coefficients, not position. Vertices change location but maintain relative positioning. Understanding invariance distinguishes rigid motions from deformations in quadric surface analysis.

Q3. An engineer models a cooling tower as \( \frac{(x-5)^2}{9} + \frac{(z+2)^2}{9} - \frac{(y-4)^2}{16} = 1 \). To reposition it so the narrowest cross-section lies in the plane \( y=0 \), what translation vector is required?

A. \( \langle 0, -4, 0 \rangle \)
B. \( \langle 0, 4, 0 \rangle \) ✅
C. \( \langle -5, 0, 2 \rangle \)
D. \( \langle 5, -4, -2 \rangle \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The narrowest cross-section of a hyperboloid of one sheet occurs at its center where \( y=4 \). Moving this to \( y=0 \) requires subtracting 4 from y-coordinates, meaning we add 4 to the variable: replace \( y \) with \( y-4 \) becomes \( y \), requiring translation vector \( \langle 0,4,0 \rangle \). This tests application of geometric features to real-world repositioning.

Q4. Given two paraboloids \( P_1: z = x^2 + y^2 \) and \( P_2: z-3 = (x-1)^2 + (y+2)^2 \), which statement correctly compares their geometric relationship?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Comparing equations shows P2 results from substituting \( x \to x-1 \), \( y \to y+2 \), \( z \to z-3 \) in P1, confirming pure translation by \( \langle 1,-2,3 \rangle \). Translation preserves all differential properties including Gaussian curvature and focal parameters. Students often mistakenly believe position affects shape; this question targets that conceptual gap through direct comparison.

Q5. A quadric surface has equation \( 4(x-1)^2 + 9(y+2)^2 + z^2 = 36 \). After translating to standard position centered at origin, what is the length of the semi-axis along the y-direction?

A. 2 ✅
B. 3
C. 4
D. 6

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Dividing by 36 gives \( \frac{(x-1)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+2)^2}{4} + \frac{z^2}{36} = 1 \). Translation doesn’t alter axis lengths; the y-semi-axis is \( \sqrt{4} = 2 \). This direct recall question verifies foundational knowledge that translation affects only position terms, not denominators representing squared semi-axes. Essential baseline for higher-order problems involving transformed quadrics.

Q7. A satellite dish is modeled by \( z = \frac{1}{8}(x^2 + y^2) \). If installed at height 10m above ground with focus at receiver located at \( (0,0,12) \), what translation was applied to the standard paraboloid \( z = \frac{1}{8}(x^2 + y^2) \)?

A. \( \langle 0,0,10 \rangle \) ✅
B. \( \langle 0,0,2 \rangle \)
C. \( \langle 0,0,8 \rangle \)
D. No translation; focus adjustment changes parameter

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Standard paraboloid \( z = \frac{1}{4p}(x^2+y^2) \) has focus at \( (0,0,p) \). Here \( p=2 \), so unshifted focus is at \( z=2 \). Receiver at \( z=12 \) implies vertical shift of 10 units. Thus translation vector is \( \langle 0,0,10 \rangle \). This multi-step problem combines focal property knowledge with translation application in engineering context, testing integrated modeling skills.

This graph-based interpretation connects visual pattern recognition to algebraic parameters, assessing ability to extract translation information from projected views rather than explicit equations.

Q10. A hyperbolic paraboloid \( z = xy \) is translated to \( z-2 = (x-1)(y+3) \). What is the new location of the saddle point?

A. \( (1,-3,2) \) ✅
B. \( (1,3,2) \)
C. \( (-1,3,-2) \)
D. \( (1,-3,-2) \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The saddle point of \( z=xy \) is at origin. Substituting \( X=x-1 \), \( Y=y+3 \), \( Z=z-2 \) transforms to \( Z=XY \), whose saddle is at \( X=Y=Z=0 \). Solving gives \( x=1, y=-3, z=2 \). This requires understanding that saddle points translate like centers despite lacking symmetry axes, testing nuanced grasp of critical point behavior under affine transformations.

But wait—recalculating: \( \frac{(z-10)^2}{25} \leq 1 \) implies \( z \in [5,15] \). At \( z=0 \), LHS >1 always. Actually, no intersection exists. Correction: Option A is correct. This trick question tests careful domain analysis before computation, exposing tendency to mechanically solve without checking feasibility—a critical HOTS in modeling validation.

Q20. Olympiad Challenge: Find all vectors \( \vec{v} \) such that translating \( x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1 \) by \( \vec{v} \) yields a surface containing the line \( x=t, y=t, z=t \). Express answer parametrically.

Coefficient of \( t^2 \) is 1≠0, impossible unless... Wait, recalculate: coefficient is 1, so identity requires 1=0 contradiction. But if line lies on surface, equation must hold identically. Since leading coefficient ≠0, no solution exists. However, reconsider: maybe specific t values? No, “containing the line” means all t. Thus D seems correct. But let’s verify special case: if a=b=c, then expression becomes \( t^2 - 2at + (2a^2 - a^2 -1) = t^2 -2at + (a^2-1) \), still quadratic. Indeed, no translation works because hyperboloid contains no straight lines through arbitrary directions? Actually hyperboloid of one sheet contains two families of lines, but not necessarily this diagonal. Final answer: D. This Olympiad-style problem demands rigorous identity analysis and knowledge of ruled surface properties, pushing beyond standard curriculum.
