What is Vector from magnitude and direction?

Definition:
A vector with known magnitude \( m \) and direction unit vector \( \hat{u} \) is reconstructed as \( \vec{v} = m \hat{u} \); in 2D with angle \( \theta \), this becomes \( \vec{v} = m\langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta \rangle \).

Example:
A force of 10 N at \( 30^\circ \) above horizontal is \( \vec{F} = 10\langle \cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ \rangle = \langle 5\sqrt{3}, 5 \rangle \) N.

Reason:
This synthesis bridges polar/geometric descriptions with Cartesian computation, essential for resolving applied forces or velocities into analyzable components.

Q1. A navigation system defines a drone's position using spherical coordinates where the radial distance is fixed at \( r = 10 \) m. If the azimuthal angle \( \theta \) increases while the polar angle \( \phi \) remains constant at \( \pi/4 \), which statement best describes the resulting path of the vector tip in three-dimensional space?

A. The vector traces a great circle passing through the poles.
B. The vector traces a horizontal circle parallel to the xy-plane with radius \( 10\sin(\pi/4) \). ✅
C. The vector traces a vertical semicircle in a plane containing the z-axis.
D. The vector moves along a straight line radiating from the origin.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

This question tests conceptual understanding of spherical coordinate geometry without direct computation. When \( \phi \) is fixed, the vector maintains a constant angle with the z-axis, creating a cone. The intersection of this cone with the sphere of radius 10 forms a horizontal circle. Students often confuse constant \( \phi \) with constant \( \theta \), leading to misconceptions about great circles versus latitude lines.

Q2. An engineer models two force vectors \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \) with equal magnitude \( F \). 📖 Explanation:

This application problem requires multi-step reasoning connecting individual axis angles to the inter-vector angle. The resultant magnitude formula \( R^2 = A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\gamma \) yields \( \cos\gamma = 0.5 \), so \( \gamma = 60^\circ \). However, students must recognize that \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are measured from different axes, making the relationship non-trivial. Distractors exploit confusion between axis-referenced angles and the actual angle between vectors.

Q4. Given a vector \( \vec{v} \) with magnitude 8 and direction angles \( \alpha = 60^\circ \), \( \beta = 60^\circ \), find all possible values for the third direction angle \( \gamma \) and explain why multiple solutions may exist.

Both positive and negative roots satisfy the equation, corresponding to vectors pointing above or below the xy-plane. Many students forget that direction angles can be obtuse (up to \( 180^\circ \)), leading them to discard the valid \( 135^\circ \) solution.

Q22. A vector \( \vec{t} \) has magnitude \( \sqrt{3} \) and equal direction angles. A transformation scales the x-component by 2, y by 3, z by 4, producing \( \vec{t}' \). What are the new direction angles of \( \vec{t}' \), and why can’t they be found by simply scaling the original angles?

A. New angles are \( \arccos(2/\sqrt{29}), \arccos(3/\sqrt{29}), \arccos(4/\sqrt{29}) \); angles don’t scale linearly because direction depends on component ratios, not absolute values ✅
B. New angles are \( 2\alpha, 3\alpha, 4\alpha \) where \( \alpha = \arccos(1/\sqrt{3}) \); scaling applies multiplicatively to angles
C. Original angles remain unchanged because direction is invariant under anisotropic scaling
D. New angles cannot be determined without knowing the sign of original components

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This challenging problem contrasts linear transformations with angular properties. Original equal angles imply components \( (1,1,1) \) (since \( \sqrt{3} \cdot 1/\sqrt{3} = 1 \)). Scaled: \( (2,3,4) \), magnitude \( \sqrt{29} \). New direction cosines are components divided by new magnitude. Angles are nonlinear functions of components; doubling x doesn’t double \( \alpha \). This exposes the fallacy of treating angles as linear quantities under transformation, requiring ratio-based recalculation.

Q23. In a dataset of 1000 random unit vectors, a histogram of \( \gamma \) (polar angle) shows uniform distribution. A colleague expects clustering near \( 90^\circ \) based on ‘equator bias’. Why is the observed uniformity actually incorrect for isotropic sampling in 3D?

Q24. A vector \( \vec{r} \) has magnitude 15 and direction angles \( \alpha=80^\circ, \beta=80^\circ \). A student computes \( \gamma = \arccos(\sqrt{1 - 2\cos^2 80^\circ}) \approx 76.4^\circ \). Q25. Given that vector \( \vec{a} \) has direction angles \( (40^\circ, 50^\circ, \gamma) \) and vector \( \vec{b} \) has \( (50^\circ, 40^\circ, \gamma) \) with same \( \gamma \), and both have magnitude 10, what can be concluded about their z-components and the angle between them without full computation?

A. Z-components are equal; angle between them depends only on the swap of \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \), and can be found via \( \cos\psi = 2\cos40^\circ\cos50^\circ + \cos^2\gamma \) ✅
B. Z-components differ because \( \gamma \) is derived differently for each vector
C. Angle between them is \( 90^\circ \) due to swapped angles
D. Z-components are equal, but angle cannot be determined without knowing \( \gamma \) explicitly

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This mixed reasoning problem exploits symmetry. Since \( \alpha_a = \beta_b \) and \( \beta_a = \alpha_b \), and \( \gamma \) is same, \( \cos^2\gamma \) is identical for both (as \( \cos^2 40 + \cos^2 50 = \cos^2 50 + \cos^2 40 \)). Thus \( \gamma \) is same, so \( z \)-components equal. Dot product: \( \cos\psi = \cos40\cos50 + \cos50\cos40 + \cos^2\gamma = 2\cos40\cos50 + \cos^2\gamma \). No need to solve for \( \gamma \). This showcases leveraging symmetry to avoid redundant calculation.

Q26. An Olympiad problem states: ‘Find the minimum possible angle between two unit vectors whose direction angles each satisfy \( \alpha \geq 60^\circ, \beta \geq 60^\circ, \gamma \geq 60^\circ \).’ A solver argues the minimum is \( 0^\circ \) (same vector). Why is this invalid, and what is the true minimum?

A. Invalid because no unit vector can have all direction angles \( \geq 60^\circ \); the feasible set is empty ✅
B. Valid; the zero angle is achievable at the boundary
C. Invalid because the constraints define a region on the sphere, and the minimum angle between distinct points in this region is positive; the smallest occurs at vertices like \( (60^\circ,60^\circ,60^\circ) \), giving angle \( 0^\circ \) only for identical vectors, but if distinctness is implied, minimum is between adjacent vertices
D. The constraints allow vectors like \( (60^\circ,60^\circ,60^\circ) \), so minimum angle is indeed \( 0^\circ \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

This Olympiad-style question tests feasibility before optimization. Check: \( \cos^2 60^\circ = 0.25 \); sum for three angles ≥ 0.75. But equality requires exactly \( 60^\circ \) each, and \( 3 \times 0.25 = 0.75 < 1 \). Wait—this is feasible! \( \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 0.75 < 1 \), so room exists. Actually, \( (60,60,60) \) gives sum 0.75, so \( \cos^2\gamma \) could be larger. To have all ≥60°, max \( \cos^2 = 0.25 \), so max sum = 0.75 < 1. Contradiction! No unit vector satisfies all three ≥60° because sum of squares would be ≤0.75 < 1. Thus feasible set is empty. Solver missed this impossibility. True answer: no such vectors exist. Option A correctly identifies emptiness.
