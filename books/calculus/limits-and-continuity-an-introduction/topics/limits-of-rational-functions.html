What is Limits of rational functions?

Definition:
The limit of a rational function f(x)=P(x)Q(x) f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} f(x)=Q(x)P(x)​, where P P P and Q Q Q are polynomials, as x→a x \to a x→a is evaluated by direct substitution if Q(a)≠0 Q(a) \neq 0 Q(a)=0, yielding P(a)Q(a) \frac{P(a)}{Q(a)} Q(a)P(a)​. If Q(a)=0 Q(a) = 0 Q(a)=0 but P(a)≠0 P(a) \neq 0 P(a)=0, the limit is infinite or does not exist; if both are zero, factorization or cancellation is needed to resolve the indeterminate form.

Example:
Evaluate lim⁡x→2x2−4x−2 \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x-2} limx→2​x−2x2−4​.
Solution: Direct substitution gives 0/0 0/0 0/0, so factor: (x−2)(x+2)x−2=x+2 \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x+2 x−2(x−2)(x+2)​=x+2, then lim⁡x→2(x+2)=4 \lim_{x \to 2} (x+2) = 4 limx→2​(x+2)=4.

Reason:
Understanding rational function limits is essential because they appear frequently in calculus, and handling cases where the denominator vanishes teaches algebraic manipulation and introduces the concept of removable discontinuities.
