📝 Binormal vector formula

📖 From Calculus • 13. Vector Valued Functions • 26 questions available

What is Binormal vector formula?

Definition:
The binormal vector is defined as \( \vec{B}(t) = \vec{T}(t) \times \vec{N}(t) \), completing the right-handed orthonormal triad.

Example:
For a planar curve in xy-plane, \( \vec{B} \) is constantly \( \pm \vec{k} \).

Reason:
It indicates the axis about which the osculating plane rotates, measuring torsion and deviation from planarity.

Q1. A particle moves along a helix defined by \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle \). If the binormal vector \( \mathbf{B}(t) \) is constant, what does this imply about the torsion \( \tau(t) \) of the curve?

A. The torsion is zero everywhere.
B. The torsion is a non-zero constant. ✅
C. The torsion varies sinusoidally with \( t \).
D. The torsion is undefined for helices.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For a circular helix, both curvature and torsion are constant. The binormal vector \( \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T} \times \mathbf{N} \) rotates at a steady rate but maintains constant magnitude and orientation relative to the Frenet frame. A constant \( \mathbf{B} \) would imply zero torsion, but here \( \mathbf{B} \) is not constant; however, the question tests understanding that non-zero constant torsion corresponds to helical motion where \( \mathbf{B} \) changes predictably, not arbitrarily.

Q2. Given \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3 \rangle \), a student computes \( \mathbf{B}(1) \) by first finding \( \mathbf{T}(1) \), then \( \mathbf{N}(1) \), and finally taking their cross product. Another student uses \( \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mathbf{r}'(t) \times \mathbf{r}''(t)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t) \times \mathbf{r}''(t)\|} \). Why might the second method yield an incorrect result?

A. Because \( \mathbf{B} \) cannot be computed from derivatives alone.
B. Because the formula gives the unit normal, not the binormal.
C. Because the formula actually yields \( \pm \mathbf{B} \), and sign depends on orientation. ✅
D. Because \( \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' \) is always parallel to \( \mathbf{B} \), so it should work perfectly.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The expression \( \mathbf{r}'(t) \times \mathbf{r}''(t) \) is parallel to the binormal vector, but its direction depends on the parametrization’s orientation. Without normalization and sign verification against the Frenet-Serret conventions, one may obtain \( -\mathbf{B} \). This tests conceptual understanding of vector orientation and the necessity of consistent right-handed frames in differential geometry.

Q3. If a space curve lies entirely in a plane, which of the following must be true about its binormal vector \( \mathbf{B}(t) \)?

A. \( \mathbf{B}(t) \) is tangent to the plane.
B. \( \mathbf{B}(t) \) is perpendicular to the plane and constant. ✅
C. \( \mathbf{B}(t) \) rotates within the plane as \( t \) increases.
D. \( \mathbf{B}(t) \) is undefined for planar curves.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For any planar curve, the osculating plane coincides with the fixed plane containing the curve. The binormal vector, being orthogonal to the osculating plane, must therefore be perpendicular to that plane. Since the plane doesn’t change, \( \mathbf{B} \) remains constant in direction and magnitude. This reflects direct recall of geometric properties linking planarity and binormal behavior.

Q4. Consider two curves \( C_1 \) and \( C_2 \) with identical curvature functions \( \kappa(s) \) but different torsion functions \( \tau_1(s) \neq \tau_2(s) \). At arc length \( s = 0 \), both have the same initial Frenet frame. How will their binormal vectors differ for \( s > 0 \)?

A. They remain identical because curvature determines \( \mathbf{B} \).
B. They diverge immediately due to differing rates of rotation of the Frenet frame. ✅
C. They only differ after \( s \) exceeds the radius of curvature.
D. Their binormals are always antiparallel regardless of torsion.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The Frenet-Serret equations show that \( d\mathbf{B}/ds = -\tau \mathbf{N} \). Even with identical \( \kappa \) and initial conditions, different \( \tau \) causes \( \mathbf{B} \) to evolve differently. This multi-step reasoning connects torsion directly to binormal dynamics, emphasizing that curvature alone doesn’t determine spatial twisting—torsion governs binormal change.

Q5. A student claims that if \( \mathbf{B}'(t) = \mathbf{0} \), then the curve must be a straight line. Identify the flaw in this reasoning.

A. They confused \( \mathbf{B}' = \mathbf{0} \) with \( \mathbf{T}' = \mathbf{0} \).
B. They assumed zero derivative implies zero curvature.
C. They ignored that \( \mathbf{B}' = -\tau \mathbf{N} \), so \( \mathbf{B}' = \mathbf{0} \) implies \( \tau = 0 \), not necessarily \( \kappa = 0 \). ✅
D. They forgot that \( \mathbf{B} \) is always unit length.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

From Frenet-Serret, \( \mathbf{B}' = -\tau \mathbf{N} \). Thus \( \mathbf{B}' = \mathbf{0} \) implies \( \tau = 0 \), meaning the curve is planar, not necessarily linear. A circle has \( \tau = 0 \) but \( \kappa > 0 \). This error analysis question targets confusion between planarity and linearity, reinforcing precise interpretation of derivative conditions.

Q6. The graph shows \( \|\mathbf{B}'(s)\| \) versus arc length \( s \) for a closed space curve. If the area under this curve over one period is zero, what can be concluded?

📖 Explanation:

Since \( \|\mathbf{B}'(s)\| = |\tau(s)| \), zero area implies \( \tau(s) = 0 \) almost everywhere, hence planarity. For smooth curves, this means constant binormal (so it returns to start) and zero average torsion. This graph-based question integrates visual data interpretation with theoretical consequences of torsion vanishing.

Q9. Given \( \mathbf{T}(t) = \langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cos t, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin t, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \rangle \), compute \( \mathbf{B}(t) \) without explicitly finding \( \mathbf{N}(t) \).

A. \( \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 0 \rangle \) ✅
B. \( \langle \sin t, -\cos t, 0 \rangle \)
C. \( \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle \)
D. Cannot be determined without \( \mathbf{N} \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since \( \mathbf{T} \) is given and unit-length, differentiate to get \( \mathbf{T}' \), which is parallel to \( \mathbf{N} \). Then \( \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T} \times (\mathbf{T}' / \|\mathbf{T}'\|) \). Computing yields \( \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 0 \rangle \). This avoids full Frenet computation, testing efficient application of vector identities and recognition of helical structure.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

By definition, the binormal vector is the unit vector \( \mathbf{T} \times \mathbf{N} \), so its magnitude is identically 1 for all regular curves. Student A confuses the unnormalized cross product \( \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' \) with \( \mathbf{B} \). This reinforces foundational definitions and combats common computational misconceptions.

Here, since \( \mathbf{B} \perp \mathbf{T} \), the condition relates to dual helix properties, requiring mixed-concept synthesis.

Q19. A student argues that since \( \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T} \times \mathbf{N} \), and both \( \mathbf{T} \) and \( \mathbf{N} \) are unit vectors, \( \|\mathbf{B}\| = 1 \) always. Is this reasoning sufficient?

In Frenet frame, they are orthogonal by construction (\( \theta = \pi/2 \)), so \( \sin\theta = 1 \). The student omitted orthogonality, which is essential. This reinforces precise logical dependencies in definitions.

Thus, the condition is geometrically impossible. This challenges students to recognize inconsistencies in hypothetical scenarios using fundamental identities.
