What is How to find arc length parametrization?

Definition:
To find arc length parametrization, compute \( s(t) = \int_a^t \| \vec{r}'(u) \| \, du \), invert to get \( t(s) \), and substitute back into \( \vec{r}(t) \).

Example:
For \( \vec{r}(t) = \langle e^t \cos t, e^t \sin t \rangle \), solving \( s = \sqrt{2}(e^t - 1) \) yields \( t = \ln(\frac{s}{\sqrt{2}} + 1) \).

Reason:
This systematic procedure standardizes curves for theoretical analysis, though inversion may require numerical methods for complex speeds.

Q1. A particle moves along a curve defined by \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2 \rangle \). If the arc length function is incorrectly computed as \( s(t) = \int_0^t \sqrt{1 + 4\tau} \, d\tau \) instead of \( \sqrt{1 + 4\tau^2} \), what fundamental misconception does this error represent?

A. Confusing velocity magnitude with derivative of position components
B. Misapplying the chain rule when differentiating \( t^2 \)
C. Assuming the integrand should be linear in \( \tau \) rather than quadratic
D. Forgetting to square the derivative before adding under the radical ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The correct speed is \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \sqrt{1 + (2t)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4t^2} \). The erroneous expression \( \sqrt{1 + 4\tau} \) suggests the student differentiated \( t^2 \) as \( 2\sqrt{\tau} \) or failed to square the derivative properly. This reflects a deep misunderstanding of how derivatives contribute to arc length integrands, not just a computational slip.

A. Check if \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| \) is constant or easily invertible ✅
B. Verify if the curve lies on a cylinder
C. Determine if the torsion is zero
D. Test whether \( \mathbf{r}''(t) \) is parallel to \( \mathbf{r}'(t) \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Arc length parametrization requires solving \( s(t) = \int \|\mathbf{r}'(u)\| du \) for \( t(s) \). For this helix, \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \sqrt{2} \), so \( s = \sqrt{2}t \) and inversion is trivial. In general, only curves with simple speed functions allow closed-form reparametrization. Recognizing this avoids futile attempts at integrating non-elementary expressions.

Q3. Two students reparametrize the same curve \( \mathbf{r}(t) \). Student A uses \( s = \int_0^t \|\mathbf{r}'(u)\| du \), while Student B uses \( s = \int_t^0 \|\mathbf{r}'(u)\| du \). How do their resulting parametrizations \( \mathbf{r}_A(s) \) and \( \mathbf{r}_B(s) \) compare?

A. They are identical because arc length is always positive
B. \( \mathbf{r}_B(s) = \mathbf{r}_A(-s) \), reversing orientation ✅
C. \( \mathbf{r}_B(s) \) is undefined for \( s > 0 \)
D. Both yield valid arc length parametrizations but with different base points

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Arc length must increase with parameter. Student B’s integral from \( t \) to 0 gives negative values for \( t > 0 \), effectively measuring backward. To maintain \( s \geq 0 \), one would need to take absolute value or redefine limits. Without correction, \( \mathbf{r}_B(s) \) traces the curve in reverse as \( s \) increases, violating standard orientation conventions for arc length parametrization.

Q4. A curve has speed \( v(t) = e^{t^2} \). Why is it generally impossible to express its arc length parametrization using elementary functions?

A. Because \( e^{t^2} \) grows too rapidly
B. Because \( \int e^{t^2} dt \) has no elementary antiderivative ✅
C. Because the curve is not smooth
D. Because \( v(t) \) is not monotonic

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The arc length function \( s(t) = \int_0^t e^{u^2} du \) involves the imaginary error function, which is non-elementary. Thus, we cannot solve \( t(s) \) explicitly using standard functions. This highlights a key limitation: even smooth, well-behaved curves may resist closed-form arc length parametrization due to integration barriers, necessitating numerical or implicit approaches in applications.

Q5. Consider the graph of speed \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| \) versus \( t \) showing a symmetric hump centered at \( t=0 \). If \( s(t) = \int_{-a}^t \|\mathbf{r}'(u)\| du \), what can be inferred about \( t(s) \) near \( s = s(0) \)?

A. \( t(s) \) is linear near that point
B. \( t(s) \) has an inflection point there
C. \( t(s) \) is steepest at that point
D. \( t(s) \) is flat (horizontal tangent) at that point ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Since speed is symmetric and maximal at \( t=0 \), \( ds/dt \) peaks there, meaning \( dt/ds = 1/(ds/dt) \) is minimized. Thus, \( t(s) \) has a horizontal tangent at \( s = s(0) \). Graphical interpretation of speed helps predict behavior of inverse arc length function without computation, emphasizing conceptual linkage between rate of change and reparametrization geometry.

Q6. Which condition ensures that a vector-valued function \( \mathbf{r}(t) \) admits a global arc length parametrization over its entire domain?

A. \( \mathbf{r}(t) \) is twice differentiable
B. \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| > 0 \) for all \( t \) in the domain
C. The curve is closed and simple
D. \( \mathbf{r}'(t) \) is continuous and never zero ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Global arc length parametrization requires \( s(t) \) to be strictly increasing and continuously differentiable, which holds iff \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| > 0 \) and continuous. Mere differentiability isn’t enough; vanishing speed causes \( s(t) \) to stall, making inversion impossible. This foundational concept distinguishes regular curves from singular ones and is prerequisite for defining unit tangent vectors consistently.

Curvature is a geometric invariant independent of parametrization. Arc length parametrization yields \( \kappa = \|\mathbf{T}'(s)\| \), a simpler formula, but the actual bending of the curve remains unchanged. The student confuses computational convenience with intrinsic property. Understanding this distinction prevents misinterpretation of curvature as a parametric artifact rather than shape descriptor.

Q8. Suppose \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^3, t^2 \rangle \). At \( t = 0 \), \( \mathbf{r}'(0) = \mathbf{0} \). What consequence does this have for arc length parametrization near the origin?

A. The curve cannot be parametrized by arc length anywhere
B. Arc length parametrization exists but \( \mathbf{r}(s) \) is not differentiable at \( s=0 \) ✅
C. The speed integral diverges near zero
D. The curve self-intersects, preventing unique parametrization

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Although \( \mathbf{r}'(0) = 0 \), the curve is still rectifiable. However, \( s(t) \sim \int |t| dt \sim t^2 \) near zero, so \( t \sim \sqrt{s} \), making \( \mathbf{r}(s) \sim \langle s^{3/2}, s \rangle \), which is not differentiable at \( s=0 \). Thus, arc length parametrization exists but loses smoothness at singular points, illustrating that regularity of \( \mathbf{r}(t) \) affects smoothness of \( \mathbf{r}(s) \).

Q11. A curve satisfies \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \sqrt{1 + t^2} \). To find \( t(s) \), one must solve \( s = \int_0^t \sqrt{1 + u^2} du \). Which method is most appropriate for obtaining \( t(s) \) numerically when analytical inversion fails?

A. Newton-Raphson on \( F(t) = \int_0^t \sqrt{1+u^2}du - s \)
B. Direct integration using trapezoidal rule only
C. Solving differential equation \( dt/ds = 1/\sqrt{1+t^2} \)
D. Both A and C are viable ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Analytical inversion of \( s(t) = \frac{1}{2}(t\sqrt{1+t^2} + \sinh^{-1} t) \) is possible here, but generally isn’t. Numerically, one can either apply root-finding to \( s(t) - s = 0 \) (A) or integrate ODE \( dt/ds = 1/v(t) \) forward (C). Both are standard in computational geometry. Option B alone doesn’t invert; it only computes \( s \). Thus D correctly identifies dual numerical strategies.

The error reflects confusion between state variables (position) and rate variables (velocity), critical in dynamics modeling.

A. None; the function is smooth everywhere

📖 Explanation:

The natural log requires positive argument, so \( \cos t > 0 \Rightarrow t \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2) \). Additionally, \( \mathbf{r}'(t) \) involves \( \tan t \), which blows up at endpoints. Thus, open interval is necessary for regularity. Attempting arc length parametrization outside this domain yields complex or undefined expressions. Domain awareness prevents invalid reparametrization attempts.

Q15. A curve has \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = t \) for \( t > 0 \). A student writes \( s = t^2/2 \), so \( t = \sqrt{2s} \), and concludes \( \mathbf{r}(s) = \mathbf{r}(\sqrt{2s}) \). What hidden assumption makes this valid?

A. That \( \mathbf{r}(t) \) is defined for all real \( t \)

D. That curvature is constant

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The relation \( s = \int_0^t u du = t^2/2 \) assumes lower limit 0 matches \( s=0 \). If base point were \( t=a>0 \), then \( s = (t^2 - a^2)/2 \), changing inversion. Correct arc length parametrization always ties \( s=0 \) to a specific \( t_0 \). Overlooking this leads to shifted parametrizations. Base point consistency is crucial for uniqueness.

Q16. In comparing numerical methods for arc length parametrization, why might adaptive quadrature be preferred over fixed-step Euler for computing \( s(t) \)?

A. Euler cannot handle vector functions
B. Adaptive quadrature controls error in \( s(t) \), ensuring accurate inversion later ✅
C. Euler always overestimates arc length
D. Fixed-step methods are never convergent

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Accurate \( s(t) \) is prerequisite for reliable \( t(s) \). Adaptive quadrature adjusts step size based on integrand variation, minimizing cumulative error in \( s \). Fixed-step Euler accumulates error unevenly, especially where speed changes rapidly, leading to poor inversion. Since downstream \( \mathbf{r}(s) \) depends sensitively on \( s(t) \) accuracy, controlling integration error is paramount in computational implementations.

Q19. Given \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle e^t \cos t, e^t \sin t \rangle \), the speed is \( \sqrt{2} e^t \). After finding \( s(t) = \sqrt{2}(e^t - 1) \), what is the correct expression for \( \mathbf{r}(s) \)?

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

From \( s = \sqrt{2}(e^t - 1) \), solve \( e^t = s/\sqrt{2} + 1 \Rightarrow t = \ln(s/\sqrt{2} + 1) \). Substitute into \( \mathbf{r}(t) \): \( e^t = s/\sqrt{2} + 1 \), and trig arguments become \( t = \ln(...) \). This multi-step substitution tests algebraic manipulation and function composition skills. Distractors reflect common errors like forgetting log or misplacing constants.

A curve like \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^3, t^3 \rangle \) is smooth but has \( \mathbf{r}'(0) = 0 \), preventing arc length parametrization at origin. Precise statement requires 'regular smooth curve'. This nuance separates topological smoothness from differential regularity, a subtle but vital distinction in advanced calculus.

Q21. In error analysis, if computed \( \mathbf{r}(s) \) yields \( \|\mathbf{r}'(s)\| \neq 1 \) numerically, what is the most likely source of error?

📖 Explanation:

By definition, arc length parametrization must satisfy \( \|d\mathbf{r}/ds\| = 1 \). Deviation indicates \( t(s) \) is inaccurate, usually from imperfect root-finding or ODE solution when inverting \( s(t) \). Even small errors in \( t(s) \) propagate to violate unit speed. This diagnostic check validates computational pipeline integrity in numerical differential geometry.

Q22. A graph shows \( s(t) \) as a concave-up increasing function. What does this imply about the original parametrization’s speed?

A. Speed is decreasing
B. Speed is constant

Concave-up means second derivative positive, so speed is increasing. Graphical interpretation links shape of arc length function to dynamic properties of motion, fostering visual-intuitive understanding beyond formulas.

Q23. When reparametrizing \( \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, \cosh t \rangle \) by arc length, why is hyperbolic identity \( \cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1 \) crucial?

A. It simplifies \( \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \cosh t \) ✅
B. It proves the curve is a catenary

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

\( \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 1, \sinh t \rangle \), so \( \|\mathbf{r}'\| = \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2 t} = \sqrt{\cosh^2 t} = \cosh t \) (since \( \cosh t > 0 \)). Without this identity, integration becomes messy. Recognizing trigonometric/hyperbolic simplifications is key to tractable reparametrization. This tests strategic knowledge of identities within applied calculus contexts.

Q24. Olympiad-style: Let \( \mathbf{r}(t) \) be a unit-speed curve with \( \kappa(s) = s \). If \( \mathbf{r}(0) = \mathbf{0} \) and \( \mathbf{T}(0) = \langle 1,0,0 \rangle \), what is \( \|\mathbf{r}(1)\| \)?

A. 1

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For unit-speed curve, \( \|\mathbf{r}(s)\| \leq \int_0^s \|\mathbf{T}(u)\| du = s \), with equality only if straight line. Here \( \kappa(s)=s>0 \) for \( s>0 \), so curve bends immediately, making chord length \( \|\mathbf{r}(1)\| < 1 \). Exact value requires solving Frenet equations, but inequality suffices. This tests geometric intuition over computation, typical of Olympiad reasoning.

Q25. Mixed concept: How does arc length parametrization interact with the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus when verifying \( \|d\mathbf{r}/ds\| = 1 \)?

A. FTC directly gives \( d\mathbf{r}/ds = \mathbf{r}'(t) / (ds/dt) \)
B. FTC confirms \( s(t) \) is antiderivative of speed, enabling chain rule application ✅
C. FTC proves speed is always positive
D. FTC is irrelevant to parametrization

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Verification uses chain rule: \( d\mathbf{r}/ds = (d\mathbf{r}/dt)(dt/ds) = \mathbf{r}'(t) / (ds/dt) \). FTC ensures \( ds/dt = \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| \), so magnitude becomes \( \|\mathbf{r}'\| / \|\mathbf{r}'\| = 1 \). Thus, FTC underpins the critical link between integral definition and derivative property. This synthesis of integration and differentiation exemplifies mixed-concept mastery.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

In \( \mathbf{r}(t) \), timing errors compound with speed variations to cause large positional drift. In \( \mathbf{r}(s) \), each \( s \) corresponds to fixed ground distance, so noisy \( s \) translates linearly to location error regardless of velocity profile. This decoupling enhances robustness in real-time systems. Application-aware understanding distinguishes theoretical from practical parametrization benefits.
