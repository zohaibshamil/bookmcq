What is Inverse functions definition and properties?

Definition:
An inverse function, denoted \( f^{-1} \), reverses the effect of a function \( f \), satisfying \( f^{-1}(f(x)) = x \) for all \( x \) in the domain of \( f \), and \( f(f^{-1}(y)) = y \) for all \( y \) in the range, provided \( f \) is one-to-one.

Example:
For \( f(x) = 2x+3 \), inverse is \( f^{-1}(y) = (y-3)/2 \), since \( f^{-1}(f(x)) = (2x+3-3)/2 = x \).

Reason:
Inverses allow us to undo" operations, essential in solving equations and in many applications like encryption and decoding."

Q1. If \(h\) is the inverse of \(f\) and \(k\) is the inverse of \(h\), what is the relationship between \(k\) and \(f\)?

A.\(k = f^{-1}\)
B.\(k = f\) ✅
C.\(k = f^{-1}\)
D.\(k\) is unrelated to \(f\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Taking the inverse of a function twice restores the original function: \((f^{-1})^{-1}=f\). Since \(h=f^{-1}\) and \(k=h^{-1}\), it follows that \(k=(f^{-1})^{-1}=f\). Thus \(k\) and \(f\) are the same function.

Q2. Suppose a function \(p\) satisfies \(p(p^{-1}(x))=x\) for all \(x\) in its range, but \(p^{-1}(p(x))=x\) fails for some \(x\). Which statement must be false?

A.\(p\) is one‑to‑one
B.\(p\) is onto
C.\(p\) has an inverse
D.\(p\) is bijective ✅
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: If the second cancellation fails, the function cannot be onto as well as one‑to‑one; bijectivity requires both properties. Therefore the claim that \(p\) is bijective must be false, even though it may still be one‑to‑one.

Q3. For \(f(x)=x^{3}+1\) and its inverse \(f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt[3]{\,x-1\,}\), which description of their domains is correct?

A.Both have domain \(\mathbb{R}\)
B.Domain of \(f\) is \(\mathbb{R}\) but range of \(f^{-1}\) is \([1,\infty)\) ✅
C.Domain of \(f^{-1}\) is \([1,\infty)\) only
D.Both are defined only for \(x\ge 1\)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The cubic function \(x^{3}+1\) is defined for every real \(x\), giving a range of all real numbers. Its inverse therefore has domain equal to that range, i.e., \(\mathbb{R}\). Both functions are defined for all real inputs.

Q4. What is \((f^{-1}\circ f)(2)\) when \(f(x)=x^{3}+1\)?

A.\(2\) ✅
B.\(8\)
C.\(\sqrt[3]{9}\)
D.\(1\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The composition \(f^{-1}(f(2))\) first evaluates \(f(2)=2^{3}+1=9\) and then applies \(f^{-1}\) to 9, yielding \(\sqrt[3]{9-1}=2\). Thus the overall result is the original input, \(2\).

Q5. Which geometric description correctly relates the graph of a function to the graph of its inverse?

A.They are reflections of each other across the line \(y=x\) ✅
B.They are reflections across the \(x\)-axis
C.They are reflections across the \(y\)-axis
D.There is no systematic relationship
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: By definition, swapping the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) reflects the set of points \((x,y)\) across the line \(y=x\). Hence the graph of a function and that of its inverse are mirror images about this line.

Q6. What does the notation \(f^{-1}(x)\) represent?

A.The reciprocal of \(f(x)\)
B.The inverse function of \(f\) evaluated at \(x\) ✅
C.The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\)
D.A negative exponent applied to \(f\)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The superscript \(-1\) on a function symbol denotes the inverse function, not a reciprocal or derivative. Thus \(f^{-1}(x)\) means “apply the inverse of \(f\) to the input \(x\).”

Q7. Why does interchanging \(x\) and \(y\) in the equation \(y = x^{3}+1\) lead to the inverse function?

A.Solving for \(x\) gives \(x = \sqrt[3]{\,y-1\,}\), which defines \(f^{-1}\) ✅
B.Taking the reciprocal of both sides yields the inverse
C.Differentiating both sides produces the inverse
D.Integrating both sides yields the inverse
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Swapping the variables treats the original output as the new input. Solving the resulting equation for the new dependent variable isolates \(x\) as \(\sqrt[3]{\,y-1\,}\), which is precisely the expression for the inverse function.

Q8. For a strictly increasing function \(f\), which property guarantees that \(f\) possesses an inverse?

A.Continuity on its domain
B.Being one‑to‑one (injective) ✅
C.Having a horizontal asymptote
D.Differentiability everywhere
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A strictly increasing function never takes the same value twice, making it injective. Injectivity is the essential condition for the existence of an inverse; continuity or differentiability are not required, though they often accompany monotonicity.

Q9. What is the inverse of \(f(x)=2x+3\)?

A.\(\displaystyle\frac{x-3}{2}\) ✅
B.\(2x-3\)
C.\(\displaystyle\frac{x+3}{2}\)
D.\(\displaystyle\frac{2}{x-3}\)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Set \(y=2x+3\), solve for \(x\): \(x=\frac{y-3}{2}\). Replacing \(y\) with the input variable yields the inverse function \(f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x-3}{2}\).

Q10. If \(f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt[3]{\,x-1\,}\), what is \(f(x)\)?

A.\(x^{3}+1\) ✅
B.\(\sqrt[3]{x}+1\)
C.\((x+1)^{3}\)
D.\(x^{1/3}+1\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The inverse relationship means that solving \(y=\sqrt[3]{\,x-1\,}\) for \(x\) gives \(x=y^{3}+1\). Replacing \(y\) with the original variable \(x\) produces \(f(x)=x^{3}+1\).

Q11. For \(f(x)=x^{3}+1\), what is \((f\circ f^{-1})(5)\)?

A.\(5\) ✅
B.\(125+1\)
C.\(\sqrt[3]{5-1}+1\)
D.Undefined
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: First apply \(f^{-1}\) to 5: \(f^{-1}(5)=\sqrt[3]{5-1}= \sqrt[3]{4}\). Then apply \(f\): \(f(\sqrt[3]{4})=(\sqrt[3]{4})^{3}+1=4+1=5\). The composition returns the original argument, 5.

Q12. True or false: Every function has an inverse.

A.true
B.false ✅
C.It depends on the function
D.Cannot be determined
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A function must be one‑to‑one (injective) to possess an inverse. Many functions fail this condition, such as \(f(x)=x^{2}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\). Hence the statement is false.

Q13. Which statement correctly describes the relationship between the domains of a function and its inverse?

A.The domain of \(f\) equals the range of \(f^{-1}\) ✅
B.The domain of \(f\) equals the domain of \(f^{-1}\)
C.The range of \(f\) equals the range of \(f^{-1}\)
D.There is no systematic relationship
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: By definition, the set of outputs (range) of \(f\) becomes the set of permissible inputs (domain) for \(f^{-1}\). Consequently, the domain of \(f\) is precisely the range of its inverse, and vice versa.

Q14. Suppose \(f\) is defined on \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(f^{-1}\) exists. If \(f(4)=10\), what is \((f^{-1})'(10)\) in terms of \(f'(4)\)?

A.\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{f'(4)}\) ✅
B.\(f'(4)\)
C.\(-f'(4)\)
D.\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{[f'(4)]^{2}}\)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The derivative of an inverse satisfies \((f^{-1})'(y)=\frac{1}{f'(x)}\) where \(y=f(x)\). Here \(y=10\) and \(x=4\), so \((f^{-1})'(10)=1/f'(4)\).

Q15. Given \(f(x)=x^{3}+1\), for which \(x\) does \((f^{-1})'(x)\) exist?

A.All real numbers
B.\(x>1\)
C.\(x\neq 1\) ✅
D.No values
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The inverse derivative exists wherever \(f'(x)\neq0\). Since \(f'(x)=3x^{2}\), it vanishes only at \(x=0\), corresponding to \(y=f(0)=1\). Thus \((f^{-1})'(x)\) is defined for every \(x\) except \(x=1\).

Q16. When graphing \(f(x)=x^{3}+1\) and its inverse together, which transformation maps the graph of \(f\) onto the graph of its inverse?

A.Reflection across the line \(y=x\) ✅
B.Rotation by \(90^\circ\) about the origin
C.Horizontal translation by 1 unit
D.Uniform scaling by a factor of 2
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Swapping the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) corresponds to reflecting each point \((x,y)\) across the line \(y=x\). This operation converts the graph of a function into the graph of its inverse.

Q17. If a function satisfies \(f^{-1}(x)=\frac{1}{x}\), which of the following could be \(f(x)\)?

A.\(x\)
B.\(\frac{1}{x}\) ✅
C.\(-x\)
D.\(x^{2}\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The function \(g(x)=1/x\) is its own inverse because solving \(y=1/x\) for \(x\) yields \(x=1/y\), which is the same expression. Therefore the original function \(f\) that has this inverse must also be \(f(x)=1/x\).
