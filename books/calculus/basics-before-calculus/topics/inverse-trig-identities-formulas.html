📝 Inverse trig identities formulas (14 MCQs)

Algebraic Identities Linking Trigonometric Inverses

Inverse trigonometric identities express relationships between trigonometric functions and their inverses, allowing nested expressions to be simplified or evaluated. They depend on principal domains and ranges rather than unrestricted cancellation. These identities prepare students for differential and integral calculus, where inverse trigonometric expressions occur in derivatives, antiderivatives, substitutions, and solutions of trigonometric equations.

Why Principal Ranges Control Inverse Identities

Applying a trigonometric function to its inverse returns the original input on the inverse function's domain. Reversing the order is more restrictive because the resulting angle must lie in the chosen principal range. Therefore, expressions such as \(\arcsin(\sin x)\) do not equal \(x\) for every real number, even though the corresponding nested operation may simplify on a restricted interval.

Fundamental Composition and Pythagorean Identities

The identities \(\sin(\arcsin x)=x\) and \(\cos(\arccos x)=x\) hold for \(x\in[-1,1]\), while \(\tan(\arctan x)=x\) holds for all real \(x\). Also, \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) can be applied when \(\theta\) is an inverse trigonometric value, with signs determined by its principal quadrant.

Simplifying an Expression with Arctangent

Let \(\theta=\arctan(3/4)\). Then \(\tan\theta=3/4\), and a right triangle with opposite side 3 and adjacent side 4 has hypotenuse 5. Therefore, \(\sin\theta=3/5\) and \(\cos\theta=4/5\). The principal range of arctangent places \(\theta\) in the first quadrant.

Inverse Identities in Differentiation and Integration

These identities simplify expressions before applying derivative and integral rules. For example, differentiating \(\arctan x\) gives \(1/(1+x^2)\), a standard result in integration. Trigonometric substitutions also use inverse relationships to convert algebraic radicals into trigonometric expressions. Incorrectly ignoring principal ranges can introduce sign errors or invalid simplifications.

Misusing Cancellation and Principal-Value Identities

'\(\arcsin(\sin x)=x\) for all real \(x\)' ignores the principal interval; it holds directly for \(x\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\). '\(\sin(\arcsin x)\) is valid for every real \(x\)' ignores the domain \([-1,1]\). 'Squaring an inverse trigonometric value determines its sign' is false because squaring removes sign information.

Inverse Trigonometric Identity Questions in Examinations

Practise simplifying nested inverse and ordinary trigonometric expressions using their domains and ranges. Use reference triangles to determine missing ratios and quadrant signs. Verify whether a proposed cancellation is valid for the specified interval. Questions may also combine Pythagorean identities with inverse functions or require simplification before differentiation or integration.

Key Terms of Inverse Trigonometric Identities

  • Composition identity - Identity for nested inverse and original functions.
  • Principal interval - Restricted interval of returned angles.
  • Pythagorean identity - Relationship among squared trigonometric ratios.
  • Reference triangle - Right triangle used to find ratios.
  • Arctangent - Inverse of restricted tangent.
  • Domain restriction - Condition limiting valid inputs.

📝 All Inverse trig identities formulas MCQs

Q1. What is \(\sin^{-1}(0)\)?

A.0 ✅
B.\(\frac{\pi}{2}\)
C.\(-\frac{\pi}{2}\)
D.\(\pi\)
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Since \(\sin 0 = 0\) and the principal value of \(\sin^{-1}\) returns the angle in \([-\,\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\) whose sine is the argument, the only angle satisfying this is \(0\). Hence \(\sin^{-1}(0)=0\).

Q2. Given \(x=\frac12\), what is \(\sin^{-1}x + \cos^{-1}x\)?

A.\(\frac{\pi}{4}\)
B.\(\pi\)
C.\(\frac{\pi}{2}\) ✅
D.0
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The fundamental identity \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\frac{\pi}{2}\) holds for any \(x\) in \([-1,1]\). Substituting \(x=\frac12\) does not change the sum, so the result is \(\frac{\pi}{2}\).

Q3. Which expression equals \(\tan(\sin^{-1}x)\) for \(-1\le x\le1\)?

A.\(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\)
B.\(\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\)
C.\(x\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\)
D.\(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\) ✅
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Let \(\theta=\sin^{-1}x\); then \(\sin\theta=x\) and \(\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) (non‑negative). By definition \(\tan\theta=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\). Hence the correct expression is \(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\).

Q4. For which interval does \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x)=x\) hold true?

A.\([-\,\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\) ✅
B.[0,\(\pi\)]
C.\([-\,\pi,\pi]\)
D.\([\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}]\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The inverse sine returns the principal angle in \([-\,\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\). Only when the original angle \(x\) lies in that interval does \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x)\) equal \(x\); otherwise the value is adjusted by adding or subtracting multiples of \(\pi\).

Q5. Using the identity \(\sec(\tan^{-1}x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\), what is \(\sec(\tan^{-1}3)\)?

A.\(\sqrt{13}\)
B.\(\sqrt{10}\) ✅
C.\(\sqrt{5}\)
D.\(\sqrt{2}\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Apply the identity directly with \(x=3\): \(\sec(\tan^{-1}3)=\sqrt{1+3^{2}}=\sqrt{10}\). This avoids constructing a triangle; the formula already gives the required value.

Q6. For \(x\ge1\), which formula gives \(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)\)?

A.\(\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{x}\) ✅
B.\(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\)
C.\(\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\)
D.\(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Let \(\theta=\sec^{-1}x\); then \(\sec\theta=x\) and \(\cos\theta=\frac{1}{x}\). By the Pythagorean theorem, \(\sin\theta=\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\theta}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{x^{2}}}=\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{x}\).

Q7. If \(\theta=\sin^{-1}x\), what is \(\cos\theta\) in terms of \(x\)?

A.\(-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\)
B.\(1-x^{2}\)
C.\(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) ✅
D.\(x\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Because \(\theta\) is defined to be in \([-\,\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\), its cosine is non‑negative. Using the identity \(\sin^{2}\theta+\cos^{2}\theta=1\) and \(\sin\theta=x\), we obtain \(\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\).

Q8. Let \(x=\frac{3\pi}{4}\). What is \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x)\)?

A.\(\frac{3\pi}{4}\)
B.\(\frac{\pi}{4}\) ✅
C.\(-\frac{\pi}{4}\)
D.\(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Since \(x\) lies in \([\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}]\), the principal value is \(-\,(x-\pi)=\pi-x\). Substituting \(x=\frac{3\pi}{4}\) gives \(\pi-\frac{3\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{4}\). Thus \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x)=\frac{\pi}{4}\).

Q9. For \(x>0\), how does \(\tan^{-1}x\) compare to \(\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\right)\)?

A.They are equal ✅
B.\(\tan^{-1}x\) is larger
C.\(\tan^{-1}x\) is smaller
D.Cannot be determined without numerical evaluation
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Starting with a right triangle where opposite side is \(x\) and adjacent side is \(1\), the hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\). The angle whose tangent is \(x\) is \(\tan^{-1}x\). The same angle has sine \(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\), so \(\sin^{-1}\) of that ratio equals the same angle. Hence the two expressions are equal.

Q10. What is the principal value range of \(\cos^{-1}x\)?

A.[0,\(\pi\)] ✅
B.[-\(\frac{\pi}{2}\),\(\frac{\pi}{2}\)]
C.[0,2\(\pi\)]
D.[-\(\pi\),\(\pi\)]
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: By definition, the inverse cosine returns the unique angle \(\theta\) in \([0,\pi]\) whose cosine equals the given number. This interval ensures the function is one‑to‑one and covers all possible cosine values from \(-1\) to \(1\).

Q11. Using \(\sin^{-1}x = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right)\), what is \(\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\)?

A.\(\frac{\pi}{6}\)
B.\(\frac{\pi}{3}\)
C.\(\frac{\pi}{2}\)
D.\(\frac{\pi}{4}\) ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Compute \(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\) with \(x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\): the denominator becomes \(\sqrt{1-\frac12}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\), so the fraction equals \(1\). Hence \(\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\tan^{-1}(1)=\frac{\pi}{4}\).

Q12. If \(\sin^{-1}x = \alpha\) and \(\cos^{-1}x = \beta\), what is \(\alpha - \beta\)?

A.0
B.\(\frac{\pi}{2}\)
C.\(-\frac{\pi}{2}\)
D.It depends on the value of \(x\) ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: From the identity \(\alpha+\beta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), we can solve for \(\alpha-\beta = 2\alpha-\frac{\pi}{2}\). Since \(\alpha\) varies with \(x\), the difference is not a constant; it changes according to the specific \(x\) chosen.

Q13. Given the extended identity \(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)=\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{|x|}\) for \(|x|\ge1\), what is \(\sin(\sec^{-1}(-2))\)?

A.\(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\)
B.\(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) ✅
C.\(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{-2}\)
D.\(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{-2}\)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Apply the formula with \(x=-2\): \(|x|=2\) and \(\sqrt{x^{2}-1}=\sqrt{4-1}=\sqrt{3}\). The expression becomes \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), which is positive because the absolute value in the denominator removes the sign of \(x\).

Q14. Which of the following statements is true for all \(x\) with \(|x|\ge1\)?

A.\(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\)
B.\(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)=\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{x}\)
C.\(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)=\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{|x|}\) ✅
D.\(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)=\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The correct form must handle both positive and negative \(x\). The identity \(\sin(\sec^{-1}x)=\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{|x|}\) does exactly that, because the absolute value in the denominator ensures the result is always non‑negative, matching the range of the sine function for the principal angle.
