What is Change of Variables in Multiple Integrals: Jacobians?

Definition:
When changing variables in multiple integrals, we use the Jacobian determinant \( J \) to adjust the area or volume element: \( dA = |J| \, du \, dv \) or \( dV = |J| \, du \, dv \, dw \).

Example:
In polar coordinates, \( J = r \); in spherical, \( J = \rho^2\sin\phi \).

Reason:
The Jacobian accounts for the distortion of area/volume elements under the transformation, ensuring the integral's value remains invariant.

Q1. For the transformation \(x=2u-v,\ y=u+3v\), which quantity correctly determines how a small area element changes under the transformation?

A.The determinant of the matrix of first derivatives ✅
B.The sum of all first derivatives
C.The product \(xy\) expressed in \(u,v\)
D.The determinant of the inverse transformation matrix only
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: A change of variables scales area according to the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant. Here the derivative matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\), whose determinant is \(7\). Therefore a small \(du,dv\) region becomes an area approximately \(7\,du\,dv\). The other choices do not correctly describe local area scaling.

Q2. A transformation satisfies \(x=u+v,\ y=u-v\). A student claims that because the transformation uses two equations symmetrically, the Jacobian must equal \(1\). Which evaluation best corrects the student's reasoning?

A.The Jacobian is \(2\), because the determinant is \(1(1)-1(-1)\) ✅
B.The Jacobian is \(0\), because the equations are dependent
C.The Jacobian is \(1\), because symmetry guarantees unit area scaling
D.The Jacobian is \(-2\), because the transformation reverses orientation
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Symmetry in the formulas does not imply unit area scaling. The derivative matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\), so its determinant is \(-2\). The absolute value, \(2\), gives the area scaling factor. The negative sign describes orientation, not a negative physical area.

Q3. Why is the absolute value of a Jacobian determinant used when converting an ordinary double integral between coordinate systems?

A.Because area and volume cannot be negative even when orientation is reversed ✅
B.Because the determinant is always positive for valid transformations
C.Because taking the absolute value removes all nonlinear behavior
D.Because the original integrand must always be positive
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The Jacobian determinant can be negative when a transformation reverses orientation. However, geometric area must remain nonnegative, so the conversion uses the absolute value of the determinant. This distinction is important because the determinant contains both scaling information and orientation information, while an area element represents magnitude.

Q4. Suppose \(x=u^2-v^2\) and \(y=2uv\). At a point where \(u\) and \(v\) are both nonzero, what does a vanishing Jacobian imply if it occurs?

A.The transformation may fail to be locally one-to-one there ✅
B.The transformed region must have zero area everywhere
C.The original integrand must be zero at that point
D.The transformation automatically becomes globally one-to-one
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: A zero Jacobian indicates that local area scaling collapses at that point. This often signals that the transformation is not locally invertible there, although it does not mean that the entire transformed region has zero area. The integrand itself also need not vanish. Local invertibility must therefore be examined carefully.

Q5. A rectangular region in the \(uv\)-plane is transformed by \(x=3u,\ y=2v\). If its area is \(5\), what is the area of its image, assuming the transformation is one-to-one?

A.10
B.15 ✅
C.6
D.5
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The transformation stretches distances in the \(u\)-direction by \(3\) and in the \(v\)-direction by \(2\). Therefore the area scaling factor is \(3\cdot2=6\), which is also the absolute Jacobian determinant. Multiplying the original area \(5\) by \(6\) gives an image area of \(30\), so none of the listed values is correct.

Q6. For \(x=u+v,\ y=u-v\), an integral over a region in the \(xy\)-plane is rewritten using \(u,v\). Which differential-area relationship is correct?

A.\(dx\,dy=2\,du\,dv\) ✅
B.\(dx\,dy=\frac12\,du\,dv\)
C.\(dx\,dy=-2\,du\,dv\)
D.\(dx\,dy=du\,dv\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The forward Jacobian is \(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=-2\). Since area uses the absolute value, \(|J|=2\), giving \(dx\,dy=2\,du\,dv\). The reciprocal value would correspond to the inverse Jacobian when expressing \(du\,dv\) in terms of \(dx\,dy\), which is a common source of mistakes.

Q7. A region is bounded by the curves \(x+y=2,\ x-y=4,\ x+y=6,\ x-y=8\). Which substitution is most useful for simplifying the region before evaluating a double integral?

A.\(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\) ✅
B.\(u=x^2+y^2,\ v=x+y\)
C.\(u=x-y,\ v=x^2+y^2\)
D.\(u=x,\ v=y^2\)
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The boundary curves already have the forms \(x+y=\text{constant}\) and \(x-y=\text{constant}\). Choosing \(u=x+y\) and \(v=x-y\) converts all four boundaries into constant-coordinate lines, producing a rectangle in the \(uv\)-plane. This substantially simplifies both the region description and the limits.

Q8. A student transforms \(x=u^2,\ y=v^2\) and writes \(dx\,dy=4uv\,du\,dv\) without considering the sign of \(u\) or \(v\). What is the main issue with this reasoning?

A.The Jacobian should be \(4|uv|\) when the full sign-changing domain is included ✅
B.The Jacobian must always equal \(4\)
C.The transformation cannot be used for any double integral
D.The correct Jacobian is \(2u+2v\)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The derivative matrix is diagonal with entries \(2u\) and \(2v\), giving determinant \(4uv\). For an area element, the required factor is \(|4uv|=4|uv|\). Writing \(4uv\) without absolute values can produce negative area factors when \(u\) and \(v\) have opposite signs, so the domain and one-to-one behavior must be considered.

Q9. A graph shows a curved region enclosed by \(xy=1,\ xy=4,\ x/y=1,\ x/y=9\), restricted to the first quadrant. Which coordinate choice would most naturally turn the boundaries into straight coordinate lines?

A.\(u=xy,\ v=x/y\) ✅
B.\(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\)
C.\(u=x^2+y^2,\ v=x^2-y^2\)
D.\(u=x,\ v=y\)
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The boundary curves are already described by constant products and ratios. Setting \(u=xy\) changes the product boundaries to \(u=1\) and \(u=4\), while \(v=x/y\) changes the ratio boundaries to \(v=1\) and \(v=9\). Thus the curved region becomes a rectangle in the transformed coordinates, making the geometry much easier to handle.

Q10. A model uses \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) to describe a circular region. One student says the Jacobian is \(1\) because \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\). Another says it is \(r\). Which conclusion is correct, and why?

A.The second student is correct because the determinant of the derivative matrix has magnitude \(r\) ✅
B.The first student is correct because the trigonometric identity directly gives the determinant
C.Both are correct because the Jacobian can be chosen freely
D.Neither is correct because polar variables cannot be used for circular regions
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The identity \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) is relevant to simplifying the determinant, but it is not itself the Jacobian. Differentiating \(x\) and \(y\) with respect to \(r,\theta\) gives a determinant of \(r\), so \(dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta\). The radial factor is essential for correct area scaling.

Q11. A transformation maps a small square in the \(uv\)-plane near a point into a thin, tilted parallelogram in the \(xy\)-plane. If the Jacobian determinant at that point is \(-3\), what can be concluded about the local geometry?

A.Area is locally scaled by \(3\), while orientation is reversed ✅
B.Area is locally scaled by \(-3\), producing negative physical area
C.The transformation preserves area but reverses only angles
D.The transformed parallelogram must have area zero
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The determinant magnitude gives the local area scaling, so a determinant of \(-3\) means the area is multiplied by \(3\). The negative sign indicates orientation reversal. A small square therefore becomes a parallelogram with approximately three times its original area, assuming the transformation is sufficiently smooth near the point.

Q12. Consider \(x=u+2v,\ y=3u+6v\). A student attempts to use this transformation to convert a double integral and computes a nonzero Jacobian. What should be checked first?

A.Whether the determinant is zero because the two rows are proportional ✅
B.Whether the integrand is continuous
C.Whether \(u\) and \(v\) have the same sign
D.Whether the transformed region is circular
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The derivative matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&6\end{pmatrix}\). Its determinant is \(1(6)-2(3)=0\), so the transformation collapses two-dimensional regions into lower-dimensional sets locally. A nonzero Jacobian calculation would indicate an algebraic error. Before changing variables, local invertibility and nonzero area scaling must be verified.

Q13. Let \(x=u^2-v^2\) and \(y=2uv\). For a point with \(u\neq0\) and \(v\neq0\), which statement best describes the Jacobian and its geometric significance?

A.The Jacobian is \(4(u^2+v^2)\), so the local area scale depends on distance from the origin ✅
B.The Jacobian is \(2(u^2-v^2)\), so area depends only on \(x\)
C.The Jacobian is \(4uv\), so the transformation always collapses near nonzero points
D.The Jacobian is \(u^2+v^2\), so no scaling factor is needed
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Differentiating gives \(x_u=2u,\ x_v=-2v,\ y_u=2v,\ y_v=2u\). The determinant is \(4u^2+4v^2=4(u^2+v^2)\). This is positive away from the origin, indicating nonzero local area scaling there. The result also shows that the scaling grows with the squared distance from the \(uv\)-origin.

Q14. A difficult integral contains the factor \(x^2+y^2\) over a region bounded by two circles centered at the origin and two rays from the origin. Which strategy most efficiently combines the geometry and the integrand?

A.Use \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\), because both the boundaries and \(x^2+y^2\) simplify ✅
B.Use \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\), because every circular boundary becomes linear
C.Use \(u=x^2,\ v=y^2\), because the Jacobian is constant
D.Keep Cartesian coordinates because the circular boundaries cannot be represented systematically
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The circular boundaries become constant values of \(r\), while the rays become constant values of \(\theta\). In addition, \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), and the area element contributes another factor \(r\). Thus the substitution simultaneously simplifies the region, the integrand, and the differential element, reducing a complicated two-dimensional problem to straightforward separated limits.
