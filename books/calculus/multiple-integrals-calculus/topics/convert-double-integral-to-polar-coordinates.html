📝 Convert double integral to polar coordinates

📖 From Calculus • 15. Multiple Integrals Calculus • 14 questions available

What is Convert double integral to polar coordinates?

Definition:
To convert, substitute \( x=r\cos\theta \), \( y=r\sin\theta \), replace \( dA \) with \( r \, dr \, d\theta \), and adjust the limits to describe the region in terms of \( r \) and \( \theta \).

Example:
The integral \( \int_{-1}^1 \int_0^{\sqrt{1-x^2}} f(x,y) \, dy \, dx \) over the upper semicircle becomes \( \int_0^\pi \int_0^1 f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \, r \, dr \, d\theta \).

Reason:
Conversion is necessary when the region or integrand exhibits radial symmetry, drastically simplifying the algebraic complexity of the integral.

Q1. For a region R that is the interior of the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 2y\), a student writes the polar integral as \(\int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\sin\theta} f(r,\theta) \, r \, dr \, d\theta\). However, the correct limit for \(\theta\) should be \(0\) to \(\pi\). What is the fundamental error in the student's reasoning if they instead used \(-\pi/2\) to \(\pi/2\)?

A. They confused the circle's center, which is at (0,1), with the origin, leading to an incorrect angular range that traces the circle twice. ✅
B. They forgot that \(r\) must be non-negative, and the negative range for \(\theta\) is invalid in polar coordinates.
C. They incorrectly solved \(r^2 = 2r\sin\theta\) to \(r = 2\sin\theta\), but \(\sin\theta\) is negative in the third and fourth quadrants, requiring a shift.
D. They used the wrong Jacobian; the correct Jacobian is \(r\), but the angular limits depend on the sign of \(r\).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 2y\) converts to \(r^2 = 2r\sin\theta\), giving \(r = 2\sin\theta\). Since \(r \ge 0\), we need \(\sin\theta \ge 0\), which is \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\). The range \(-\pi/2\) to \(\pi/2\) includes negative \(\sin\theta\) values, which would make \(r\) negative, an invalid representation. This is a common error where students treat the circle as if centered at the origin and fail to enforce \(r \ge 0\) when determining angular bounds.

Q10. The graph of a region R is a cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\). To compute the moment of inertia about the z-axis, \(I_z = \iint_R (x^2 + y^2) \, dA\), a student sets up \(I_z = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1+\cos\theta} r^2 \, dr \, d\theta\). What is the error in this setup?

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

The angular range for a standard cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\) is indeed \(0\) to \(2\pi\) to trace the full curve; there is no inner loop. So the only error is the missing \(r\). This is a very common misconception where students forget to multiply by the Jacobian when converting the area element.
