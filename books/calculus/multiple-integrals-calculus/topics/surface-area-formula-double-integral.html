📝 Surface area formula double integral

📖 From Calculus • 15. Multiple Integrals Calculus • 14 questions available

What is Surface area formula double integral?

Definition:
For a surface defined explicitly as \( z = f(x, y) \), the surface area is \( A = \iint_R \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)^2} \, dA \).

Example:
The area of the plane \( z = 2x + 3y \) over the unit square is \( \iint_R \sqrt{1+4+9} \, dA = \sqrt{14} \cdot 1 = \sqrt{14} \).

Reason:
This formula accounts for the slope of the surface; steeper surfaces have larger area elements than their projections on the xy-plane, ensuring accurate measurement of curved surfaces.

📝 All Surface area formula double integral MCQs

Q1. For a surface defined by \( z = f(x,y) \) over a region \( R \), the surface area formula is \( \iint_R \sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2} \, dA \). What does the term \( \sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2} \) geometrically represent?

A. The length of the normal vector to the surface at a point
B. The magnitude of the gradient of \( f \) at a point
C. The area scaling factor when mapping a small rectangle in \( R \) to a parallelogram on the tangent plane ✅
D. The square of the distance from the point to the origin

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The expression is the norm of the cross product of the two tangent vectors \( \mathbf{r}_x = (1,0,f_x) \) and \( \mathbf{r}_y = (0,1,f_y) \). This norm \( ||\mathbf{r}_x \times \mathbf{r}_y|| = \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2} \) is the area of the infinitesimal parallelogram on the tangent plane that corresponds to a unit area in the \( xy \)-plane. Option A is incorrect as the normal vector is not fully specified; the gradient lacks the vertical component; and D misinterprets the geometric meaning.

Q2. Find the surface area of the portion of the plane \( z = 2x + 3y \) that lies above the rectangle \( 0 \le x \le 1 \), \( 0 \le y \le 2 \).

A. \( 2\sqrt{14} \) ✅
B. \( \sqrt{14} \)
C. \( 2\sqrt{10} \)
D. \( \sqrt{10} \)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Here \( f_x = 2 \) and \( f_y = 3 \). The integrand is \( \sqrt{1+4+9} = \sqrt{14} \). The area of the rectangular region \( R \) is \( 1 \times 2 = 2 \). Therefore, the surface area is \( \sqrt{14} \times 2 = 2\sqrt{14} \). Option B forgets to multiply by the region's area, a common mistake. Options C and D come from incorrectly computing \( 1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2 \).

Q3. A student computes the surface area of \( z = x^2 + y^2 \) over the unit disk by using polar coordinates and obtains \( \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 \sqrt{1+4r^2} \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta \). The student then evaluates this as \( 2\pi \int_0^1 (1+4r^2) \, dr \). What error did the student make?

A. They forgot the Jacobian factor \( r \)
B. They incorrectly set \( f_x^2 + f_y^2 = 4r^2 \)
C. They incorrectly simplified \( \sqrt{1+4r^2} \cdot r \) to \( (1+4r^2) \) ✅
D. They used the wrong limits for \( \theta \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The student's error is in the algebraic simplification: \( \sqrt{1+4r^2} \cdot r \) is not equal to \( (1+4r^2) \). The correct integral is \( 2\pi \int_0^1 r\sqrt{1+4r^2} \, dr \), which requires a substitution like \( u=1+4r^2 \). The Jacobian \( r \) is correctly included, and the expression \( 1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2 = 1 + 4r^2 \) is correct. The simplification error is a common but critical mistake that dramatically alters the result.

Q4. Consider the surface area of \( z = x^2 \) over the rectangle \( 0 \le x \le 1 \), \( 0 \le y \le 1 \). What is the difference between the surface area and the area of its projection onto the \( xz \)-plane?

A. \( \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \sqrt{1+4x^2} \, dx \, dy - \int_0^1 \int_0^1 1 \, dx \, dy \) ✅
B. \( \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \sqrt{1+4x^2} \, dy \, dx - \int_0^1 \int_0^1 2x \, dy \, dx \)
C. \( \int_0^1 \sqrt{1+4x^2} \, dx - 1 \)
D. \( \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \sqrt{1+4x^2} \, dx \, dy - \int_0^1 2x \, dx \)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The surface is \( z = x^2 \), so \( f_x = 2x \), \( f_y = 0 \). The surface area is \( \iint_R \sqrt{1 + 4x^2} \, dA \). The projection onto the \( xz \)-plane is the region where \( y \) is constant, and its area is the area of the rectangle \( 0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1 \), which is 1. Option A correctly identifies the difference as the surface area integral minus the area of the projection (which is 1). Option B incorrectly subtracts the integral of \( f_x \). Option C loses the \( y \)-dimension. Option D misrepresents the projection area.

Q5. Which of the following surfaces, over the unit square \( [0,1] \times [0,1] \), has the largest surface area?

A. \( z = x + y \)
B. \( z = x^2 + y^2 \)
C. \( z = \sin(\pi x) \sin(\pi y) \)
D. \( z = e^{x+y} \) ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

This requires comparing integrals without direct evaluation. The integrands are \( \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2} \). For A, \( f_x=f_y=1 \), integrand \( \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732 \). For B, \( f_x=2x, f_y=2y \), max integrand \( \sqrt{1+8} = 3 \) at (1,1), but average is lower. For C, derivatives vary from 0 to \( \pi \), integrand up to \( \sqrt{1+2\pi^2} \approx 4.58 \). For D, \( f_x=f_y=e^{x+y} \), integrand \( \sqrt{1+2e^{2(x+y)}} \), which is at least \( \sqrt{1+2} \approx 1.732 \) and grows exponentially, reaching \( \sqrt{1+2e^4} \approx 10.9 \) at (1,1). Over the entire square, D's integrand dominates, so its surface area is largest.

Q6. A surface is given by \( z = f(x,y) \) with \( f \) differentiable. If the gradient \( \nabla f \) is zero everywhere on \( R \), what is the surface area of the portion over \( R \)?

A. Equal to the area of \( R \) ✅
B. Greater than the area of \( R \)
C. Less than the area of \( R \)
D. Cannot be determined without knowing \( f \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

If \( \nabla f = (f_x, f_y) = (0,0) \) everywhere on \( R \), then \( f \) is constant on \( R \). The surface is a horizontal plane. For a horizontal plane \( z = c \), the surface area element is \( \sqrt{1+0+0} \, dA = dA \). Therefore, the surface area over \( R \) is exactly the area of \( R \). Option B is incorrect as a non-horizontal plane has greater area. Option C is impossible for a differentiable surface over a region. Option D is false because the condition fully determines the geometry.

Q7. Let \( R_1 \) be the disk \( x^2 + y^2 \le 1 \) and \( R_2 \) be the square \( [-1,1] \times [-1,1] \). For the surface \( z = x^2 - y^2 \), how do the surface areas \( S_1 \) and \( S_2 \) over \( R_1 \) and \( R_2 \) compare?

A. \( S_1 > S_2 \)
B. \( S_1 < S_2 \) ✅
C. \( S_1 = S_2 \)
D. The relationship depends on the specific function

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \( R_2 \) contains \( R_1 \) (the unit disk is inside the square), the surface area over \( R_2 \) is the integral over a larger region. The integrand \( \sqrt{1+4x^2+4y^2} \) is always positive. Therefore, the integral over the larger region \( R_2 \) must be strictly greater than the integral over the smaller region \( R_1 \). So \( S_1 < S_2 \). Option A is impossible. Option C would only be true if the integrand were zero outside the disk. Option D is incorrect because the containment is independent of the function, as long as the integrand is positive.

Q8. For the cone \( z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \), which parameterization correctly sets up the surface area integral over the disk \( x^2+y^2 \le a^2 \)?

A. \( \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a \sqrt{2} \, r \, dr \, d\theta \)
B. \( \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a \sqrt{1+r^2} \, r \, dr \, d\theta \)
C. \( \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a \sqrt{2} \, r \, dr \, d\theta \) ✅
D. \( \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a \sqrt{1+r^2} \, dr \, d\theta \)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

For \( z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \), we have \( f_x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \) and \( f_y = \frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \). Then \( 1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2 = 1 + \frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2} + \frac{y^2}{x^2+y^2} = 2 \). In polar coordinates, \( dA = r \, dr \, d\theta \). So the integral is \( \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a \sqrt{2} \, r \, dr \, d\theta \). Option A is the correct setup but is repeated as C. Option B incorrectly uses \( \sqrt{1+r^2} \). Option D misses the Jacobian \( r \). This tests the common misconception that \( f_r \) is used instead of the correct partial derivatives.

Q9. A surface \( z = f(x,y) \) has \( f_x = 0 \) and \( f_y = 0 \) at a single point \( P \). What is the significance of this point for the surface area integral?

A. The integrand \( \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2} \) has a local minimum at \( P \)
B. The surface area integral is minimized over all surfaces passing through \( P \)
C. The tangent plane at \( P \) is horizontal, so the surface area element equals \( dA \) locally ✅
D. The point \( P \) contributes zero area to the surface, so it can be ignored

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

At \( P \), \( f_x = f_y = 0 \), so the tangent plane is horizontal. The local surface area element \( dS = \sqrt{1+0+0} \, dA = dA \). This means locally, the surface is 'flat' and the area scaling factor is 1. While the integrand has a local minimum (since it's 1 at P and ≥1 elsewhere), it doesn't globally minimize the integral over all surfaces. Option B is false because the integral depends on the entire region. Option D is a dangerous misconception; a single point has measure zero and doesn't affect the integral value, but its geometric interpretation is important.

Q10. You are modeling a parabolic reflector \( z = x^2 + y^2 \) over a circular region. Which method would most efficiently compute its surface area, and why?

A. Use rectangular coordinates because the partial derivatives are simpler.
B. Use polar coordinates because the region and the function \( 1+f_x^2+f_y^2 \) simplify. ✅
C. Use a numerical method because the integral has no closed form.
D. Use spherical coordinates because it's a surface of revolution.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The function \( 1+f_x^2+f_y^2 = 1+4x^2+4y^2 = 1+4r^2 \) in polar coordinates. The region is a disk, so its bounds are constants in polar coordinates (\( 0 \le r \le a, 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi \)). This allows the integral to be separated and evaluated using a simple substitution. Option A is less efficient because the region bounds would involve square roots. Option C is incorrect; the integral \( \int r\sqrt{1+4r^2} dr \) has a closed form. Option D is inappropriate as spherical coordinates are for 3D volumes, not surface areas of graphs.

Q11. Suppose the surface area of \( z = f(x,y) \) over a region \( R \) is \( S \). If we scale the function vertically by a factor of \( k \) (i.e., \( z = k f(x,y) \)), what happens to the surface area?

A. It scales by a factor of \( k \)
B. It scales by a factor of \( k^2 \)
C. It increases, but the scaling is non-linear and depends on \( f_x \) and \( f_y \) ✅
D. It remains the same if \( R \) is not changed

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The new integrand is \( \sqrt{1 + k^2 f_x^2 + k^2 f_y^2} = \sqrt{1 + k^2 (f_x^2+f_y^2)} \). This is not a simple multiple of the original integrand \( \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2} \). The scaling factor depends on the magnitude of the gradient. For example, if \( f_x=f_y=0 \), the area stays the same. If the gradient is large, the area increases roughly by factor \( k \). Therefore, the scaling is non-linear and depends on the function's derivatives. Options A and B are false because they assume linearity. Option D is incorrect because changing \( f \) changes the surface.

Q12. Two surfaces, \( S_1: z = f(x,y) \) and \( S_2: z = f(x,y) + C \), where \( C \) is a constant, are given over the same region \( R \). What is the relationship between their surface areas?

A. \( S_2 = S_1 + \text{Area}(R) \)
B. \( S_2 = S_1 + C \)
C. \( S_2 > S_1 \) because the surface is higher
D. \( S_2 = S_1 \) ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

A vertical translation of a surface does not change its partial derivatives, i.e., \( f_x \) and \( f_y \) remain the same. The surface area integrand \( \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2} \) is unchanged. Therefore, the surface area over the same region \( R \) is identical. Options A and B mistakenly treat area as a linear function of height. Option C is a common physical intuition that is mathematically incorrect; shifting a plane vertically does not change its slope or area.

Q13. Which of the following is a correct interpretation of the surface area integral \( \iint_R \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2} \, dA \) if \( f \) represents the height of a tent over a region \( R \)?

A. The total volume of the tent
B. The total amount of fabric needed to cover the tent (excluding the base) ✅
C. The area of the shadow of the tent on the ground
D. The average height of the tent

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The surface area integral computes the area of the curved surface (the fabric). The tent's fabric is the graph of \( z=f(x,y) \). Option A is incorrect; volume is \( \iint f \, dA \). Option C is incorrect; the shadow is the area of \( R \). Option D is incorrect; average height is \( \frac{1}{\text{Area}(R)} \iint f \, dA \). This question connects the mathematical formula to a real-world context, reinforcing the interpretation of the surface area as the 'material' needed to cover the 3D shape.

Q14. A student is asked to find the surface area of \( z = x^2 y \) over the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), and (0,1). They write the integral as \( \int_0^1 \int_0^{1-x} \sqrt{1+4x^2 y^2 + x^4} \, dy \, dx \). Which part of their setup is incorrect?

A. The limits of integration are correct but the order is wrong.
B. The integrand is missing a factor of 2.
C. The partial derivative \( f_y \) is incorrectly computed.
D. The integrand should be \( \sqrt{1+4x^2 y^2 + x^4} \), so the setup is correct. ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

For \( f(x,y) = x^2 y \), we have \( f_x = 2xy \) and \( f_y = x^2 \). Then \( 1+f_x^2+f_y^2 = 1 + (2xy)^2 + (x^2)^2 = 1 + 4x^2 y^2 + x^4 \). The student's integral has the correct integrand and the correct limits for a triangular region \( 0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1-x \). The setup is completely correct. Option A is false because the limits are correct. Option B is false because no factor of 2 is missing. Option C is false because \( f_y = x^2 \) is correct. This question tests the ability to identify a correct setup amid plausible errors.
