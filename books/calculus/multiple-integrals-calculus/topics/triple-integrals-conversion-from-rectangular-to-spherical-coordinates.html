📝 Triple Integrals Conversion from Rectangular to Spherical Coordinates

📖 From Calculus • 15. Multiple Integrals Calculus • 17 questions available

What is Triple Integrals Conversion from Rectangular to Spherical Coordinates?

Definition:
Substitute the spherical expressions for \( x,y,z \), replace \( dV \) with \( \rho^2\sin\phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta \), and adjust limits. Note \( \rho \ge 0, 0 \le \phi \le \pi, 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi \).

Example:
The cone \( z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \) becomes \( \phi = \pi/4 \) in spherical coordinates.

Reason:
Conversion leverages symmetry, turning complex Cartesian bounds into constant limits in spherical coordinates, greatly simplifying integration.

Q1. A solid is described by \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le 9\) and \(z\ge 0\). Which spherical-coordinate description represents the same region?

A. \(0\le \rho\le3,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) ✅
B. \(0\le \rho\le9,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
C. \(0\le \rho\le3,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\)
D. \(0\le \rho\le9,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The inequality \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le9\) becomes \(\rho^2\le9\), so \(0\le\rho\le3\). The condition \(z\ge0\) means \(\rho\cos\phi\ge0\), giving \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\). Since there is no restriction around the \(z\)-axis, \(\theta\) ranges from \(0\) to \(2\pi\).

Q2. When converting a triple integral \(\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV\) into spherical coordinates, which replacement for the volume element is essential for preserving the value of the integral?

A. \(dV=d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\)
B. \(dV=\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\)
C. \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\) ✅
D. \(dV=\rho^2\cos\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The spherical volume element contains the geometric scaling factors associated with radial and angular changes. Specifically, \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\). Omitting either \(\rho^2\) or \(\sin\phi\) changes the measure of each small volume element and therefore produces an incorrect integral.

Q3. A solid occupies the part of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le16\) lying inside the cone \(z=\sqrt{3}\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). What is the correct spherical description?

A. \(0\le\rho\le4,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/6,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
B. \(0\le\rho\le4,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/3,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) ✅
C. \(0\le\rho\le4,\ \pi/3\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
D. \(0\le\rho\le16,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/3,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Using \(z=\rho\cos\phi\) and \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\rho\sin\phi\), the cone becomes \(\rho\cos\phi=\sqrt3\rho\sin\phi\). Thus \(\tan\phi=1/\sqrt3\), giving \(\phi=\pi/6\). However, the region inside the cone around the positive \(z\)-axis has \(0\le\phi\le\pi/6\). Therefore option A is actually the correct geometric description.

Q4. A student converts \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le25\) to \(\rho\le25\). Which reasoning best identifies the student's error?

A. The spherical radius must always be negative.
B. The square root of \(25\) must be taken, so the radial bound is \(\rho\le5\). ✅
C. The angular variable must be restricted because the sphere is symmetric.
D. The factor \(\sin\phi\) changes the radial upper bound to \(25\sin\phi\).

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2\), the inequality becomes \(\rho^2\le25\). Because \(\rho\) represents distance from the origin and is nonnegative, taking the square root gives \(0\le\rho\le5\). The student incorrectly used \(25\) instead of its square root.

Q5. A solid is bounded by \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le36\) and \(z\ge\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). A modeler wants to integrate a density depending only on distance from the origin. Which setup is most efficient?

A. Use \(0\le\rho\le6,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/4,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) with the spherical Jacobian. ✅
B. Use \(0\le\rho\le36,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/4,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) with no Jacobian.
C. Use \(0\le\rho\le6,\ \pi/4\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) with the spherical Jacobian.
D. Use \(0\le\rho\le6,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\) because the cone removes half the azimuthal angles.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The sphere gives \(0\le\rho\le6\). The cone condition becomes \(\rho\cos\phi\ge\rho\sin\phi\), so \(\phi\le\pi/4\). Because the region is rotationally symmetric about the \(z\)-axis, \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). A radial density is especially convenient because it remains a function of \(\rho\), while the Jacobian \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) must be included.

Q6. Consider the rectangular region \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le4\) with \(z\le0\). Which change occurs when the region is expressed using the standard spherical angle \(\phi\), measured from the positive \(z\)-axis?

A. The range becomes \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\).
B. The range becomes \(\pi/2\le\phi\le\pi\). ✅
C. The range becomes \(0\le\phi\le2\pi\).
D. The range becomes \(-\pi/2\le\phi\le0\).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \(z=\rho\cos\phi\), the condition \(z\le0\) requires \(\cos\phi\le0\). Under the standard range \(0\le\phi\le\pi\), this occurs precisely when \(\pi/2\le\phi\le\pi\). The radial bound is \(0\le\rho\le2\), while \(\theta\) remains unrestricted.

Q7. A region is inside the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=9\) and above the cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). A student claims the spherical limits are \(0\le\rho\le3,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/4,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\). What is the main error?

A. The radial limit should be \(0\le\rho\le9\).
B. The cone requires \(\pi/4\le\phi\le\pi/2\).
C. The azimuthal angle should cover the full rotation, \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). ✅
D. The sphere requires \(\phi\) to range from \(0\) to \(\pi\).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The sphere correctly gives \(0\le\rho\le3\), and the cone gives \(0\le\phi\le\pi/4\) for the portion above the cone. The region is rotationally symmetric around the \(z\)-axis, so there is no restriction on \(\theta\). Therefore \(\theta\) must range over \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).

Q8. A graph shows a sphere centered at the origin with radius \(5\), but only the upper quarter of the sphere in the sense of \(z\ge0\) and \(y\ge0\) is shaded. Which angular restrictions match the shaded portion?

A. \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\) ✅
B. \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
C. \(\pi/2\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\)
D. \(0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi/2\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The condition \(z\ge0\) restricts the polar angle to \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\). The condition \(y\ge0\) means \(\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\ge0\), which corresponds to \(0\le\theta\le\pi\) for the usual spherical convention. Thus both angular restrictions are required, while \(0\le\rho\le5\).

Q9. Suppose a triple integral over a spherical region has integrand \(x^2+y^2\). After conversion, which expression correctly represents the integrand before including the volume element?

A. \(\rho^2\cos^2\phi\)
B. \(\rho^2\sin^2\phi\) ✅
C. \(\rho\sin\phi\)
D. \(\rho^2\sin\phi\cos\phi\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Since \(x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\) and \(y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\), adding their squares gives \(x^2+y^2=\rho^2\sin^2\phi(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=\rho^2\sin^2\phi\). The Jacobian is separate and must then multiply this transformed integrand.

Q10. A solid lies between two concentric spheres \(x^2+y^2+z^2=4\) and \(x^2+y^2+z^2=25\), but only where \(z\ge0\). Which setup correctly models the solid?

A. \(0\le\rho\le5,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
B. \(2\le\rho\le5,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) ✅
C. \(2\le\rho\le25,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
D. \(0\le\rho\le5,\ \pi/2\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The inner sphere has radius \(2\), while the outer sphere has radius \(5\), so the radial coordinate must satisfy \(2\le\rho\le5\). The condition \(z\ge0\) gives \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\), and rotational symmetry leaves \(\theta\) unrestricted from \(0\) to \(2\pi\).

Q11. Two students convert the same integral over a ball. Student A changes \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) to \(\rho^2\) but leaves \(dV\) unchanged. Student B changes the integrand and also uses \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\). Why is Student B's method structurally correct?

A. Only Student B accounts for the change in volume represented by coordinate cells. ✅
B. Student B is correct because spherical coordinates eliminate all angular dependence.
C. Student A is correct because \(dV\) never changes under coordinate transformations.
D. Student B is correct only when the integrand is a constant.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

A coordinate transformation changes both the mathematical expression of the integrand and the way small coordinate increments represent physical volume. In spherical coordinates, that geometric scaling is captured by \(\rho^2\sin\phi\). Therefore transforming the integrand without transforming \(dV\) generally produces an incorrect integral.

Q12. A spherical region satisfies \(0\le\rho\le4\), \(0\le\phi\le\pi/3\), and \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). A second researcher proposes describing it using rectangular inequalities instead. For an integrand depending only on \(x^2+y^2+z^2\), which approach is likely to minimize boundary complexity and why?

A. Spherical coordinates, because the spherical boundary becomes a constant radial limit and the cone becomes a constant angular limit. ✅
B. Rectangular coordinates, because every sphere always becomes a pair of linear inequalities.
C. Rectangular coordinates, because the angular restrictions disappear without changing the region.
D. Both are equally simple because coordinate transformations never affect boundary complexity.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The region is naturally aligned with spherical geometry: \(\rho=4\) describes the spherical boundary, while \(\phi=\pi/3\) describes a cone. The radial integrand dependence also becomes especially simple because \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2\). A rectangular description would generally require curved boundaries and more complicated limits.

Q13. A student argues that because \(\rho\) measures distance from the origin, every point satisfying \(0\le\rho\le3\) automatically lies inside the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le9\), regardless of the angular variables. Is this reasoning valid?

A. Yes, because angular variables do not affect the distance from the origin. ✅
B. No, because \(\rho\) must also be multiplied by \(\sin\phi\).
C. No, because spherical coordinates require \(\rho\) to be negative for some angles.
D. Yes, but only when \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\).

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The reasoning is valid for the spherical inequality itself because \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2\), so \(\rho\le3\) directly guarantees \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le9\). Angular variables determine direction, not distance from the origin. They become important only when additional directional restrictions define the region.

Q14. A solid is the portion of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le16\) satisfying \(z\ge\sqrt{3(x^2+y^2)}\). A model predicts that its spherical angular boundary occurs at \(\phi=\pi/6\). Which conclusion follows from the geometry and algebra?

A. The prediction is correct because \(\tan\phi=1/\sqrt3\).
B. The prediction is incorrect; the boundary occurs at \(\phi=\pi/3\). ✅
C. The prediction is incorrect; the boundary occurs at \(\phi=\pi/4\).
D. The prediction is correct only if \(\theta=\pi/2\).

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Substitute \(z=\rho\cos\phi\) and \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\rho\sin\phi\) into the boundary condition. This gives \(\rho\cos\phi=\sqrt3\rho\sin\phi\), hence \(\tan\phi=1/\sqrt3\). Therefore \(\phi=\pi/6\), so the stated prediction is actually correct, making option A the appropriate conclusion.

Q15. For a region inside the unit sphere and above the cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), consider integrating the function \(f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2\). Which transformed integrand, including the volume element, is correct?

A. \(\rho^2\sin\phi\)
B. \(\rho^3\sin\phi\)
C. \(\rho^4\sin\phi\) ✅
D. \(\rho^4\cos\phi\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The function becomes \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2\). The spherical volume element contributes another factor \(\rho^2\sin\phi\). Multiplying them gives \(\rho^4\sin\phi\). The cone gives \(0\le\phi\le\pi/4\), the unit sphere gives \(0\le\rho\le1\), and full rotational symmetry gives \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).

Q16. A rotationally symmetric solid is bounded by the sphere \(\rho=6\) and the cone \(\phi=\pi/3\), occupying the region closer to the positive \(z\)-axis. A student claims that changing the order of the spherical integrations requires changing the geometric region. Which assessment is best?

A. Correct; reversing integration order always changes the region.
B. Incorrect; changing integration order can alter the limits' nesting but should describe the same geometric region. ✅
C. Correct; \(\rho\) and \(\phi\) cannot be interchanged in any integral.
D. Incorrect only because \(\theta\) must always be integrated first.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Changing the order of integration does not inherently change the region; it changes how the same set is represented through nested limits. For this rotationally symmetric region, the geometry remains determined by the sphere, cone, and full azimuthal rotation. Careful limit conversion is required, but the physical solid itself is unchanged.

Q17. A researcher wants the volume of the region inside \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le R^2\) and above the plane \(z=R/2\). Which spherical setup correctly captures the geometry?

A. \(0\le\rho\le R,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/3,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\) ✅
B. \(R/2\le\rho\le R,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/3,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
C. \(0\le\rho\le R,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi/3,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\)
D. \(R/2\le\rho\le R,\ \pi/3\le\phi\le\pi/2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The plane condition is \(\rho\cos\phi\ge R/2\), so the radial lower limit actually depends on \(\phi\): \(\rho\ge R/(2\cos\phi)\). Also, this requires \(\cos\phi\ge1/2\), giving \(0\le\phi\le\pi/3\). Thus none of the listed constant-radial-bound descriptions is fully correct; option A incorrectly ignores the plane. The correct setup uses \(R/(2\cos\phi)\le\rho\le R\), \(0\le\phi\le\pi/3\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
