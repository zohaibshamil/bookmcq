📝 Gradient vector properties

📖 From Calculus • 14. Partial Derivatives Calculus • 14 questions available

What is Gradient vector properties?

Definition:
Key properties: \( \nabla f \perp \) level curves/surfaces, \( |\nabla f| \) = max directional derivative, \( \nabla(cf) = c\nabla f \), \( \nabla(f+g) = \nabla f + \nabla g \).

Example:
On contour map, gradient arrows always cross elevation lines at right angles; doubling function doubles gradient magnitude.

Reason:
These algebraic and geometric properties make gradient computationally tractable and intuitively interpretable for navigation and field analysis.

Q1. A scalar field \( T(x,y) \) represents temperature on a metal plate. At point \( P \), \( \nabla T = \langle 3, -4 \rangle \). A particle moves through \( P \) with velocity vector \( \vec{v} = \langle 1, 2 \rangle \). What is the instantaneous rate of change of temperature experienced by the particle, and how does this relate to the gradient's geometric property?

A. The rate is 11, representing the projection of the gradient onto the velocity vector, showing directional dependence.
B. The rate is -5, calculated via dot product, demonstrating that the gradient measures spatial change independent of path speed. ✅
C. The rate is 5, which equals the magnitude of the gradient times the cosine of the angle between vectors, confirming orthogonality.
D. The rate is -5, but this value depends only on the gradient’s magnitude, not its direction relative to motion.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The instantaneous rate of change of a scalar field along a path is given by the dot product \( \nabla T \cdot \vec{v} \). Computing \( \langle 3, -4 \rangle \cdot \langle 1, 2 \rangle = 3 - 8 = -5 \). This reflects the core property that the gradient encodes directional derivatives via projection, emphasizing that physical rates depend on both field geometry and trajectory orientation, not just gradient magnitude alone.

Q2. Consider a function \( f(x,y) \) whose level curves are concentric ellipses centered at the origin. At a point \( Q \) on one such curve, a student claims the gradient vector must be tangent to the ellipse because it points in the direction of greatest increase. Which statement best identifies the flaw in this reasoning?

A. The gradient is indeed tangent; the error lies in assuming level curves represent constant rate of change rather than constant value.
B. The gradient is perpendicular to level curves, not tangent; the student confused direction of steepest ascent with direction of no change. ✅
C. The gradient is zero at all points on closed level curves, so tangency is undefined; the student ignored critical point conditions.
D. The gradient is parallel to the position vector, not the curve; the student misapplied symmetry properties of ellipses.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Level curves represent sets where \( f(x,y) = c \), meaning no change occurs along them. The gradient \( \nabla f \) always points orthogonal to these curves because it indicates the direction of maximum increase. Confusing tangency with orthogonality stems from misinterpreting 'direction of increase' as aligned with the curve. Correct understanding requires recognizing that zero directional derivative along the curve implies gradient normality, a foundational geometric property often misunderstood in multivariable calculus.

Q3. Given \( f(x,y) = x^2 y - y^3 \), suppose at point \( (a,b) \) the gradient is \( \langle 0, 0 \rangle \). A classmate argues this guarantees a local extremum at \( (a,b) \). Evaluate this claim using higher-order reasoning about gradient properties and critical point classification.

A. The claim is valid because vanishing gradient is sufficient for extrema in smooth functions.
B. The claim is invalid; zero gradient identifies critical points but cannot distinguish minima, maxima, or saddle points without second-derivative analysis. ✅
C. The claim is conditionally true only if the Hessian determinant is positive; otherwise, the gradient being zero implies non-differentiability.
D. The claim holds because gradient magnitude correlates directly with curvature sign near critical points.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

While \( \nabla f = \vec{0} \) is necessary for local extrema in differentiable functions, it is not sufficient. Critical points may be saddles where gradient vanishes but function increases in some directions and decreases in others. This question tests understanding that gradient properties alone cannot classify extrema; one must examine higher-order behavior. Recognizing this limitation prevents overgeneralization and reinforces the distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions in optimization theory.

Q4. An environmental model defines pollutant concentration as \( C(x,y,z) \). Sensors report \( \nabla C = \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle \) at location \( L \). Engineers want to place a barrier perpendicular to the direction of fastest pollutant spread. Which vector should guide barrier orientation, and why?

A. \( \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle \), because barriers must align with gradient to block flow effectively.
B. Any vector orthogonal to \( \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle \), since fastest spread is along the gradient and barriers should be normal to that direction. ✅
C. \( \langle -2, 1, -3 \rangle \), because negative gradient indicates inflow direction requiring opposite alignment.
D. A unit vector in the xy-plane, since vertical component is irrelevant for ground-level barriers.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Pollutants diffuse most rapidly in the direction of \( \nabla C \). To impede this spread maximally, a barrier must be oriented perpendicular to this direction, meaning its normal vector should be parallel to \( \nabla C \), or equivalently, the barrier surface itself should contain vectors orthogonal to \( \nabla C \). Thus, any vector orthogonal to the gradient defines a valid barrier plane orientation. This applies gradient geometry to real-world containment design, emphasizing practical interpretation over rote computation.

Q5. Examine a contour plot of \( g(x,y) \) where level curves become increasingly dense moving rightward. At point \( R \) in this region, compare \( \| \nabla g(R) \| \) to its value at a leftward point \( S \) with sparser contours. Justify your comparison using gradient-magnitude properties.

A. \( \| \nabla g(R) \| < \| \nabla g(S) \| \) because denser contours indicate slower change.
B. \( \| \nabla g(R) \| > \| \nabla g(S) \| \) since contour density inversely relates to gradient magnitude.
C. \( \| \nabla g(R) \| > \| \nabla g(S) \| \) because closer level curves imply greater rate of change per unit distance. ✅
D. Magnitudes are equal; contour spacing affects direction, not gradient strength.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Contour plots visually encode gradient magnitude: tighter spacing means the function changes more rapidly over shorter distances, corresponding to larger \( \| \nabla g \| \). Since \( R \) lies in a region of high contour density compared to \( S \), the gradient magnitude at \( R \) must exceed that at \( S \). This interpretation links abstract vector calculus concepts to graphical representation, testing ability to extract quantitative information from visual data without explicit formulas, a crucial skill in applied mathematics and scientific visualization.

Q6. Let \( h(x,y) = e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} \). Without computing partial derivatives explicitly, determine the direction of \( \nabla h \) at any point \( (x_0, y_0) \neq (0,0) \) using symmetry and functional structure. Explain your reasoning.

A. Radially outward from origin, because exponential decay implies increase toward center.
B. Tangential to circles centered at origin, due to rotational symmetry preserving function value.
C. Radially inward toward origin, since function decreases away from peak at center. ✅
D. Direction depends on quadrant; no universal radial pattern exists.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The function \( h \) depends solely on radial distance \( r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \), making it radially symmetric. As \( r \) increases, \( h \) decreases monotonically, so maximum increase occurs when \( r \) decreases—i.e., toward the origin. Thus, \( \nabla h \) must point radially inward everywhere except at the origin. This leverages composition rules and symmetry to infer gradient direction without differentiation, integrating conceptual understanding of composite functions with geometric intuition about level sets and monotonic transformations.

Q7. A student computes \( \nabla f \) for \( f(x,y) = \sin(xy) \) at \( (\pi/2, 1) \) and obtains \( \langle 0, 0 \rangle \). They conclude the function is locally constant near this point. Identify the specific error in linking gradient value to local behavior.

A. Assuming zero gradient implies constancy in a neighborhood, confusing critical points with flat regions. ✅
B. Miscalculating partial derivatives; actual gradient is nonzero at this point.
C. Believing sine functions cannot have isolated critical points due to periodicity.
D. Confusing gradient with Laplacian; constancy requires second derivatives to vanish.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

While \( \nabla f = \vec{0} \) at \( (\pi/2, 1) \) is correct (since \( \cos(\pi/2)=0 \)), this only indicates a critical point, not local constancy. Functions can have isolated zeros in gradient while varying nearby (e.g., saddle points). Local constancy would require \( \nabla f \equiv \vec{0} \) in an open set. The error reflects a common misconception equating instantaneous stationarity with regional flatness. Proper analysis demands examining higher-order terms or neighborhood behavior beyond first derivatives.

Q8. Suppose \( \nabla f(a,b) = \langle 4, -3 \rangle \). If you wish to move from \( (a,b) \) to decrease \( f \) as quickly as possible while constrained to stay on the line \( y = b \), what is the optimal direction and rate of decrease?

A. Leftward along x-axis with rate 4, since constraint eliminates y-component influence. ✅
B. Rightward along x-axis with rate -4, ignoring constraint violation.
C. No feasible direction exists; constraint makes descent impossible.
D. Downward along y-axis with rate 3, prioritizing unconstrained steepest descent.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

On the constraint \( y = b \), movement is restricted to horizontal direction \( \langle 1, 0 \rangle \). The directional derivative in this direction is \( \nabla f \cdot \langle 1, 0 \rangle = 4 \). To decrease \( f \), move opposite: direction \( \langle -1, 0 \rangle \) yields rate \( -4 \). This demonstrates constrained optimization using gradient projection, where full steepest descent isn't permissible. Students must reconcile unconstrained gradient properties with manifold restrictions, applying dot product selectively within feasible subspaces—a key skill in engineering and physics modeling.

Q9. For a differentiable function \( p(x,y) \), it is known that \( \nabla p \) is always parallel to the position vector \( \langle x, y \rangle \). What can be definitively concluded about the level curves of \( p \)?

A. They are straight lines passing through the origin.
B. They are circles centered at the origin. ✅
C. They are arbitrary curves symmetric about both axes.
D. They are logarithmic spirals emanating from origin.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

If \( \nabla p \parallel \langle x, y \rangle \), then gradient is radial everywhere. Level curves are orthogonal to gradient, so they must be perpendicular to radial lines—i.e., circular arcs centered at origin. Full circles arise if domain includes full rotation. This follows from the geometric duality between gradient direction and level set tangency. Recognizing this implication connects vector field structure to implicit curve geometry, illustrating how global gradient constraints dictate topological features of solutions without solving PDEs explicitly.

Q10. In thermodynamics, entropy \( S(U,V) \) has gradient \( \nabla S = \langle 1/T, P/T \rangle \). If during a process \( dS = 0 \), which relationship between \( dU \) and \( dV \) must hold, derived purely from gradient properties?

A. \( dU = -P \, dV \), from setting total differential to zero using gradient components. ✅
B. \( dU = T \, dV / P \), inverting gradient ratio incorrectly.
C. \( dU = 0 \) and \( dV = 0 \) independently, since gradient nonzero implies no admissible paths.
D. \( dU = P \, dV \), matching signs without considering conjugate variables.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The condition \( dS = 0 \) means displacement \( \langle dU, dV \rangle \) is orthogonal to \( \nabla S \). Thus, \( \nabla S \cdot \langle dU, dV \rangle = (1/T)dU + (P/T)dV = 0 \). Multiplying by \( T \) gives \( dU + P \, dV = 0 \), so \( dU = -P \, dV \). This derives fundamental thermodynamic relations directly from gradient orthogonality to level sets, showcasing cross-disciplinary application. It emphasizes that conservation laws emerge naturally from geometric properties of state functions, reinforcing unity between mathematical structure and physical principles.

Q11. A machine learning loss function \( L(w_1, w_2) \) has gradient \( \nabla L = \langle 2w_1, 4w_2 \rangle \). Starting at \( (1,1) \), gradient descent takes step size \( \alpha = 0.5 \). After one update, compute new position and explain why this step doesn’t reach minimum despite following negative gradient.

A. New position \( (0, -1) \); overshoot occurs because gradient magnitude varies anisotropically across coordinates. ✅
B. New position \( (0, 0) \); minimum reached exactly due to quadratic form.
C. New position \( (0, -1) \); error arises from using Euclidean metric instead of natural gradient.
D. New position \( (0.5, 0.5) \); insufficient step size prevents convergence.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Update rule: \( \vec{w}_{new} = \vec{w} - \alpha \nabla L = \langle 1,1 \rangle - 0.5 \langle 2,4 \rangle = \langle 0, -1 \rangle \). Minimum at \( (0,0) \) isn't reached because gradient components scale differently; y-direction has twice the curvature. Fixed step size causes oscillation in steeper dimension. Optimal step would require adaptive scaling or exact line search. This highlights limitation of naive gradient descent in ill-conditioned landscapes, connecting gradient properties to algorithmic performance—an advanced insight bridging pure math and computational practice.

By definition, level curves satisfy \( f(x,y) = c \), so traversing them produces zero elevation change. This recalls fundamental gradient properties: orthogonality to level sets and maximization of directional derivative. While seemingly basic, precise articulation distinguishes true understanding from vague intuition, forming bedrock for advanced applications in navigation, geophysics, and optimization where directional efficiency matters critically.

Q13. Function \( q(x,y) \) satisfies \( \nabla q \cdot \langle y, -x \rangle = 0 \) everywhere. What does this imply about \( q \)'s dependence on angular versus radial coordinates in polar system?

A. \( q \) depends only on radius \( r \), invariant under rotation. ✅
B. \( q \) depends only on angle \( \theta \), constant on rays from origin.
C. \( q \) is identically zero due to orthogonality constraint.
D. \( q \) separates as product \( R(r)\Theta(\theta) \) with specific coupling.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Vector \( \langle y, -x \rangle \) is tangential to circles centered at origin (angular direction in polar coords). Dot product zero means \( \nabla q \) has no angular component, so \( q \) doesn't change with \( \theta \). Hence \( q = q(r) \) only. This translates Cartesian gradient condition into polar coordinate independence, demonstrating coordinate-invariant interpretation of vector equations. Mastery requires recognizing geometric meaning of vector fields beyond algebraic manipulation, essential for solving PDEs and analyzing symmetric systems in physics and engineering contexts.

Q14. At point \( M \), measurements show directional derivatives: \( D_{\vec{u}}f = 6 \) for \( \vec{u}=\langle 1,0\rangle \), \( D_{\vec{v}}f = 8 \) for \( \vec{v}=\langle 0,1\rangle \). A researcher claims \( \| \nabla f(M) \| = 10 \). Assess validity considering possible inconsistencies.

A. Valid; Pythagorean combination of orthogonal directional derivatives gives gradient magnitude. ✅
B. Invalid; directional derivatives must satisfy Cauchy-Schwarz bound relative to gradient norm.
C. Conditionally valid only if \( \vec{u}, \vec{v} \) are principal axes of Hessian.
D. Invalid; gradient magnitude equals sum, not root-sum-square, of directional derivatives.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since \( \vec{u}, \vec{v} \) are orthonormal basis vectors, \( \nabla f = \langle D_{\vec{u}}f, D_{\vec{v}}f \rangle = \langle 6,8 \rangle \). Magnitude is \( \sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10 \). This uses the property that gradient components in orthonormal basis equal directional derivatives. Claim is valid under standard assumptions. However, if measurements were inconsistent (e.g., violating linearity), issue would arise—but here data is consistent. Question tests verification of internal consistency in empirical gradient estimation, blending measurement interpretation with theoretical constraints, vital in experimental sciences where noisy data must be validated against mathematical structure before use.
