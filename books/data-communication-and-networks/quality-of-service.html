📖 Quality of Service

How Quality Of Service Allocates Network Resources

Quality of Service (QoS) manages network resources to meet the different delivery requirements of applications. Voice, video, and interactive services require appropriate bandwidth, delay, jitter, and packet-loss levels, while ordinary data transfers can often tolerate greater variation. Scheduling, traffic shaping, policing, resource reservation, and admission control regulate how network traffic uses available capacity. Integrated Services (IntServ) provides per-flow resource management, whereas Differentiated Services (DiffServ) classifies traffic into service groups. These mechanisms build on routing and transport-layer delivery.


Packet Delivery And Traffic Measurement Fundamentals

Students should understand bandwidth, throughput, latency, jitter, packet loss, and network congestion. Familiarity with packet forwarding, queues, traffic flows, and transport protocols helps explain resource allocation. Basic knowledge of router scheduling, IP headers, and traffic classification also supports the study of IntServ, DiffServ, and resource reservation.


Skills For Managing Delay Sensitive Network Flows

Students should identify QoS requirements for different applications and classify network traffic into appropriate flow classes. They should explain scheduling, flow control, traffic shaping, and policing techniques; distinguish resource reservation from admission control; describe IntServ flow specifications and service classes; explain RSVP operations; and interpret DiffServ DS fields, per-hop behaviours, and traffic conditioners.


Flow Requirements Scheduling And Resource Allocation

  • QoS Flow Characteristics - Identifies bandwidth, delay, jitter, reliability, and loss requirements of different applications.
  • Flow Control And Scheduling - Explains transmission regulation and packet scheduling techniques for managing competing traffic.
  • Traffic Shaping And Policing - Describes token bucket and leaky bucket mechanisms for controlling transmission rates and bursts.
  • Resource Reservation And Admission Control - Examines bandwidth allocation and decisions about accepting new network flows.
  • Integrated Services And RSVP - Covers per-flow resource reservation, flow specifications, guaranteed service, and controlled-load service classes.
  • Differentiated Services Architecture - Explains DS field classification, per-hop behaviour, and traffic conditioning at network boundaries.

A Sequence From Flow Needs To Service Guarantees

Begin with application flow characteristics to understand why different traffic requires different treatment. Study scheduling and flow control before examining traffic shaping and policing. Next, distinguish resource reservation from admission control. Continue with IntServ, its flow specifications, service classes, and RSVP signalling. Finish with DiffServ to understand traffic classification and per-hop treatment. This sequence connects application requirements with the mechanisms that allocate and regulate network resources.


How IntServ And DiffServ Divide QoS Responsibilities

Flow characteristics establish the bandwidth and delay requirements used by scheduling and admission decisions. Traffic shaping regulates bursts before packets enter the network, while policing checks whether traffic conforms to configured limits. IntServ uses RSVP to request resources for individual flows, and routers decide whether the requested service can be supported. DiffServ instead marks aggregated traffic using the DS field and applies configured per-hop behaviours through traffic conditioners and scheduling mechanisms.


Reserving Network Capacity For A Voice Stream

A voice application at 192.0.2.10 sends traffic to 198.51.100.20 at 1.2 Mbps. It requests a 1.5 Mbps reservation through RSVP using a flow specification. Each participating router checks available resources before accepting the guaranteed-service request. A token bucket configured for 1.5 Mbps with a 64 KB burst allowance regulates traffic entering the network. In a DiffServ network, packets marked with the EF codepoint receive expedited forwarding treatment according to the configured network policy.


Why QoS Protects Real Time Applications

Without QoS mechanisms, delay-sensitive applications compete with other traffic without predictable resource allocation or differentiated treatment. Congestion can increase packet loss, delay, and jitter, degrading voice and video communication. Scheduling, reservation, shaping, and traffic classification help maintain application requirements and improve resource utilization across heavily loaded networks.


Separating Shaping Policing And Reservation

Students may say 'traffic shaping and policing perform identical operations'; shaping delays excess traffic to control its rate, while policing typically drops or re-marks nonconforming packets. They may say 'admission control reserves bandwidth'; reservation allocates resources, while admission control decides whether a request can be accepted. They may also say 'DiffServ reserves resources for every flow'; it generally applies treatment to aggregated traffic classes.


Calculations And Comparisons In QoS Problems

Exam questions commonly classify applications according to bandwidth, delay, jitter, and packet-loss sensitivity. Numerical problems test token bucket behaviour, traffic rates, and burst allowances. Students should compare scheduling and flow-control mechanisms, distinguish shaping from policing, and explain resource reservation and admission decisions. Protocol questions focus on RSVP messages, IntServ service classes, DS field interpretation, per-hop behaviours, and DiffServ traffic conditioners.


How Flow Treatment Shapes Network Quality

QoS connects application requirements with network resource-management decisions. Scheduling determines packet transmission order, while shaping and policing regulate traffic entering network paths. IntServ offers individual flow reservations with explicit service requirements, whereas DiffServ provides scalable treatment through traffic classes and per-hop behaviours. Together, these mechanisms help networks manage congestion, distribute available capacity, and provide different levels of service according to application requirements.


Standards Behind Integrated And Differentiated Services

  • RFC 2205 - Defines the Resource Reservation Protocol (RSVP) for requesting and maintaining network resource reservations.
  • RFC 2211 And RFC 2212 - Specify the Controlled-Load and Guaranteed Quality of Service service models for Integrated Services.
  • RFC 2474 - Defines the Differentiated Services field in the IPv4 and IPv6 headers.
  • RFC 2475 - Describes the Differentiated Services architecture, including traffic classification, conditioning, and per-hop behaviours.

Essential Terms For QoS Resource Management

  • Quality Of Service - The ability of a network to provide differentiated performance according to application requirements.
  • Traffic Shaping - Controls transmission rates by delaying packets that exceed configured traffic profiles.
  • Traffic Policing - Monitors traffic against configured limits and drops or re-marks nonconforming packets.
  • Admission Control - Determines whether sufficient network resources exist to accept a new flow.
  • IntServ - An Integrated Services architecture providing resource management and service guarantees for individual flows.
  • RSVP - A signalling protocol used to request and maintain resource reservations along network paths.
  • DS Field - An IP header field containing the Differentiated Services Code Point and Explicit Congestion Notification bits.
  • Per-Hop Behaviour - The forwarding treatment applied to packets belonging to a particular DiffServ traffic class.
