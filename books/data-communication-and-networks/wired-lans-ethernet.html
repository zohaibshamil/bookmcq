📖 Wired LANs: Ethernet

Wired LANs Ethernet From Shared Buses To Switched Links

Ethernet provides a widely adopted framework for communication across wired local area networks. Its development spans early coaxial bus systems, twisted-pair connections, switched Fast Ethernet, Gigabit Ethernet, and 10 Gigabit Ethernet. IEEE 802.3 defines Ethernet standards, while MAC addressing and frame structures organize local delivery. CSMA/CD controls access on traditional shared half-duplex networks, and successive Ethernet generations increase transmission speeds and improve physical-layer capabilities. Together, these developments show how Ethernet maintains compatibility while supporting changing network requirements.

Signal And Address Basics For Ethernet Frames

Students should understand physical transmission media, binary data, data-link frames, MAC addresses, and shared communication channels. Knowledge of CSMA/CD, collision detection, and transmission efficiency provides the foundation for traditional Ethernet operation. Basic familiarity with copper cables, optical fiber, network topologies, and data rates supports comparisons between Ethernet generations.

Abilities For Analyzing Ethernet Media And Frames

Students should identify IEEE 802 standards, explain Ethernet development, and interpret standard Ethernet frame fields and MAC addresses. They should describe CSMA/CD operation and calculate traditional Ethernet efficiency, distinguish 10BASE5, 10BASE2, and 10BASE-T implementations, and compare Fast Ethernet, Gigabit Ethernet, and 10 Gigabit Ethernet physical-layer technologies. They should also explain Gigabit Ethernet carrier extension and identify the operating characteristics of 100BASE-TX and 1000BASE-X.

A Progression From Original Ethernet To 10 Gigabit

Start with IEEE 802 standards and Ethernet history to understand the development of wired LAN technologies. Next, learn the standard frame format and MAC addressing before studying CSMA/CD and efficiency calculations. Compare the original coaxial and twisted-pair implementations, then move to Fast Ethernet and 100BASE-TX. Finish with Gigabit Ethernet, carrier extension, 1000BASE-X, and 10 Gigabit Ethernet to understand successive performance improvements.

How Ethernet Framing And Access Methods Evolved

The IEEE 802.3 framework establishes the rules governing Ethernet communication. MAC addresses identify local interfaces, while frame fields organize transmitted information. Traditional shared Ethernet uses CSMA/CD to manage collisions, making propagation delay important to efficiency. Fast Ethernet increases the data rate while retaining Ethernet framing. Gigabit Ethernet extends these principles, with carrier extension supporting half-duplex operation, while modern 10 Gigabit Ethernet uses full-duplex communication without CSMA/CD.

A Frame Crossing A Switched Ethernet Network

A workstation sends a 1,500-byte payload through a 100BASE-TX connection to an Ethernet switch. The resulting untagged Ethernet frame contains 1,518 bytes, including 14 bytes of header and 4 bytes of FCS. The switch examines the 48-bit destination MAC address and forwards the frame through its appropriate port. A 1000BASE-X optical uplink carries the frame toward another switch, and a 10 Gigabit Ethernet connection provides higher-speed connectivity to the network core.

Why Ethernet Compatibility Supports Network Growth

Without standardized framing, addressing, and physical-layer specifications, devices from different manufacturers would face compatibility problems. Ethernet generations provide increasingly capable transmission options while maintaining familiar frame structures and addressing principles. These foundations support switched LAN design, network upgrades, Ethernet troubleshooting, and enterprise network infrastructure.

Separating Ethernet Speeds Frames And Collision Rules

Students may say 'all Ethernet networks use CSMA/CD'; modern switched full-duplex Ethernet does not require collision detection. They may say '100BASE-TX and 1000BASE-X use identical media'; 100BASE-TX uses twisted-pair copper, whereas 1000BASE-X specifies physical-layer variants using optical fiber or other supported media. They may also say 'carrier extension increases the actual frame payload'; it extends the carrier event, not the original frame data.

Calculations And Comparisons In Ethernet Questions

Exam questions frequently test Ethernet frame fields, MAC address length, and minimum and maximum frame sizes. Numerical problems involve CSMA/CD efficiency, propagation delay, and transmission time. Comparison questions distinguish 10BASE5, 10BASE2, and 10BASE-T, followed by 10 Mbps, 100 Mbps, 1 Gbps, and 10 Gbps generations. Students should identify 100BASE-TX and 1000BASE-X specifications and explain Gigabit Ethernet carrier extension.

How Ethernet Connects Media Addressing And Speed

Ethernet combines standardized frames, MAC addressing, and physical transmission technologies into a consistent LAN communication system. Traditional shared networks rely on collision management, while switched full-duplex links provide more efficient communication. Fast Ethernet, Gigabit Ethernet, and 10 Gigabit Ethernet extend transmission capacity through improved physical-layer specifications. Their evolution demonstrates how standardized interfaces allow network infrastructure to expand without abandoning fundamental Ethernet communication principles.

IEEE Specifications For Ethernet Generations

  • IEEE 802.3 Ethernet Standard - Defines Ethernet MAC operations, frame structures, and physical-layer specifications.
  • IEEE 802.3u Fast Ethernet - Introduced standardized 100 Mbps Ethernet technologies, including 100BASE-TX.
  • IEEE 802.3z Gigabit Ethernet - Established 1000 Mbps Ethernet specifications, including the 1000BASE-X family.
  • IEEE 802.3ae 10 Gigabit Ethernet - Defines 10 Gbps Ethernet physical-layer specifications and full-duplex operation.

Essential Terms For Ethernet LAN Design

  • IEEE 802.3 - The family of standards defining Ethernet communication and physical-layer technologies.
  • Ethernet Frame - A data-link unit containing destination and source MAC addresses, control information, payload, and FCS.
  • MAC Address - A 48-bit link-layer address used to identify an Ethernet interface.
  • CSMA/CD - A shared-medium access method that senses transmission activity and detects collisions.
  • 10BASE-T - A traditional 10 Mbps Ethernet implementation using twisted-pair cabling.
  • 100BASE-TX - A Fast Ethernet physical-layer standard providing 100 Mbps transmission over twisted-pair copper.
  • 1000BASE-X - A Gigabit Ethernet physical-layer family commonly associated with optical-fiber links and 8B/10B encoding.
  • Carrier Extension - A half-duplex Gigabit Ethernet mechanism that extends short transmission events to preserve the collision-domain slot time.
