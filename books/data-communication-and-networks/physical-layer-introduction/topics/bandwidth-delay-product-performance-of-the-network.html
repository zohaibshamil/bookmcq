📝 Bandwidth Delay Product performance of the network

📖 From Data Communication and Networks • 3. Physical Layer Introduction • 12 questions available

What is Bandwidth Delay Product performance of the network?

The bandwidth-delay product is the multiplication of link bandwidth and round-trip latency, representing the maximum amount of unacknowledged data that can be in transit at any moment, and determines the required buffer sizes and window parameters for efficient utilization because if the sender's window is smaller than this product, the link will be underutilized regardless of available bandwidth.

Q1. What does the bandwidth‑delay product represent in a communication link?

A. The product of channel capacity and signal attenuation
B. The maximum number of bits that can fill the link at any instant ✅
C. The ratio of propagation delay to bandwidth
D. The minimum time to transmit a single bit

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The bandwidth‑delay product (BDP) is defined as the bandwidth multiplied by the propagation delay of a link. It indicates the amount of data that can be \in flight\ or stored in the pipe, i.e., the maximum number of bits that can occupy the link simultaneously. This metric is essential for sizing buffers and TCP windows.

Q2. A link has a bandwidth of 10 Mbps and a propagation delay of 50 ms. What is the bandwidth‑delay product, and what is the minimum TCP window size needed to fully utilize the link?

A. 500 kb bits, 62.5 kB
B. 500,000 bits, 62.5 kB ✅
C. 5 Mb, 625 kB
D. 50 kb, 6.25 kB

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

First compute the BDP: \(10\,\text{Mbps}\times0.05\,\text{s}=500{,}000\,\text{bits}\). To keep the pipe full, the TCP window must be at least this size, which is \(500{,}000/8=62.5\,\text{kB}\). Therefore the correct pair is 500 000 bits and 62.5 kB.

Q3. If a receiver’s buffer can hold only 40 kB while the link’s bandwidth‑delay product is 80 kB, what is the most likely impact on throughput?

A. Throughput will be limited to half the link capacity ✅
B. The link will be fully utilized
C. Delay will increase beyond propagation delay
D. Error rate will increase

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Since the BDP (80 kB) exceeds the available buffer (40 kB), the sender cannot keep the pipe full. Only half of the potential data can be in flight, so the effective throughput is roughly half of the link’s raw capacity, causing under‑utilization.

Q4. Two links have the same bandwidth of 5 Mbps. Link X has a propagation delay of 20 ms, while Link Y has 40 ms. Which link has the larger bandwidth‑delay product?

A. Link X
B. Link Y ✅
C. Both have equal BDP
D. Cannot determine

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

BDP is calculated as bandwidth multiplied by delay. With identical bandwidth, the link with the larger delay (Link Y, 40 ms) yields a larger product, so Link Y has the greater BDP.

Q5. If the bandwidth of a link is doubled while its propagation delay remains unchanged, how does the bandwidth‑delay product change?

A. It doubles ✅
B. It halves
C. It stays the same
D. It quadruples

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The BDP formula is \( \text{BDP}= \text{bandwidth}\times\text{delay}\). Doubling the bandwidth while keeping delay constant multiplies the product by two, so the BDP doubles.

Q6. A link’s bandwidth is reduced from 100 Mbps to 50 Mbps and its propagation delay is reduced from 40 ms to 20 ms. What is the net effect on the bandwidth‑delay product?

A. It remains the same
B. It halves
C. It doubles
D. It decreases by 75 % ✅

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Original BDP: \(100\,\text{Mbps}\times0.04\,\text{s}=4\times10^{6}\,\text{bits}\). New BDP: \(50\,\text{Mbps}\times0.02\,\text{s}=1\times10^{6}\,\text{bits}\). The product drops from 4 Mbits to 1 Mbit, a reduction of 75 %, so the correct choice is a 75 % decrease.

Q7. A satellite link has a round‑trip time of 500 ms and a bandwidth of 2 Mbps. Approximate the bandwidth‑delay product for this link.

A. 1 Mb
B. 250 kB
C. 125 kB ✅
D. 500 kB

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Using the round‑trip time, the one‑way propagation delay is 250 ms. BDP = \(2\,\text{Mbps}\times0.25\,\text{s}=0.5\,\text{Mbit}=125\,\text{kB}\). Thus the approximate BDP is 125 kB.

Q8. How does the bandwidth‑delay product influence the choice between stop‑and‑wait ARQ and sliding‑window protocols?

A. Higher BDP favors stop‑and‑wait because acknowledgments arrive quickly
B. Higher BDP favors sliding‑window to keep the pipe full ✅
C. BDP does not affect protocol choice
D. Sliding‑window is only used when BDP is zero

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A large BDP means a lot of data can be in transit simultaneously. Stop‑and‑wait cannot keep the pipe full, so sliding‑window protocols are preferred because they allow multiple outstanding frames, matching the pipe capacity and improving utilization.

Q9. Explain why the bandwidth‑delay product is a critical factor for designing high‑speed networks, particularly in relation to the concept of “pipe filling”.

A. It determines the maximum packet size that can be transmitted
B. It indicates the amount of data that can be in transit, so a large BDP requires larger buffers and window sizes to keep the pipe full ✅
C. It measures the physical length of the cable
D. It is unrelated to throughput

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The BDP measures how many bits can occupy the link at any moment, analogous to the amount of water in a pipe. In high‑speed networks, a large BDP means the pipe can hold many bits, requiring proportionally larger buffers and TCP window sizes to keep it filled; otherwise, the link remains under‑utilized.

Q10. A link has a bandwidth‑delay product of 1 Gbit. If TCP uses a window scaling factor of 8 (i.e., the window size can be multiplied by 2^8), what is the maximum achievable throughput assuming no other constraints?

A. 1 Gbps ✅
B. 256 Mbps
C. 8 Gbps
D. 2 Gbps

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Even with a large scaling factor, throughput cannot exceed the physical capacity of the link. The BDP simply tells the amount of data that can be in flight; the maximum achievable throughput is limited by the link’s bandwidth, which is 1 Gbps.

Q11. When approximating a digital signal with the first harmonic versus the first three harmonics, how does the required bandwidth and resulting bandwidth‑delay product compare for a fixed bit rate?

A. Using three harmonics reduces required bandwidth and BDP
B. Using three harmonics increases required bandwidth but does not change BDP
C. Using three harmonics increases required bandwidth, thus increasing BDP proportionally ✅
D. Bandwidth and BDP remain unchanged regardless of harmonics

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Adding the third and fifth harmonics raises the highest frequency component, so the required bandwidth grows (approximately three‑times for the first three harmonics). Because BDP equals bandwidth times delay, a larger bandwidth directly raises the BDP proportionally.

Q12. A low‑pass channel provides a bandwidth of 200 kHz. Using only the first harmonic, what is the maximum bit rate that can be transmitted, and what is the resulting bandwidth‑delay product if the propagation delay is 2 ms?

A. 400 kbps, 800 bits ✅
B. 200 kbps, 400 bits
C. 400 kbps, 400 bits
D. 200 kbps, 800 bits

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

With the first harmonic, the maximum bit rate is twice the bandwidth: \(2\times200\,\text{kHz}=400\,\text{kbps}\). Multiplying this rate by the 2 ms delay gives a BDP of \(400{,}000\times0.002=800\,\text{bits}\). Hence the correct pair is 400 kbps and 800 bits.
