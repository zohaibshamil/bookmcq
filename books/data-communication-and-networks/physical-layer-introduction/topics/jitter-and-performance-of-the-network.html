📝 Jitter and performance of the network (10 MCQs)

Variation in Packet Delay

Jitter is variation in packet delay over time. Packets sent at regular intervals may arrive with changing gaps because of queuing, congestion, route changes, or scheduling differences. Jitter differs from latency: latency describes delay, while jitter describes how that delay varies across packets or measurements. This establishes the physical representation at both communication endpoints. Compatible transmitter and receiver conventions are required.

Sources of Delay Variation

Variable router queue lengths are a common source of jitter. Competing traffic, wireless interference, link-layer retransmissions, and different paths can also change packet arrival times. A stable network path may still have nonzero latency, but its packet delays can vary only slightly. These properties are selected to match the physical medium.

Observing Packet Arrival Variation

A sender transmits voice packets every 20 milliseconds. If they arrive after delays of 30, 35, 31, and 45 milliseconds, their one-way delays vary. The changing arrival intervals can disrupt playback even though every packet may eventually reach the destination. The receiver uses this sequence to reconstruct the intended data.

A Simple Jitter Comparison

Suppose four packets have measured one-way delays of 20, 22, 21, and 37 milliseconds. The delays are not constant, and the final packet experiences a much larger delay. The spread indicates delay variation; a precise jitter value depends on the measurement definition used. This isolates the relevant principle using realistic link parameters.

Jitter Buffers and Real-Time Applications

Voice and video applications often use a jitter buffer to hold packets briefly and play them at a regular rate. This can smooth arrival variation but adds buffering delay. Excessive jitter can cause late packets to be discarded, gaps in audio, or uneven video playback. It therefore influences end-to-end communication quality.

Jitter and Latency Misconceptions

'Jitter is another word for high latency' is incorrect because a constant large delay can have low jitter. 'A jitter buffer removes network delay' is false because it trades additional buffering time for smoother playback. 'Only lost packets cause jitter' is incorrect; packets can arrive late or at uneven intervals without being lost.

Interpreting Jitter in Performance Questions

Distinguish average latency from the variation among packet delays, and state the metric or calculation convention if one is provided. Use packet timestamps or delay samples to compare arrival consistency. Explain how jitter buffers improve playback regularity while potentially increasing end-to-end latency. Show the formula, units, assumptions, and intermediate steps.

Jitter and Real-Time Traffic Terms

  • Jitter - Variation in packet delay.
  • Packet delay - Time for packet delivery.
  • Jitter buffer - Buffer smoothing packet arrival variation.
  • Real-time traffic - Traffic requiring timely delivery.
  • Queuing - Waiting in a network buffer.
  • Late packet - Packet missing playback deadline.

📝 All Jitter and performance of the network MCQs

Q1. What is the allocated bandwidth per FM radio station in the United States?

A.10 kHz
B.200 kHz ✅
C.500 kHz
D.1 MHz
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: FM stations are assigned a much wider channel than AM stations to accommodate the larger frequency deviation of frequency‑modulated signals. In the United States each FM station occupies a 200 kHz slice of spectrum, which is the standard allocation defined by the FCC.

Q2. If jitter increases the effective frequency spread of a digital signal by 10 %, how does the required bandwidth change?

A.Increase by 10 % ✅
B.Increase by 5 %
C.Remain unchanged
D.Decrease by 10 %
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Jitter adds uncertainty to the timing of signal transitions, effectively spreading the signal’s spectral components. If the spread increases by 10 %, the Nyquist bandwidth must also increase by roughly the same proportion to faithfully capture the signal without distortion, so the required bandwidth rises by 10 %.

Q3. Considering that AM stations have a 10‑kHz bandwidth while FM stations have a 200‑kHz bandwidth, which statement best explains the difference in jitter tolerance?

A.AM can tolerate more jitter because its bandwidth is narrower
B.FM tolerates less jitter due to larger bandwidth
C.FM’s larger bandwidth provides greater margin for timing variations, making it more tolerant to jitter ✅
D.Both AM and FM have equal jitter tolerance
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: FM’s 200 kHz bandwidth provides a larger guard band around the carrier, allowing the system to tolerate greater timing variations (jitter) without causing inter‑symbol interference. AM’s narrow 10 kHz channel offers less margin, making FM more robust to jitter‑induced frequency excursions.

Q4. A signal has eight discrete amplitude levels. How many bits can each level represent?

A.2 bits
B.3 bits ✅
C.4 bits
D.5 bits
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The number of bits that can be represented by each level is given by the binary logarithm of the number of levels: \(\log_2 8 = 3\). Therefore each of the eight amplitude levels can encode three bits of information per symbol.

Q5. Given a resolution of 525×700 scanned 30 times per second, what is the minimum bandwidth required assuming 2 pixels per cycle?

A.5.5124 MHz ✅
B.3.85 MHz
C.4 MHz
D.6 MHz
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The total pixel rate is \(525 \times 700 \times 30 = 11{,}025{,}000\) pixels per second. In the worst case two pixels can be sent per cycle, so the minimum cycle rate is \(11{,}025{,}000 / 2 = 5.5125 \times 10^6\) Hz, i.e., about 5.5124 MHz bandwidth.

Q6. If jitter causes a timing uncertainty of 0.2 µs in the pixel clock of the TV example, what is the maximum percentage error in the estimated bandwidth of 5.5124 MHz?

A.0.1
B.0.35
C.1.1 ✅
D.2
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The nominal period of a 5.5124 MHz signal is \(T = 1 / 5.5124\times10^{6} \approx 181.5\) ns. A jitter of 0.2 µs equals 200 ns, which is \(200 / 181.5 \approx 1.10\) or 110 % of the period. Thus the timing error could exceed the entire cycle, causing a >100 % error in the bandwidth estimate.

Q7. If a digital modulation scheme uses 16 levels, how many bits per symbol are transmitted?

A.2 bits
B.3 bits
C.4 bits ✅
D.5 bits
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: For a modulation scheme with \(L = 16\) discrete levels, the bits per symbol are \(\log_2 16 = 4\). This means each transmitted symbol carries four bits of information, allowing higher data rates compared with lower‑level schemes.

Q8. A system requires synchronization within ±5 kHz of a 240 kHz carrier. If jitter introduces a frequency deviation of 7 kHz, what is the likely outcome?

A.Synchronization is maintained
B.Synchronization is lost ✅
C.Carrier frequency is shifted
D.System crashes
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The system’s synchronization tolerance is ±5 kHz. A jitter‑induced deviation of 7 kHz exceeds this limit, causing the received carrier to fall outside the permitted range. Consequently the receiver cannot maintain proper synchronization, leading to loss of lock.

Q9. Analog color TV channel is allocated 6 MHz bandwidth. If the video portion uses 70 % of this bandwidth, how many MHz remain for audio and synchronization?

A.1.8 MHz ✅
B.2.0 MHz
C.2.1 MHz
D.2.2 MHz
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: If 70 % of the 6 MHz channel is used for video, the remaining portion is \(30\% \times 6\text{ MHz} = 1.8\text{ MHz}\). This residual bandwidth is allocated to audio, control, and synchronization signals, ensuring the overall channel meets broadcasting standards.

Q10. Jitter in a digital communication system refers to:

A.Amplitude variation
B.Timing variation ✅
C.Frequency variation
D.Phase variation
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Jitter describes the short‑term variations in the timing of signal edges relative to their ideal positions. It is a temporal phenomenon, unlike amplitude, frequency, or phase variations, and directly impacts the reliability of digital data recovery.
