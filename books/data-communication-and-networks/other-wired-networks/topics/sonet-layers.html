Q1. Why does SONET use a synchronous framing structure instead of an asynchronous one?

🔹 A. It allows variable-length frames to be accommodated
🔹 B. It simplifies routing decisions
🔹 C. It ensures that all nodes transmit and receive at the same rate, eliminating timing drift
🔹 D. It reduces the need for error correction

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The synchronous framing guarantees that every node in the SONET network operates on a common clock, so the start of each frame is predictable. This eliminates timing drift that would occur with asynchronous framing, ensuring consistent alignment of payload and overhead across the entire link, which is essential for high‑speed transport.

Q2. Which statement correctly compares an STS‑1 and an STS‑3c in terms of bandwidth and multiplexing?

🔹 A. STS‑3c provides three times the bandwidth of STS‑1 and is formed by concatenating three STS‑1 signals without additional overhead
🔹 B. STS‑3c has the same bandwidth as STS‑1 but uses three separate wavelengths
🔹 C. STS‑3c is a lower‑rate variant of STS‑1 designed for voice channels
🔹 D. STS‑3c requires separate framing for each constituent STS‑1

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

An STS‑3c is created by concatenating three STS‑1 signals, yielding roughly three times the bandwidth (≈ 155 Mbps × 3 ≈ 465 Mbps). The concatenation is performed without adding extra overhead, unlike separate STS‑1 streams, allowing a single higher‑capacity payload to be carried efficiently.

Q3. In SONET, a Virtual Container (VC) is mapped into a payload area. How does this mapping support transport of PDH signals?

🔹 A. VC mapping adds extra framing bytes, increasing latency
🔹 B. VCs are encapsulated in Ethernet frames before transport
🔹 C. VCs are directly placed into the physical layer without any overhead
🔹 D. VCs are placed into the payload area, allowing the original PDH framing to be preserved inside the SONET container

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The VC is inserted into the SONET payload, preserving the PDH sub‑frame structure inside the container. This allows existing PDH tributaries to be carried unchanged, facilitating migration to SONET without redesigning the lower‑level equipment while keeping the overhead minimal.

Q4. If the overhead bytes in the SONET frame increase by 10 % while the payload remains unchanged, what is the most direct effect on the effective data throughput?

🔹 A. Overall throughput increases proportionally
🔹 B. Effective throughput decreases because a larger proportion of the frame is occupied by overhead
🔹 C. Throughput remains unchanged as overhead does not affect payload
🔹 D. The frame size expands, keeping throughput constant

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Increasing overhead consumes a larger fraction of each frame’s total bits, leaving fewer bits available for user data. Since the payload size is unchanged, the ratio of payload to total bits drops, directly reducing the effective data throughput that applications can achieve on the link.

Q5. How does increasing the line rate from STS‑1 (≈ 51 Mbps) to STS‑12 (≈ 622 Mbps) affect the end‑to‑end latency for a fixed‑size packet, assuming processing delay is negligible?

🔹 A. Latency increases proportionally with bandwidth
🔹 B. Latency remains the same because propagation delay dominates
🔹 C. Latency decreases because higher line rates allow faster serialization of the packet
🔹 D. Latency decreases due to reduced serialization time, assuming processing delay is negligible

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

When the line rate rises, the time needed to serialize a packet (packet size ÷ line rate) shrinks dramatically. With processing delay ignored, the dominant component becomes serialization, so the packet traverses the link more quickly, yielding lower end‑to‑end latency.

Q6. Explain how SONET’s hierarchical multiplexing enables a network operator to scale capacity without redesigning the entire physical layer.

🔹 A. SONET requires a complete redesign of hardware for each new capacity level
🔹 B. SONET’s fixed‑size frames can be concatenated, allowing lower‑rate signals to be embedded within higher‑rate frames without altering the physical layer
🔹 C. SONET uses adaptive modulation to change bandwidth on the fly
🔹 D. SONET scales capacity by increasing the voltage of the optical signal

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

SONET frames are defined in a hierarchy where lower‑rate containers (e.g., STS‑1) can be mapped into higher‑rate payloads (e.g., STS‑12). By simply adding multiplexers and demultiplexers, operators increase capacity while keeping the same optical infrastructure, avoiding wholesale hardware replacement.

Q7. A regenerator in a SONET ring fails, causing a loss of signal on one span. What is the most likely immediate impact on the ring’s protection switching mechanism?

🔹 A. The ring will immediately invoke its automatic protection switching (APS) to reroute traffic around the failed span
🔹 B. Only the affected span will stop transmitting, while the rest of the ring continues normally
🔹 C. The entire ring shuts down to prevent data corruption
🔹 D. Traffic is delayed until the regenerator is manually repaired

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

When a span loses signal, SONET’s built‑in Automatic Protection Switching (APS) detects the fault and quickly reroutes traffic onto the alternate direction of the ring. This switchover occurs within milliseconds, preserving continuity of service while the failed regenerator is repaired.

Q8. Compare the error detection capabilities of SONET’s cyclic redundancy check (CRC) with Ethernet’s frame check sequence (FCS). Which statement is accurate?

🔹 A. Both SONET CRC and Ethernet FCS use a 16‑bit polynomial, providing identical detection capabilities
🔹 B. Ethernet FCS can detect more error patterns because it operates at a lower layer
🔹 C. SONET CRC uses a 32‑bit polynomial that provides robust detection of longer burst errors, while Ethernet’s 32‑bit FCS, though using the same polynomial, is optimized for shorter Ethernet frames, making SONET’s error detection slightly stronger for large frames
🔹 D. Ethernet does not employ any error detection mechanism

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Both SONET and Ethernet employ a 32‑bit CRC, but SONET frames are much longer, so the CRC’s ability to detect long burst errors becomes more critical. Consequently, SONET’s CRC offers slightly stronger protection against multi‑bit errors in large frames, whereas Ethernet’s FCS is tuned for its shorter frame lengths.

Q9. How does the pointer adjustment mechanism in SONET allow the transport of asynchronous payloads without causing phase misalignment?

🔹 A. The pointer is fixed, so asynchronous payloads cannot be transported
🔹 B. Pointer adjustment adds delay to synchronize payloads
🔹 C. Pointer changes are ignored, causing phase errors
🔹 D. The pointer indicates the start of the payload; by adjusting it, SONET can align the payload’s byte boundaries without shifting the entire frame, allowing asynchronous data to be carried transparently

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

The pointer field tells the receiver where the payload begins within the frame. By incrementing or decrementing this pointer, SONET can compensate for slight timing differences of an asynchronous payload, keeping the overall frame phase stable while ensuring the payload’s bytes are correctly aligned for extraction.

Q10. Given a SONET optical carrier OC‑48 (≈ 2.5 Gbps) carrying VC‑4 containers of 150 Mbps each, what fraction of the total bandwidth is allocated to VC‑4 payloads?

🔹 A. About 2 %
🔹 B. Approximately 6 %
🔹 C. Roughly 12 %
🔹 D. Near 25 %

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

OC‑48 provides roughly 2.5 Gbps. Each VC‑4 container carries 150 Mbps, so the proportion is 150 Mbps ÷ 2 500 Mbps ≈ 0.06, or about 6 % of the total optical bandwidth, with the remainder used for overhead, other VCs, and management channels.

Q11. What is the standard line rate of an STS‑1 signal in SONET?

🔹 A. ≈ 51 Mbps
🔹 B. ≈ 155 Mbps
🔹 C. ≈ 622 Mbps
🔹 D. ≈ 2.5 Gbps

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

An STS‑1 (Synchronous Transport Signal level‑1) is the fundamental SONET channel and operates at a line rate of approximately 51.84 Mbps, which is the base rate used for constructing higher‑order STS signals through multiplexing.

Q12. Evaluate the advantages of deploying SONET over traditional PDH hierarchies in a metropolitan area network.

🔹 A. SONET offers lower latency but higher cost compared to PDH
🔹 B. SONET provides less flexibility in bandwidth allocation than PDH
🔹 C. SONET enables synchronous multiplexing, higher data rates, and built‑in protection switching, which simplify network management compared to PDH’s hierarchy of independent channels
🔹 D. PDH supports better error detection than SONET

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

SONET’s synchronous multiplexing allows multiple lower‑rate streams to be combined without complex timing adjustments, providing higher aggregate data rates and standardized protection switching (e.g., APS). This reduces operational complexity and improves reliability versus PDH’s independent, non‑synchronous channels, which require manual coordination and lack built‑in resilience.

Q13. Design a mixed‑technology SONET network that uses STS‑1 for legacy links and STS‑12 for new high‑capacity links. Which principle ensures seamless interworking?

🔹 A. By converting all STS‑1 signals to Ethernet frames before transport
🔹 B. By using the same frame structure and allowing lower‑rate STS‑1 containers to be mapped into higher‑rate STS‑12 payloads via virtual containers
🔹 C. By assigning separate optical fibers for each signal type
🔹 D. By employing dynamic wavelength allocation only for STS‑12 links

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

SONET’s hierarchical design permits lower‑rate STS‑1 containers to be encapsulated within higher‑rate STS‑12 frames using virtual containers. This mapping preserves the original payload while enabling the network to carry both legacy and modern traffic over a common optical infrastructure without requiring protocol conversion.
