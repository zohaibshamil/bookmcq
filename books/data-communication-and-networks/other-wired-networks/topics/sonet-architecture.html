📝 All SONET architecture MCQs

Q1. The downstream data band begins at which frequency?

🔹 A. 500 MHz
🔹 B. 550 MHz
🔹 C. 600 MHz
🔹 D. 650 MHz

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The downstream portion of the cable‑access spectrum starts at 550 MHz. This upper segment carries data from the provider toward the subscriber, and its lower edge is defined by regulatory standards that separate it from the lower video band.

Q2. Given the video band spans from 54 MHz to 550 MHz and each channel occupies 6 MHz, what is the maximum number of whole channels that can be placed without overlap?

🔹 A. 80
🔹 B. 81
🔹 C. 79
🔹 D. 82

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Subtracting the lower limit from the upper limit yields 496 MHz of usable spectrum. Dividing 496 MHz by the 6 MHz per channel gives 82.66, meaning only 82 full channels fit without exceeding the band limits.

Q3. If a new service requires reserving 12 MHz of continuous spectrum within the downstream band (550‑750 MHz), how many 6‑MHz channels will be eliminated for this service?

🔹 A. 1
🔹 B. 3
🔹 C. 2
🔹 D. 4

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

A 12 MHz reservation corresponds to exactly two standard 6 MHz channels, because 12 ÷ 6 = 2. Therefore, two channels must be removed from the pool to accommodate the new service.

Q4. Assume the upstream band occupies 5 MHz to 55 MHz. If 6‑MHz channels are allocated starting from the lowest frequency, how many full upstream channels can be created, and what is the leftover frequency?

🔹 A. 9 channels, 1 MHz left
🔹 B. 8 channels, 7 MHz left
🔹 C. 8 channels, 2 MHz left
🔹 D. 9 channels, 7 MHz left

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The total upstream bandwidth is 55 − 5 = 50 MHz. Dividing by 6 MHz yields eight full channels (48 MHz) with a remainder of 2 MHz that cannot form another complete channel.

Q5. If the video band can support more than 80 channels, what is the minimum total bandwidth (in MHz) required to accommodate exactly 81 channels?

🔹 A. 492 MHz
🔹 B. 498 MHz
🔹 C. 504 MHz
🔹 D. 486 MHz

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Each channel needs 6 MHz, so 81 × 6 = 486 MHz. Starting at the lower edge of 54 MHz, the upper edge would be 54 + 486 = 540 MHz, which lies within the 54‑550 MHz video band.

Q6. Suppose the downstream band (550‑750 MHz) is fully utilized by 6‑MHz channels. If a policy mandates that at least 10 % of the downstream spectrum be reserved for future services, what is the maximum number of channels that can remain active?

🔹 A. 28
🔹 B. 30
🔹 C. 32
🔹 D. 34

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The downstream band spans 200 MHz. Ten percent reserved equals 20 MHz, leaving 180 MHz for active channels. Dividing 180 MHz by 6 MHz yields 30 full channels that can stay operational.

Q7. Compare the total number of possible channels in the video band (54‑550 MHz) with the downstream band (550‑750 MHz). Which band offers more channels?

🔹 A. Downstream band offers more
🔹 B. Both offer equal number
🔹 C. Video band offers more
🔹 D. Cannot be determined

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The video band provides about 496 MHz of spectrum, which supports roughly 82 channels (496 ÷ 6). The downstream band offers only 200 MHz, supporting about 33 channels. Therefore, the video band can accommodate a larger number of channels.

Q8. Evaluate the impact of increasing each channel width from 6 MHz to 8 MHz on the maximum number of channels in the video band. How many fewer channels would be possible?

🔹 A. About 12 fewer
🔹 B. About 20 fewer
🔹 C. About 30 fewer
🔹 D. No change

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

With 6 MHz channels, the video band holds roughly 82 channels (496 ÷ 6). Using 8 MHz channels reduces the count to 62 (496 ÷ 8). The difference is about 20 fewer channels, highlighting the trade‑off between bandwidth per channel and total channel count.

Q9. Differentiate the frequency allocation strategy for upstream versus downstream data in terms of interference susceptibility. Which direction is more prone to external noise?

🔹 A. Upstream is more prone
🔹 B. Downstream is more prone
🔹 C. Both equally prone
🔹 D. Neither experiences noise

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Lower‑frequency upstream signals travel through the subscriber’s premises and are more exposed to household electrical noise, making them generally more susceptible to interference compared with the higher‑frequency downstream signals that benefit from better shielding and less ambient noise.

Q10. Analyze how the division of the downstream band into 6‑MHz channels influences the design of modulation schemes for high‑speed data transmission. Which characteristic must the scheme prioritize?

🔹 A. Spectral efficiency
🔹 B. Low latency
🔹 C. High power consumption
🔹 D. Simple error correction

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Because the downstream bandwidth is limited to a fixed number of 6‑MHz slots, a modulation scheme must maximize the amount of data carried within each slot. Spectral efficiency therefore becomes the primary design goal to achieve high‑speed broadband services.

Q11. If the video band were to be repurposed for data services, what would be the primary advantage over using the existing downstream band?

🔹 A. Larger total bandwidth
🔹 B. Higher frequency reduces attenuation
🔹 C. Lower frequency improves device compatibility
🔹 D. Existing infrastructure already supports data

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The video band spans roughly 496 MHz, far exceeding the 200 MHz downstream band. Reallocating this larger spectrum to data traffic would dramatically increase the aggregate capacity available for broadband applications.

Q12. Contrast the channel count possible if the upstream band were allocated the same 6‑MHz channel size as the downstream band versus being allocated 3‑MHz channels. How does the count change?

🔹 A. It doubles
🔹 B. It triples
🔹 C. It remains same
🔹 D. It halves

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Halving the channel width from 6 MHz to 3 MHz doubles the number of channels that can fit into the same total bandwidth, because twice as many narrower slices can be placed within the fixed frequency range.

Q13. Apply the principle of frequency reuse to explain why multiple TV channels can operate simultaneously within the 54‑550 MHz range without interfering.

🔹 A. Each channel uses a distinct 6‑MHz slice
🔹 B. Channels are time‑divided
🔹 C. Signals are encoded with different codes
🔹 D. Channels are physically separated

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Frequency reuse relies on allocating each channel its own non‑overlapping 6‑MHz segment. By keeping the spectral footprints separate, adjacent channels do not interfere, allowing many channels to coexist within the same overall band.

Q14. Synthesize a scenario where the upstream band (5‑55 MHz) and the video band (54‑550 MHz) overlap. What technical challenge would arise?

🔹 A. Increased intermodulation distortion
🔹 B. Reduced channel capacity
🔹 C. Higher power consumption
🔹 D. Simplified antenna design

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

If the upstream and video bands overlapped, signals from the two services could mix, creating intermodulation products that fall within each other's pass‑bands. This distortion would degrade both TV and data quality, requiring complex filtering or redesign of the spectrum plan.

Q15. Given that the downstream band is 550‑750 MHz, propose how a DOCSIS system could allocate sub‑carriers to maximize throughput while respecting the 6‑MHz channel constraint. Which approach best meets the goal?

🔹 A. Use OFDM sub‑carriers spanning the entire 200 MHz then partition into 6‑MHz groups
🔹 B. Assign a single carrier per 6 MHz channel
🔹 C. Employ frequency hopping across channels
🔹 D. Allocate narrowband channels of 1 MHz each

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

DOCSIS 3.1 employs OFDM across the whole downstream spectrum, then groups the sub‑carriers into the mandated 6‑MHz channels. This method optimizes spectral efficiency while still complying with the regulatory channel size requirement.

Q16. Explain how the choice of 6‑MHz channel width in North American TV broadcasting influences the design of cable modems for downstream data.

🔹 A. Modems must align their symbol rate to 6 MHz spacing
🔹 B. Modems ignore channel width
🔹 C. Modems use 12 MHz channels to double speed
🔹 D. Modems only operate in upstream band

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Cable modems synchronize their symbol rates and FFT sizes to the 6‑MHz channel grid, ensuring that the transmitted data fits precisely within each allocated slice. This alignment simplifies channel planning and reduces the need for complex guard intervals.

Q17. If a new regulation reduces the downstream band upper limit from 750 MHz to 700 MHz, calculate the new maximum number of 6‑MHz channels and discuss the potential effect on broadband capacity.

🔹 A. 8 fewer channels, reducing capacity proportionally
🔹 B. 5 fewer channels, minimal impact
🔹 C. 10 fewer channels, significant reduction
🔹 D. No change in channel count

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The original 200 MHz span (550‑750 MHz) supported 33 full 6‑MHz channels. Limiting the upper edge to 700 MHz shrinks the band to 150 MHz, allowing only 25 channels—a loss of eight channels—directly decreasing the total downstream throughput available to subscribers.

Q18. Apply the concept of spectral guard bands to justify why the total usable bandwidth is slightly less than the arithmetic difference between band limits.

🔹 A. Guard bands are reserved to prevent adjacent channel interference
🔹 B. Guard bands increase total capacity
🔹 C. Guard bands are only used in upstream
🔹 D. Guard bands are optional and rarely used

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Regulatory standards insert small guard bands between adjacent channels to suppress spill‑over and prevent interference. Consequently, the sum of usable channel widths is a bit smaller than the raw difference between the lower and upper frequency limits, preserving signal integrity.
