📝 ATM design goals and cell structure

📖 From Data Communication and Networks • 14. Other Wired Networks • 10 questions available

What is ATM design goals and cell structure?

Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) was designed to integrate voice, video, and data on a single network using fixed-size 53-byte cells (5-byte header, 48-byte payload) to enable predictable latency, efficient hardware switching, statistical multiplexing, and quality-of-service guarantees across diverse traffic types.

Q1. If a network design emphasizes minimizing delay rather than increasing throughput, which design goal is being prioritized?

A. Reliability
B. Scalability
C. Performance ✅
D. Security

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Performance focuses on how quickly data is delivered, measured by latency. When designers accept lower bandwidth but strive for the smallest possible delay, they are targeting performance. Reliability concerns uptime, scalability concerns growth, and security concerns protection, none of which directly address delay. Therefore the correct answer is Performance.

Q2. Given that analog POTS cannot reliably transmit data beyond voice frequencies, what logical conclusion can be drawn about the need for digital conversion in modern networks?

A. Analog signals are sufficient for video streaming
B. Digital encoding is unnecessary for data integrity
C. Analog systems provide higher security
D. Digital conversion enables higher bandwidth and error correction ✅

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Because analog POTS is limited to the voice frequency band (approximately 300–3400 Hz) and cannot reliably carry higher‑frequency data, the logical outcome is that digital conversion is required to expand bandwidth and incorporate error‑correction coding. Digital encoding transforms analog signals into binary form, enabling modern services such as video, internet, and reliable data transfer.

Q3. If a design goal prioritizes fault tolerance, which of the following effects is most likely to occur in the network architecture?

A. Reduced redundancy
B. Increased single points of failure
C. Higher availability through backup paths ✅
D. Lower cost due to fewer components

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Fault tolerance aims to keep the network functional despite component failures. Implementing this goal typically introduces redundant paths, duplicate devices, or fail‑over mechanisms, which raise the overall availability of the service. Consequently, the architecture will exhibit higher availability through backup routes, whereas options suggesting reduced redundancy or lower cost contradict the principle of fault tolerance.

Q4. How does reliability differ from availability in the context of network design goals?

A. Reliability measures uptime, availability measures performance
B. Reliability refers to error rates, availability refers to service readiness
C. Reliability is about hardware durability, availability is about software licensing
D. Reliability focuses on consistent operation over time, while availability measures the proportion of time the service is usable ✅

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Reliability describes the consistency of a system’s operation over time, often expressed as mean time between failures. Availability, on the other hand, measures the proportion of time the service is accessible to users, factoring in both failures and maintenance periods. Thus, reliability focuses on uninterrupted performance, while availability quantifies usable uptime.

Q5. When evaluating scalability versus cost, which statement best reflects their trade‑off?

A. Higher scalability always reduces cost ✅
B. Increasing scalability typically raises upfront expenses but lowers long‑term operational cost
C. Cost is independent of scalability
D. Scalability only affects security, not cost

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Scalability allows a network to grow in capacity or users without redesign, but achieving this flexibility usually requires additional hardware, modular software, and more complex management, which increase initial capital expenditure. Over the long term, however, the ability to accommodate growth without major overhauls can lower operational costs, making the trade‑off favorable.

Q6. In distinguishing security from privacy as design goals, which pair correctly captures their primary focus?

A. Security protects data from unauthorized access; privacy ensures data is not collected without consent ✅
B. Security ensures data accuracy; privacy guarantees data speed
C. Security and privacy both only address physical layer issues
D. Security is about user interface design; privacy is about network topology

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Security is primarily concerned with protecting data and resources from unauthorized access, attacks, and breaches, often implemented through encryption, authentication, and firewalls. Privacy centers on ensuring that personal or sensitive information is collected, used, and shared only with the individual's consent, focusing on data handling policies rather than technical defenses. Hence, the pair correctly distinguishes their core objectives.

Q7. Applying modularity, how should a legacy analog subsystem be integrated into a new digital network to meet design goals?

A. Replace it entirely before integration
B. Encapsulate it behind a protocol adapter that isolates analog functions ✅
C. Directly connect analog wires to digital routers
D. Ignore modularity and treat it as a monolithic block

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Modularity recommends isolating legacy components behind well‑defined interfaces. By placing the analog subsystem behind a protocol adapter or gateway, its internal details remain encapsulated, allowing the surrounding digital network to interact through standardized messages. This approach preserves existing investment, simplifies integration, and supports future upgrades without disrupting the overall architecture.

Q8. Explain the relationship between fault tolerance and redundancy in achieving high availability.

A. Redundancy decreases fault tolerance
B. Fault tolerance is achieved solely by software patches, not redundancy
C. Redundancy provides alternate components, enabling fault tolerance and thus higher availability ✅
D. Redundancy only impacts security, not fault tolerance

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Redundancy introduces extra elements—such as duplicate links or devices—that can assume the workload when a primary component fails. This surplus capacity enables the system to tolerate faults, maintaining service continuity and thereby increasing overall availability. Consequently, redundancy is a fundamental mechanism for achieving fault‑tolerant designs.

Q9. Synthesize the primary design goals—reliability, scalability, performance, and security—to propose an architecture for a network transitioning from POTS to hybrid analog‑digital services.

A. Prioritize security above all, ignore scalability
B. Design a layered architecture with redundant core, scalable edge, performance‑optimized routing, and encrypted tunnels ✅
C. Use a single monolithic switch to handle all traffic
D. Focus solely on analog upgrades, discarding digital features

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A balanced hybrid architecture can be realized by layering the network: a resilient core with redundant routers ensures reliability; scalable edge switches allow incremental addition of analog or digital endpoints; performance‑oriented routing protocols prioritize low‑latency paths; and end‑to‑end encryption provides security across both legacy and modern segments. This synthesis meets the four primary design goals.

Q10. In which year was the plain old telephone system (POTS) first commercially deployed in the United States?

A. 1865
B. 1878 ✅
C. 1890
D. 1901

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The plain old telephone system was first introduced commercially by the Bell Telephone Company in 1878, providing the United States with a nationwide analog voice network. This deployment marked the beginning of widespread telephone service, establishing the foundation for later digital and data‑centric communication technologies.
