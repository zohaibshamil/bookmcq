What is Hybrid Fiber Coaxial (HFC) network structure?

Hybrid Fiber Coaxial (HFC) networks combine fiber optic cables for long-distance backbone transmission with coaxial cables for last-mile distribution to homes, enabling bidirectional communication, higher bandwidth, improved signal quality, and support for modern broadband internet and digital TV services.

Q1. If the length of the coaxial segment in an HFC network is increased, what is the most likely effect on the downstream signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) at the subscriber?

A.The SNR will increase
B.The SNR will decrease ✅
C.The SNR will remain unchanged
D.The SNR will fluctuate randomly
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Increasing coax length adds attenuation, reducing the received signal power while the noise floor stays roughly constant. Because SNR is the ratio of signal power to noise power, a lower signal level directly lowers the SNR, making the downstream link more vulnerable to errors.

Q2. Which of the following statements best differentiates an active optical node from a passive optical node in an HFC system?

A.A passive node splits optical power without amplification, while an active node amplifies the signal before splitting
B.A passive node requires external power, whereas an active node operates solely on harvested energy
C.A passive node converts optical signals to electrical signals, while an active node keeps the signal in optical form
D.A passive node can adjust gain dynamically, unlike an active node which has fixed gain ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: Active nodes contain electronic components such as amplifiers that boost the optical signal and often provide equalization, whereas passive nodes rely only on the inherent splitting of light and do not add gain. This functional difference determines where each type is deployed within the HFC architecture.

Q3. Considering typical bandwidths, how does the maximum downstream capacity of a fiber segment (e.g., 1 Gbps) compare to that of a coaxial segment (e.g., 100 Mbps) in an HFC system?

A.Fiber is 10 times higher ✅
B.Coaxial is higher
C.Both have the same capacity
D.Fiber is 2 times higher
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Fiber optics can carry signals at gigabit rates because they have extremely low attenuation and large bandwidth, while coaxial cable is limited by higher loss and narrower usable frequency spectrum, typically supporting up to a few hundred megabits per second. Hence the fiber segment provides roughly an order of magnitude greater downstream capacity.

Q4. In an HFC network, a passive optical splitter with a 1:4 split ratio is introduced. How does this affect the optical power reaching each downstream branch compared to a 1:2 splitter?

A.Power halves
B.Power quarters ✅
C.Power unchanged
D.Power doubles
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: A 1:4 splitter divides the incoming optical power into four equal parts, so each branch receives roughly one‑quarter of the original power. Compared with a 1:2 splitter, which delivers half the power to each output, the power per branch is reduced by an additional factor of two, i.e., to one‑quarter.

Q5. When multiple subscribers simultaneously use the upstream channel, which phenomenon primarily limits the total upstream data rate in an HFC network?

A.Coaxial attenuation
B.MAC layer contention ✅
C.Fiber dispersion
D.Amplifier saturation
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Upstream traffic in an HFC system shares a common channel, and the Media Access Control (MAC) protocol must arbitrate among many users. Contention and collision avoidance mechanisms therefore become the bottleneck, capping the aggregate upstream throughput regardless of the physical medium’s capacity.

Q6. If an HFC node is moved 500 m farther from the fiber head‑end, what is the expected change in the required amplifier gain to maintain the same downstream signal level?

A.Increase by ~5 dB
B.Increase by ~10 dB ✅
C.Decrease by ~5 dB
D.No change
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Coaxial cable typically exhibits about 0.02 dB of loss per meter at high frequencies. Extending the run by 500 m adds roughly 10 dB of extra attenuation, so the downstream amplifier must provide an additional gain of about 10 dB to compensate and keep the signal level constant.

Q7. Designers aim to minimize latency while supporting HD video. Which combination of architectural choices best achieves this goal?

A.Long coax runs, passive splitters, lower fiber capacity
B.Short coax runs, active optical nodes, and higher‑order modulation ✅
C.Standard coax length, passive splitters, lower modulation
D.Long coax, active nodes, lower modulation
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Shortening the coaxial segments reduces propagation delay, while active optical nodes can amplify and equalize signals, preserving quality. Using higher‑order modulation (e.g., 256‑QAM) maximizes data throughput without increasing physical distance, together delivering low latency and sufficient bandwidth for high‑definition video.

Q8. If a subscriber experiences frequent signal reflections (echoes) on the downstream channel, which network condition is most likely responsible?

A.Impedance mismatch at the tap ✅
B.Fiber chromatic dispersion
C.Amplifier overload
D.Weather interference
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Reflections arise when the transmission line’s impedance is not matched at a connector or tap, causing part of the signal to bounce back toward the source. This creates echoes and standing‑wave patterns, degrading the downstream signal quality perceived by the subscriber.

Q9. A 10 MHz coaxial channel in an HFC system uses 64‑QAM modulation (6 bits per symbol) with a symbol rate equal to the channel bandwidth. What is the theoretical maximum data rate?

A.60 Mbps ✅
B.120 Mbps
C.30 Mbps
D.6 Mbps
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The symbol rate equals the bandwidth (10 MHz). Each 64‑QAM symbol carries 6 bits, so the raw data rate is 10 MHz × 6 bits = 60 Mbps. This represents the ideal maximum before accounting for coding overhead or error‑correction.

Q10. Why does an HFC network typically allocate a separate frequency band for upstream traffic rather than using the same band as downstream?

A.To avoid interference between upstream and downstream signals ✅
B.Because upstream requires higher bandwidth
C.To simplify modulation
D.Because fiber cannot carry upstream frequencies
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Separating upstream and downstream into distinct frequency bands prevents the two signals from colliding on the same medium, which would cause inter‑modulation distortion and severe crosstalk. This duplexing scheme enables simultaneous bidirectional communication without mutual interference.

Q11. When a second fiber node is added to an existing HFC system, splitting the subscriber base evenly, how does this affect the average downstream SNR assuming all other factors remain constant?

A.SNR improves by ~3 dB ✅
B.SNR degrades by ~3 dB
C.No change
D.SNR improves by ~6 dB
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Dividing the subscriber load between two nodes reduces the number of taps on each coax segment, lowering cumulative splitter loss and return‑path attenuation. The resulting reduction in loss raises the received signal level relative to noise, typically yielding an SNR gain of about 3 dB.

Q12. What does the acronym 'HFC' stand for in broadband networking?

A.High‑Frequency Cable
B.Hybrid Fiber‑Coaxial ✅
C.Home Fiber Connection
D.Heavy Fiber Cable
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: The term HFC describes the hybrid architecture that combines optical fiber for long‑haul transmission with coaxial cable for the final “last‑mile” delivery to homes and businesses.
