Q1. Why is time‑sharing necessary when 1000 subscribers must use only six 6‑MHz upstream channels?

🔹 A. Because each subscriber receives a dedicated 6‑MHz channel.
🔹 B. Because the cable provider can increase the upstream bandwidth beyond 37 MHz.
🔹 C. Because only a limited number of channels exist, so subscribers must alternate access.
🔹 D. Because upstream and downstream use the same frequency band.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Time‑sharing is required because the physical medium provides only six 6‑MHz upstream slots. With far more subscribers than slots, each user cannot have a permanent channel; instead, they must wait for their turn, sharing the limited bandwidth in time. This ensures that all users can eventually transmit, albeit with possible delays.

Q2. If the total upstream spectrum is 37 MHz and each channel occupies 6 MHz, how many full upstream channels are available?

🔹 A. Six
🔹 B. Five
🔹 C. Seven
🔹 D. Four

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Dividing the total spectrum by the channel width gives \(37\text{ MHz} \div 6\text{ MHz} = 6.166\ldots\). Only whole channels can be allocated, so six complete 6‑MHz channels fit within the 37 MHz band, leaving a small unused portion.

Q3. Assume each upstream channel can carry 10 Mbps. If 1000 subscribers share six channels equally, what average time fraction must a subscriber obtain to sustain a 1 Mbps demand?

🔹 A. \(1/6\) of the channel time
🔹 B. \(1/10\) of the channel time
🔹 C. \(1/60\) of the channel time
🔹 D. \(1/100\) of the channel time

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Each channel offers 10 Mbps, so six channels provide \(6 \times 10 = 60\) Mbps total. To deliver 1 Mbps per subscriber, a user needs \(1/60\) of the aggregate capacity, which translates to \(1/60\) of the total channel time. Since one subscriber’s share is \(1/1000\) of all users, the required fraction is \(1/100\) of a single channel’s time, matching option D.

Q4. In downstream multicasting, a subscriber receives a packet addressed to another user. What does the cable modem do with that packet?

🔹 A. It forwards it to the subscriber’s computer.
🔹 B. It stores it for later processing.
🔹 C. It discards the packet after address mismatch.
🔹 D. It retransmits the packet upstream.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The downstream stream is broadcast to all modems on the channel. Each modem checks the embedded address; if it does not match the subscriber’s assigned address, the modem discards the packet. This filtering prevents irrelevant data from reaching the user’s equipment, conserving processing resources.

Q5. How does static channel allocation differ from dynamic allocation for upstream access?

🔹 A. Static allocation assigns a fixed channel per subscriber permanently; dynamic allocation changes assignments based on demand.
🔹 B. Static allocation changes channels each second; dynamic allocation never changes channels.
🔹 C. Static allocation uses only downstream channels; dynamic allocation uses only upstream channels.
🔹 D. Static allocation requires no coordination with the CMTS; dynamic allocation does.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Static allocation reserves a specific upstream channel for a group of users regardless of traffic, leading to potential under‑utilization. Dynamic allocation, by contrast, assigns channels on‑the‑fly according to current demand, improving efficiency and allowing more subscribers to share the limited spectrum.

Q6. What is a key advantage of having many narrow 6‑MHz downstream channels versus fewer wider channels?

🔹 A. More channels increase total downstream bandwidth.
🔹 B. Narrow channels simplify modulation and reduce interference.
🔹 C. Wider channels allow better error correction.
🔹 D. Narrow channels enable finer granularity for multicast group management.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Having numerous narrow channels lets the provider allocate each to different subscriber groups, facilitating multicast distribution where only relevant groups receive certain data. This granularity improves resource utilization and makes it easier to manage address filtering, even though the total raw bandwidth may be comparable.

Q7. If the same six upstream channels serve 10,000 subscribers instead of 100, what is the expected impact on latency for a real‑time voice packet?

🔹 A. Latency decreases because more users share the same bandwidth.
🔹 B. Latency remains unchanged because the channel bandwidth is fixed.
🔹 C. Latency increases due to longer waiting times before a channel becomes free.
🔹 D. Latency becomes unpredictable because packets are dropped more often.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

With more users contending for the same limited channels, each packet must wait longer for an available time slot, raising the average queuing delay. Real‑time voice, which is sensitive to latency, therefore experiences higher end‑to‑end delay, potentially degrading call quality.

Q8. Which statement best describes the role of the CMTS compared to a DSL modem?

🔹 A. Both terminate the subscriber’s connection and forward data to the Internet, but the CMTS also aggregates many cable modems onto a shared medium.
🔹 B. The CMTS only handles downstream video, while the DSL modem handles both upstream and downstream data.
🔹 C. The CMTS performs encryption, whereas the DSL modem does not.
🔹 D. The CMTS provides power to the cable modem, which the DSL modem cannot do.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The CMTS is the central device that receives upstream data from many cable modems, aggregates it, and sends downstream data to them. While functionally similar to a DSL modem in providing a bridge to the Internet, the CMTS must also manage channel allocation and multicast distribution across the shared coaxial network.

Q9. How does address filtering enable multicast delivery in the downstream direction?

🔹 A. By encrypting the data so only intended recipients can decode it.
🔹 B. By tagging each packet with a subscriber’s address, allowing each modem to keep only matching packets.
🔹 C. By assigning a unique frequency to each subscriber.
🔹 D. By converting multicast streams into unicast streams for each subscriber.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

In downstream multicast, every packet is broadcast on a channel, but each packet carries a destination address. Each cable modem examines this address; if it matches the subscriber’s registered address, the modem retains the packet, otherwise it discards it. This selective acceptance implements multicast without requiring separate physical channels for each user.

Q10. If a cable provider cannot acquire additional spectrum, which technique could increase upstream capacity?

🔹 A. Deploying higher‑order modulation schemes within the existing 6‑MHz channels.
🔹 B. Adding more physical cables to the network.
🔹 C. Reducing the number of downstream channels.
🔹 D. Increasing the length of each upstream time slot.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

By using more sophisticated modulation (e.g., moving from QPSK to 16‑QAM), each 6‑MHz channel can carry more bits per symbol, effectively raising the data rate without expanding the frequency band. This approach leverages signal processing advances while staying within the allocated spectrum.

Q11. Why does time‑sharing of upstream channels make it difficult to guarantee Quality of Service (QoS) for interactive applications?

🔹 A. Because time‑sharing introduces variable waiting times that can cause jitter and delay, which conflict with strict QoS requirements.
🔹 B. Because time‑sharing reduces the total bandwidth available to each subscriber.
🔹 C. Because time‑sharing eliminates the need for error correction.
🔹 D. Because time‑sharing only affects downstream traffic, not upstream traffic.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When many users compete for the same upstream channel, each must wait for its turn, leading to unpredictable transmission delays (jitter). Interactive applications such as VoIP or online gaming require consistent latency; the variability introduced by shared time slots hampers the ability to meet those QoS guarantees.

Q12. What is the typical bandwidth of a single upstream channel in a first‑generation (1G) cable system?

🔹 A. \(4\) Mbps
🔹 B. \(6\) Mbps
🔹 C. \(10\) Mbps
🔹 D. \(12\) Mbps

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

First‑generation cable systems allocate 6‑MHz slots for upstream transmission, and each slot is generally capable of supporting data rates up to about 12 Mbps, depending on modulation and coding schemes. This figure reflects the practical throughput achievable on a single upstream channel.
