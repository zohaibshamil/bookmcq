📖 Media Access Control (MAC)

Media Access Control Coordinates Shared Channels

Media Access Control determines when multiple devices can transmit over a shared communication medium without uncontrolled interference. Random access protocols allow stations to compete for transmission opportunities, while controlled access methods coordinate participation through reservation, polling, and token passing. ALOHA and CSMA introduce different approaches to contention, with CSMA/CD adding collision detection. FDMA, TDMA, and CDMA separate communication resources through frequency, time, and code assignments. These mechanisms build on data-link framing and physical transmission to organize access to shared network resources.


Signal Sharing And Frame Transmission Prerequisites

Students should understand data-link frames, physical addresses, transmission media, bandwidth, and shared communication channels. Basic knowledge of transmission delay, collision, and signal propagation helps explain contention-based protocols. Familiarity with frequency, time intervals, and digital signal representation also supports the study of FDMA, TDMA, and CDMA.


Abilities For Comparing MAC Access Methods

Students should distinguish random access, controlled access, and channelization techniques. They should calculate Pure ALOHA and Slotted ALOHA efficiency, explain CSMA variants and the CSMA/CD collision-handling process, and compare reservation, polling, and token passing. They should also describe how FDMA, TDMA, and CDMA allocate separate communication resources and identify the operating principle behind each method.


Contention Controlled Access And Channelization Methods

  • Random Access Protocols - Introduces contention-based transmission in which stations independently compete for shared channel access.
  • Pure And Slotted ALOHA - Compares transmission opportunities, collision probabilities, and maximum theoretical channel efficiency.
  • CSMA And Collision Detection - Explains carrier sensing, CSMA variations, and collision detection with retransmission procedures.
  • Controlled Access Methods - Describes reservation, polling, and token passing as coordinated channel-sharing mechanisms.
  • Frequency And Time Division Access - Explains FDMA frequency allocation and TDMA time-slot distribution among communication stations.
  • Code Division Multiple Access - Introduces CDMA and the use of distinct spreading codes for simultaneous communication.

A Progression From Contention To Channel Allocation

Begin with random access to understand why simultaneous transmissions can produce collisions. Study Pure and Slotted ALOHA before moving to CSMA and its carrier-sensing improvements. Continue with CSMA/CD to understand collision detection and recovery. Next, examine controlled access through reservation, polling, and token passing. Finish with FDMA, TDMA, and CDMA to compare contention-based access with frequency-, time-, and code-based resource allocation.


How Access Rules Divide Shared Medium Use

ALOHA establishes the basic contention problem, while Slotted ALOHA reduces collision opportunities through synchronized transmission intervals. CSMA adds carrier sensing, and CSMA/CD detects collisions during transmission. Reservation, polling, and token passing replace independent competition with coordinated access. Channelization takes another approach by dividing available resources into frequency bands, time slots, or spreading codes, allowing multiple stations to communicate through separated transmission resources.


A Collision And Recovery On Shared Ethernet

Three stations share a 10 Mbps half-duplex Ethernet segment. Station A has a 1,500-byte frame, requiring 1.2 milliseconds of raw transmission time. Stations A and B sense an idle medium and begin transmitting nearly simultaneously. Their signals collide, so CSMA/CD detects the collision and both stations stop transmission. Each waits for a randomized backoff interval before retrying. After one station succeeds, the other waits for the medium to become available before transmitting its frame.


Why Networks Need Coordinated Medium Access

Without access-control mechanisms, simultaneous transmissions could repeatedly collide, wasting available channel capacity. Uncoordinated devices would also struggle to share limited transmission resources efficiently. Reservation, polling, token passing, and channelization provide alternative access arrangements. These mechanisms support Ethernet, wireless networks, and communication systems that require organized access among multiple stations.


Separating Collision Detection From Scheduled Access

Students may say 'CSMA prevents every collision'; carrier sensing reduces collision chances but cannot eliminate simultaneous transmissions caused by propagation delay. They may say 'CSMA/CD and polling follow the same access process'; CSMA/CD detects contention, whereas polling grants permission through a controller. They may also say 'FDMA and CDMA divide time slots'; FDMA separates frequencies, while CDMA separates transmissions using spreading codes.


ALOHA Efficiency And MAC Method Comparisons

Exam questions frequently calculate Pure ALOHA and Slotted ALOHA efficiency using their theoretical maximum values of approximately 18.4% and 36.8%, respectively. Other questions trace CSMA/CD collision handling or compare CSMA variations. Students should also distinguish reservation, polling, and token passing, identify FDMA, TDMA, and CDMA resource assignments, and select the appropriate access method from a communication scenario.


How Competing Stations Share Transmission Capacity

Shared-medium communication requires rules that determine which station transmits and how available resources are distributed. Random access permits contention, controlled access coordinates transmission opportunities, and channelization separates communication resources. Their operating differences influence collision frequency, channel efficiency, waiting time, and network organization. Together, these methods provide alternative solutions for coordinating multiple devices across shared communication channels.


Standards Behind Ethernet And LAN Access Methods

  • IEEE 802.3 Ethernet Standard - Defines Ethernet MAC operations and the historical CSMA/CD shared-medium mechanism.
  • IEEE 802.5 Token Ring Standard - Provides a historical reference for token-based controlled access in local area networks.
  • IEEE 802.11 Wireless LAN Standard - Specifies wireless LAN MAC operations and coordinated access to shared radio channels.
  • Data Communications and Networking MAC Sections - Provides further study of random access, controlled access, and channelization techniques.

Essential Terms For Describing Medium Access

  • Random Access - Allows stations to compete independently for opportunities to transmit over a shared medium.
  • Pure ALOHA - Permits transmission whenever data becomes available, without synchronized time slots.
  • Slotted ALOHA - Restricts transmission starts to synchronized time-slot boundaries.
  • CSMA/CD - Uses carrier sensing and collision detection to manage shared-medium transmission.
  • Polling - Gives stations transmission opportunities through requests controlled by a central coordinator.
  • Token Passing - Controls transmission by allowing the station holding a circulating token to transmit.
  • FDMA - Allocates separate frequency bands to different communication channels.
  • CDMA - Separates simultaneous transmissions through distinct spreading codes.
