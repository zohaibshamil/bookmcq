What is Multiplexing and Demultiplexing in the TCP/IP Protocol Suite?

Multiplexing and demultiplexing in the TCP/IP protocol suite enable multiple applications to share a single network connection simultaneously by combining various data streams at the sender using port numbers and separating them at the receiver to ensure each packet reaches the correct destination process.

Q1. A network uses a single TDM multiplexer followed by a demultiplexer to send two independent video streams. If the multiplexer correctly interleaves bits, what is the most likely effect on the received video quality?

A. Both videos will retain their original quality ✅
B. Both videos will suffer identical degradation
C. Only the video with higher bitrate will degrade
D. The videos will be combined into one stream

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Because TDM simply interleaves bits from each source without altering them, the logical content of each stream remains unchanged. Therefore, assuming the multiplexer and demultiplexer operate correctly, each video is reconstructed exactly as transmitted, preserving its original quality.

Q2. In a TDM system carrying three traffic streams, Stream B experiences a temporary pause causing its time slot to be idle. What can be inferred about the overall latency of Streams A and C during this pause?

A. Latency of A and C increases due to the idle slot
B. Latency of A and C remains unchanged ✅
C. Latency of A and C decreases because the idle slot frees bandwidth
D. Latency of A and C becomes unpredictable

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When a time slot assigned to a specific stream is idle, the scheduler still reserves that slot for the same stream, so the timing for other streams does not shift. Consequently, the latency of the remaining active streams stays the same despite the idle period.

Q3. A demultiplexer receives a combined signal but forwards the sub‑channel intended for Host X to Host Y. Which underlying cause most directly explains this misrouting?

A. Incorrect clock synchronization
B. Faulty address decoding logic ✅
C. Insufficient signal amplitude
D. Excessive noise on the transmission medium

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Demultiplexers rely on address or tag decoding to associate each sub‑channel with its destination. A fault in this decoding logic will cause the demultiplexer to map the incoming sub‑channel to the wrong output, leading to the observed misrouting.

Q4. Which statement best compares circuit‑switched and packet‑switched networks regarding their multiplexing techniques?

A. Circuit‑switched uses TDM, while packet‑switched uses statistical multiplexing ✅
B. Both use FDM exclusively
C. Circuit‑switched employs statistical multiplexing, packet‑switched uses TDM
D. Neither employs multiplexing; they send raw bits

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Circuit‑switched networks allocate a dedicated time slot (TDM) for the duration of a call, whereas packet‑switched networks share the link dynamically, employing statistical multiplexing to combine packets based on demand. This contrast highlights the fundamental difference in how each paradigm handles multiplexing.

Q5. When comparing Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM) to Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) on a 100 Mbps LAN, which outcome most accurately reflects bandwidth efficiency for bursty traffic?

A. FDM always yields higher efficiency because it avoids time slot gaps
B. TDM provides better efficiency by allocating slots only when data is present ✅
C. Both achieve identical efficiency for bursty traffic
D. FDM is less efficient because each channel reserves a fixed portion of the spectrum

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

TDM can dynamically assign time slots to active sources, reducing wasted capacity during idle periods. In bursty traffic, this dynamic allocation often results in higher effective bandwidth utilization compared to FDM, where each frequency band is permanently reserved regardless of usage.

Q6. Consider four input streams with average rates of \(2\), \(3\), \(5\), and \(8\) Mbps sharing a \(20\) Mbps link via fixed‑slot TDM. Which analysis correctly explains why statistical multiplexing might outperform this TDM arrangement?

A. Statistical multiplexing guarantees each stream its peak rate, preventing congestion
B. Statistical multiplexing allows unused slots from idle streams to be reassigned, reducing wasted bandwidth ✅
C. TDM automatically adapts slot lengths to traffic, making it more efficient than statistical multiplexing
D. Statistical multiplexing introduces additional latency, making it less suitable for real‑time streams

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Fixed‑slot TDM reserves a constant portion of the link for each stream regardless of actual usage, so when some streams are idle, their slots remain unused. Statistical multiplexing dynamically reallocates these unused portions to active streams, improving overall link utilization and preventing congestion under bursty conditions.

Q7. How does a demultiplexer determine which sub‑channel to forward to a particular output port?

A. By measuring the signal amplitude of each sub‑channel
B. By decoding a header or tag embedded in the multiplexed signal ✅
C. By synchronizing to a global clock signal
D. By using a random selection algorithm

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Demultiplexers rely on embedded identifiers—such as tags, headers, or address fields—within the multiplexed signal. By decoding this information, the demultiplexer can associate each sub‑channel with its designated output port, ensuring correct delivery.

Q8. Explain the relationship between Layer 2 frames and multiplexing performed at the physical layer in a typical Ethernet link.

A. Layer 2 frames are multiplexed only after being converted to optical signals
B. Multiplexing at the physical layer combines multiple frames into a single electrical signal for transmission ✅
C. Physical‑layer multiplexing replaces the need for Layer 2 framing entirely
D. Layer 2 frames are unaffected by any physical‑layer multiplexing

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

At the physical layer, multiple binary streams representing different Layer 2 frames are interleaved (e.g., via TDM) into a single electrical or optical waveform. This process does not alter the frame structure but merely combines their bit streams for efficient transmission over the shared medium.

Q9. In a system where data is first encrypted, then multiplexed, and finally transmitted, which statement best synthesizes the effect of this order on security and multiplexing efficiency?

A. Encryption after multiplexing would be more secure because it hides the multiplexing pattern
B. Multiplexing before encryption reduces the overhead of encrypting multiple streams separately
C. Encrypting before multiplexing ensures each stream remains confidential even if the multiplexed signal is intercepted ✅
D. The order does not matter; security and efficiency are unchanged

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Encrypting each individual stream before they are combined preserves confidentiality for each constituent, so an adversary who captures the multiplexed signal cannot separate or read any individual payload without the appropriate keys. This ordering also avoids the need to re‑encrypt after demultiplexing, maintaining both security and processing efficiency.

Q10. What is the definition of multiplexing in the context of data communications?

A. The process of converting digital bits to analog signals
B. The method of routing packets through multiple paths
C. Combining multiple independent data streams into a single transmission medium ✅
D. Separating a combined signal into its original components

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Multiplexing refers to the technique of taking several distinct data streams and merging them so they share a common transmission medium, allowing efficient utilization of the available bandwidth.

Q11. Given four input streams with rates \(2\), \(3\), \(5\), and \(8\) Mbps sharing a \(20\) Mbps link via fixed‑slot TDM, can the link carry all streams without congestion? Identify the primary cause if it cannot.

A. Yes, because the sum \(2+3+5+8 = 18\) Mbps is less than 20 Mbps
B. No, because TDM requires each stream to be allocated an equal share, leading to a total of \(4 \times 5 = 20\) Mbps, which exceeds the capacity ✅
C. Yes, because TDM dynamically adjusts slots based on instantaneous demand
D. No, because the link capacity must be at least twice the highest stream rate

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Fixed‑slot TDM divides the link equally among the four streams, giving each a \(20/4 = 5\) Mbps slot. The stream requiring 8 Mbps cannot be accommodated within its 5 Mbps allocation, causing congestion. Hence, the equal‑share nature of TDM, not the aggregate sum, is the limiting factor.

Q12. How does the design of a demultiplexer differ when handling synchronous versus asynchronous streams?

A. Synchronous demultiplexers ignore timing information, while asynchronous ones rely on it
B. Synchronous demultiplexers use fixed time slots; asynchronous demultiplexers use start‑stop markers ✅
C. Both use identical hardware; the difference is only in software configuration
D. Asynchronous demultiplexers require higher signal amplitude

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Synchronous streams are organized into predetermined time slots, so the demultiplexer can route data based on slot position. Asynchronous streams lack fixed timing, so the demultiplexer must detect start‑stop markers or other framing cues to identify each sub‑channel, leading to different hardware or logic requirements.

Q13. In a router that multiplexes outgoing packets onto a shared link, how does this multiplexing interact with Quality‑of‑Service (QoS) scheduling mechanisms?

A. Multiplexing bypasses QoS, sending all packets in a round‑robin fashion
B. QoS scheduling determines the order and priority of packets before they are multiplexed, ensuring higher‑priority traffic gets earlier slots ✅
C. QoS only applies after demultiplexing at the destination, not during multiplexing
D. Multiplexing guarantees equal bandwidth for all QoS classes regardless of priority

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

QoS mechanisms prioritize packets by assigning them to different queues. Before the router combines packets onto the shared link, the scheduler selects packets from these queues based on their priority, so higher‑priority packets are placed into earlier or more frequent multiplexed slots, preserving service guarantees.
