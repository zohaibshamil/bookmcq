📝 History and evolution of Ethernet

📖 From Data Communication and Networks • 13. Wired LANs: Ethernet • 9 questions available

What is History and evolution of Ethernet?

Ethernet originated in the 1970s at Xerox PARC as a 2.94 Mbps experimental network and has continuously evolved through IEEE standardization into faster versions like Fast Ethernet, Gigabit Ethernet, and beyond, adapting from coaxial cable bus topologies to twisted-pair star topologies while maintaining backward compatibility.

Q1. What is the primary purpose of the CRC field added to each frame in the Stop‑and‑Wait protocol?

A. To indicate the priority of the frame
B. To detect and signal corrupted frames
C. To identify the sender’s MAC address ✅
D. To schedule transmission timing

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The CRC (Cyclic Redundancy Check) is a checksum calculated from the frame’s contents. When the receiver recomputes the CRC and finds a mismatch, it knows the frame was corrupted during transmission and discards it, providing an implicit signal to the sender that retransmission is needed.

Q2. If a sender’s timer expires before an acknowledgment is received, which of the following is the most plausible inference?

A. The frame arrived correctly but the receiver is busy
B. The frame was either lost or corrupted during transmission ✅
C. The receiver deliberately ignored the frame
D. The network layer rejected the packet

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

When the timer expires, the sender has not received an ACK within the expected interval, which, according to the Stop‑and‑Wait design, indicates that the transmitted frame did not reach the receiver intact or was lost. Hence the sender must retransmit, assuming loss or corruption.

Q3. Which statement best contrasts the simple protocol with the Stop‑and‑Wait protocol regarding flow control?

A. Both protocols use acknowledgments to regulate sending rate
B. The simple protocol relies on implicit flow control, while Stop‑and‑Wait uses explicit timers
C. The simple protocol has no flow control, whereas Stop‑and‑Wait pauses after each frame until an ACK is received ✅
D. Stop‑and‑Wait allows multiple frames to be in transit simultaneously, unlike the simple protocol

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The simple protocol sends frames continuously without checking whether the receiver can keep up, implying no flow control. In contrast, Stop‑and‑Wait enforces flow control by sending one frame and waiting for an acknowledgment before proceeding, thus preventing the sender from overwhelming the receiver.

Q4. Assume an Ethernet link with propagation delay \(d_p\) and frame transmission time \(t_f\). How does increasing \(d_p\) affect the maximum achievable throughput of a Stop‑and‑Wait system?

A. Throughput increases linearly with \(d_p\)
B. Throughput remains unchanged because \(t_f\) dominates
C. Throughput decreases because the idle waiting period grows with \(d_p\) ✅
D. Throughput oscillates unpredictably with \(d_p\)

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

In Stop‑and‑Wait, after transmitting a frame (time \(t_f\)), the sender must wait for the round‑trip time, which includes the propagation delay \(d_p\). As \(d_p\) grows, the waiting interval becomes longer relative to \(t_f\), reducing the fraction of time spent transmitting and thus lowering overall throughput.

Q5. When the receiver silently discards a corrupted frame, what indirect signal does the sender interpret?

A. A negative acknowledgment (NAK) is sent
B. A timeout will eventually occur ✅
C. The sender receives a duplicate frame
D. The sender’s CRC is recalculated

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The protocol specifies that corrupted frames are dropped without sending any explicit response. Consequently, the sender, lacking an acknowledgment, will wait until its timer expires. The timeout acts as the indirect indication that the frame was either lost or corrupted, prompting a retransmission.

Q6. How does increasing the sliding‑window size from 1 (Stop‑and‑Wait) to \(W>1\) influence the efficiency of Ethernet transmission over a high‑delay link?

A. Efficiency remains the same because each frame still requires an ACK
B. Efficiency improves proportionally to \(W\), reducing idle time between frames ✅
C. Efficiency declines because more frames increase collision probability
D. Efficiency is unaffected since Ethernet uses CSMA/CD regardless of window size

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A larger window allows the sender to transmit up to \(W\) frames before waiting for acknowledgments, effectively overlapping propagation delays with transmission time. This overlap reduces the idle waiting periods that dominate Stop‑and‑Wait, especially on links with high \(d_p\), thereby raising channel utilization and overall efficiency.

Q7. If the simple protocol were extended with error detection, which modification to its FSM would be required?

A. Add a new “Error‑Recovery” state after frame receipt ✅
B. Replace the single ready state with a “Waiting‑ACK” state
C. Introduce a timer event that triggers retransmission
D. No change; error detection does not affect the FSM

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Adding error detection means the receiver must react to corrupted frames, potentially requesting retransmission. To model this behavior, the FSM needs an additional state representing the error‑recovery process, allowing the system to handle detection, notification, and possible retransmission before returning to the ready state.

Q8. Given that the simple protocol does not use sequence numbers, what logical problem arises if frames are lost and later retransmitted?

A. The receiver cannot differentiate between new and retransmitted frames ✅
B. The sender cannot schedule frame ordering
C. Both sender and receiver will experience deadlock
D. The protocol will automatically correct the loss

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Without sequence numbers, the receiver has no way to know whether an incoming frame is a fresh transmission or a duplicate caused by a previous loss. This ambiguity can lead to duplicate delivery of the same packet or missed data, compromising reliability.

Q9. Why did Ethernet evolve from 10 Mbps half‑duplex with Stop‑and‑Wait to full‑duplex and higher speeds?

A. To simplify the protocol by removing error detection
B. To increase throughput by eliminating collisions and allowing simultaneous send/receive ✅
C. Because half‑duplex hardware was too expensive
D. To conform to IEEE 802.3 standards only

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Early Ethernet used half‑duplex and the Stop‑and‑Wait approach, which limited throughput due to collisions and waiting for acknowledgments. Transitioning to full‑duplex removed the need for collision detection, permitting simultaneous transmission and reception, and higher link speeds dramatically raised data rates, addressing the growing demand for bandwidth.
