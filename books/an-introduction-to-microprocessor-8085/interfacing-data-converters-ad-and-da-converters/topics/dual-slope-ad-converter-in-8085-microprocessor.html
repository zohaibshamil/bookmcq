Q1. What is the primary advantage of a dual‑slope analog‑to‑digital converter compared with other ADC types?

🔹 A. High conversion speed
🔹 B. Low manufacturing cost
🔹 C. Excellent noise rejection
🔹 D. Minimal power consumption

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The dual‑slope design averages the input over a long integration period, which cancels out most periodic noise and improves accuracy. Answer C is correct because noise rejection is the standout benefit. Answers A and D describe traits of other converters, not the dual‑slope. Answer B is unrelated to performance; cost is not the defining advantage. This noise‑rejection property makes the converter valuable in precision instrumentation.

Q2. In a dual‑slope ADC, what determines the length of the integration period?

🔹 A. Reference voltage magnitude
🔹 B. Clock frequency
🔹 C. Amplitude of the input signal
🔹 D. Number of resolution bits

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The integration period is set by counting a fixed number of clock cycles; thus the clock frequency directly controls how long the integrator runs. Answer B is correct. The reference voltage (A) sets the de‑integration slope, not the integration time. The input amplitude (C) influences the final count but not the period length. The resolution bits (D) affect total conversion time, not the fixed integration interval.

Q3. Why does the dual‑slope ADC provide good rejection of line‑frequency (e.g., 60 Hz) noise?

🔹 A. Its integration time can be chosen as an integer multiple of the noise period
🔹 B. It uses a high‑speed comparator that filters out low‑frequency components
🔹 C. The reference voltage is modulated to cancel the noise
🔹 D. The converter operates only during the positive half‑cycle of the mains waveform

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

By integrating the input for a duration equal to an integer multiple of the mains period, positive and negative noise contributions cancel out, giving strong line‑frequency rejection. Answer A captures this principle. Options B, C, and D describe techniques not employed in dual‑slope converters and therefore are incorrect.

Q4. How does the resolution of a dual‑slope ADC depend on the chosen integration time?

🔹 A. Resolution improves linearly with integration time
🔹 B. Resolution is inversely proportional to integration time
🔹 C. Resolution is independent of integration time
🔹 D. Resolution improves with the square root of integration time

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The smallest detectable voltage change corresponds to one clock count during the de‑integration phase; longer integration yields more clock counts for the same voltage range, thus higher resolution. Answer B correctly states the inverse relationship. Answer A suggests a direct linear improvement, which overstates the effect. Answer C ignores the timing relationship, and D introduces an incorrect square‑root dependence.

Q5. What is the effect of increasing the reference voltage on the conversion time of a dual‑slope ADC, assuming a fixed integration period?

🔹 A. Conversion time decreases because the de‑integration slope becomes steeper
🔹 B. Conversion time increases because the integrator must charge to a higher level
🔹 C. Conversion time remains unchanged; only the count range changes
🔹 D. Conversion time doubles regardless of integration period

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

With a higher reference, the de‑integration slope is steeper, so the discharge (de‑integration) completes faster, reducing the variable portion of conversion time. However, the fixed integration period stays the same, so overall conversion time shortens, not doubles. Therefore answer D is incorrect; the correct effect is described by answer A. Answers B and C misinterpret the relationship between reference voltage and timing.

Q6. A dual‑slope ADC integrates for 10 000 clock cycles and then de‑integrates up to a 12‑bit maximum count. If the clock runs at 1 MHz, what is the approximate total conversion time?

🔹 A. 10 µs
🔹 B. 14 µs
🔹 C. 20 µs
🔹 D. 25 µs

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The fixed integration takes 10 000 cycles / 1 MHz = 10 µs. The worst‑case de‑integration adds up to 2^12‑1 = 4095 cycles, or about 4.1 µs. Summing gives roughly 14 µs, matching answer B. Answer A ignores the de‑integration portion, answer C overestimates, and answer D is far too large. This calculation shows how the dual‑slope’s total time combines a constant integration phase with a variable de‑integration phase.

Q7. If the input voltage is exactly one‑half of the reference voltage, what fraction of the integration period will the de‑integration phase occupy?

🔹 A. One‑quarter of the integration period
🔹 B. One‑third of the integration period
🔹 C. One‑half of the integration period
🔹 D. Two‑thirds of the integration period

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The de‑integration time is proportional to the ratio \(V_{in}/V_{ref}\). With \(V_{in}=0.5\,V_{ref}\), the discharge lasts half as long as the integration interval, i.e., 50 % of the period. Answer C reflects this direct proportionality. The other fractions do not correspond to the 0.5 ratio and therefore are incorrect.

Q8. Design a dual‑slope ADC to achieve 0.1 % accuracy using a 50 kHz clock. What minimum integration time should be selected?

🔹 A. 10 µs
🔹 B. 20 µs
🔹 C. 50 µs
🔹 D. 100 µs

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

0.1 % accuracy requires at least a 1 / 1000 resolution, meaning at least 1000 distinct counts during integration. With a 50 kHz clock, 1000 cycles correspond to \(1000/50\,000 = 0.02\) s, i.e., 20 µs. Answer B meets the count requirement. The other options provide either too few (10 µs) or unnecessarily long (50 µs, 100 µs) integration periods.

Q9. A measurement system is affected by 60 Hz mains interference. Which integration time choice best suppresses this noise in a dual‑slope ADC?

🔹 A. Any arbitrary time, because the converter inherently filters noise
🔹 B. A time equal to half the mains period (8.33 ms)
🔹 C. A time equal to one‑quarter the mains period (4.17 ms)
🔹 D. A time equal to an integer multiple of the mains period (20 ms)

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

Choosing an integration interval that is an exact multiple of the 60 Hz period (≈16.67 ms) ensures that positive and negative cycles of the interference cancel out. The nearest convenient multiple is 20 ms, which provides strong rejection. Answers A, B, and C do not guarantee complete cancellation and thus are less effective.

Q10. A temperature sensor outputs 0–5 V and the required resolution is 0.01 V. Using a dual‑slope ADC, what minimum number of clock cycles is needed for the integration period?

🔹 A. 256
🔹 B. 512
🔹 C. 1024
🔹 D. 2048

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A 0.01 V step over a 5 V range yields 500 distinct levels, requiring at least \(\lceil \log_2 500 \rceil = 9\) bits of resolution. A 9‑bit count corresponds to \(2^9 = 512\) possible values, so the integration period must accommodate at least 512 clock cycles. Answer B satisfies this requirement; the other options either under‑sample (256) or provide unnecessary excess resolution.

Q11. Why is a dual‑slope ADC generally slower than a successive‑approximation ADC?

🔹 A. It uses a slower clock source
🔹 B. It must perform a long integration followed by a variable de‑integration
🔹 C. It requires multiple analog comparators
🔹 D. It needs to store intermediate digital results in RAM

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The dual‑slope technique first integrates the input for a fixed duration, then de‑integrates using the reference, which adds a variable time component dependent on the input level. This two‑step process lengthens conversion time compared with the single‑step binary search of a successive‑approximation ADC. Answer B captures this mechanism. The other choices describe unrelated hardware constraints.

Q12. How does the dual‑slope architecture achieve automatic gain control (AGC) of the input signal?

🔹 A. By adjusting the reference voltage during conversion
🔹 B. By varying the integration time based on input amplitude
🔹 C. By using a feedback loop that modifies the integrator’s slope
🔹 D. By scaling the final digital count after conversion

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation:

In a dual‑slope converter, the relationship between input voltage and count is linear; any gain variation appears as a proportional change in the digital result, which can be compensated by scaling the count in software or downstream circuitry. Thus answer D is correct. Changing the reference (A) or integration time (B) would alter the conversion principle, and a feedback loop (C) is not part of the basic dual‑slope design.

Q13. A dual‑slope ADC uses a 2.5 V reference and an integration time of 200 µs. If the input voltage is 1.25 V and the clock is 1 MHz, what count value will be produced?

🔹 A. 50
🔹 B. 100
🔹 C. 150
🔹 D. 200

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

With a 1 MHz clock, 200 µs corresponds to 200 clock cycles. The count equals \((V_{in}/V_{ref}) \times\) integration cycles = \((1.25/2.5) \times 200 = 0.5 \times 200 = 100\). Therefore answer B is correct. The other options result from incorrect scaling or misreading the ratio.

Q14. What is the typical number of phases in a dual‑slope conversion cycle?

🔹 A. One
🔹 B. Two
🔹 C. Three
🔹 D. Four

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

A dual‑slope conversion consists of two distinct phases: a fixed‑duration integration of the input signal, followed by a variable‑duration de‑integration using the reference voltage. Hence answer B is correct. The other options suggest additional phases that are not part of the standard dual‑slope operation.
