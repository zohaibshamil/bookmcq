What is Two key Lockout of 8279?

Two Key Lockout is a safety mode in the 8279 where if two or more keys are pressed simultaneously, only the first detected key is accepted and entered into the FIFO buffer while subsequent simultaneous presses are ignored until the first key is released, preventing erroneous multiple character entries caused by accidental double-key presses.

Q1. In an 8253 timer circuit, which pin provides the ground connection?

A. Pin 12 ✅
B. Pin 13
C. Pin 21
D. Pin 24

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Pin 12 is designated as the ground terminal for the 8253 device. Grounding this pin completes the circuit and provides a reference voltage for all internal logic. Pin 13 is an output for counter 1, Pin 21 is the chip‑select input, and Pin 24 supplies +5 V power. Using the correct ground pin prevents erratic behavior and ensures reliable timing, which is essential in any real‑world system that relies on accurate counting.

Q2. A microcontroller wants to read the current value of counter 1. Which combination of control signals must be asserted?

A. CS=0, RD=0, WR=1, A1=0, A0=1 ✅
B. CS=0, RD=1, WR=0, A1=0, A0=1
C. CS=0, RD=0, WR=1, A1=1, A0=0
D. CS=1, RD=0, WR=1, A1=0, A0=1

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Reading counter 1 requires the chip‑select (CS) low to enable the device, the read line (RD) low to indicate a read operation, and the write line (WR) high so that no write occurs. The address lines A1=0 and A0=1 select counter 1 according to the table of actions. Options B and C select the wrong counter or use the wrong write state, while D disables the chip entirely. Applying the correct signals lets the CPU retrieve the count reliably.

Q3. To write a mode word to counter 0, which control‑signal settings are required?

A. CS=0, WR=0, RD=1, A1=0, A0=0
B. CS=0, WR=1, RD=0, A1=0, A0=0 ✅
C. CS=1, WR=0, RD=1, A1=0, A0=0
D. CS=0, WR=0, RD=0, A1=0, A0=0

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

Writing a mode word uses the active‑low chip‑select (CS=0) and write (WR=0) signals, while the read line must stay high (RD=1) to avoid a read operation. The address bits A1=0 and A0=0 identify counter 0, matching the table that defines a mode‑word write. Option B reverses WR and RD, causing a read instead of a write. Option C disables the device (CS=1). Option D asserts both WR and RD low, which is an illegal “no‑operation” state. The correct combination programs the counter’s mode correctly.

Q4. Why is the CS (chip‑select) pin defined as an active‑low signal in the 8253?

A. Active‑low signals are easier for TTL logic.
B. Active‑low signals reduce power consumption.
C. Active‑low signals improve noise immunity. ✅
D. Active‑low signals simplify bus wiring.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

The CS pin being active‑low means the device responds when the line is driven low. This convention improves noise immunity because a low level is less likely to be mistaken for a spurious high‑frequency glitch, especially in noisy environments. While TTL compatibility and power savings are benefits of many designs, the primary reason for the active‑low choice is to make the device robust against interference. Simplified bus wiring is a secondary advantage, but noise resilience is the key factor.

Q5. How do the address lines A1 and A0 determine which counter or register is accessed?

A. 00 selects counter 0, 01 selects counter 1, 10 selects counter 2. ✅
B. 00 selects counter 1, 01 selects counter 0, 10 selects counter 2.
C. 00 selects counter 2, 01 selects counter 1, 10 selects counter 0.
D. 00 selects counter 0, 01 selects counter 2, 10 selects counter 1.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

The 8253 uses a two‑bit address (A1, A0) to choose among its three counters and the control word register. The binary code 00 points to counter 0, 01 to counter 1, and 10 to counter 2; the remaining combination 11 is reserved for the control word register. This mapping follows the table of actions and allows a CPU to address each counter directly. Options B, C, and D scramble the mapping, leading to the wrong counter being programmed or read, which would cause incorrect timing in practical applications.

Q6. What is the meaning of the “X” (undefined) entries in the control‑signal table?

A. Undefined states cause the device to reset.
B. Undefined states are ignored, allowing any value. ✅
C. Undefined states make the output high‑impedance.
D. Undefined states trigger a fault flag.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The “X” entries indicate that the particular signal does not affect the operation for that row; the hardware simply ignores whatever level appears on that line. Therefore, any logical value (0 or 1) is acceptable and will not cause a reset, change output impedance, or raise a fault. Options A, C, and D describe behaviors that do not occur; the 8253 does not reset or generate faults because of undefined inputs, and its outputs remain driven as defined by the active control signals.

Q7. What voltage is supplied to the +VCC pin of the 8253?

B. +12 V
C. +3.3 V
D. +15 V

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

Supplying any other voltage (such as +12 V, +3.3 V, or +15 V) would exceed the device’s specifications and likely damage the internal circuitry. Maintaining the correct +5 V level ensures reliable timing and proper logic levels in real‑world hardware.

Q8. If the CS pin is inadvertently tied to ground, what is the most likely effect on the 8253?

A. The device will always be enabled.
B. The device will never respond to commands.
C. The device will toggle enable state. ✅
D. The device will generate an error.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

Tying CS permanently low forces the chip‑select condition to be continuously active, meaning the 8253 will respond to every set of control signals on the bus. This makes the device always enabled, which can lead to unintended counting or mode changes when other peripherals are addressed. Options B and D suggest disabling or error generation, which does not happen because a low on CS simply keeps the device responsive. Option C describes a toggling behavior that requires additional circuitry, not a static short.

Q9. You need to generate a periodic interrupt using counter 0. Which pins and settings should be used?

A. Connect a periodic pulse to pin 10 (OUT0), hold pin 11 (GATE0) low, and program mode word via WR low.
B. Connect a periodic pulse to pin 11 (GATE0), leave pin 10 floating, and set CS high. ✅
C. Tie pin 10 to VCC, set pin 11 high, and use RD low.
D. Use pin 12 as clock source and ignore pins 10 and 11.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

For a periodic interrupt, the clock input must receive a regular pulse; pin 10 (OUT0) provides the output that can be routed back as a clock or driven externally. Pin 11 (GATE0) must be held low (logic 0) to enable counting continuously. The mode word is written by pulling WR low while CS is low and selecting counter 0 with A1=0, A0=0. Option B mis‑uses the gate pin and disables the device, Option C forces the counter high permanently, and Option D omits the required pins, so only option A correctly configures the hardware for reliable periodic interrupts.
