📖 Interfacing Data converters: A/D and D/A Converters

Digital-to-Analog and Analog-to-Digital Conversion in Microprocessor Systems

Microprocessor-based measurement and control systems must exchange information between digital processing circuits and real-world analog signals. Digital-to-analog converters transform processor-generated binary values into corresponding analog signals, while analog-to-digital converters translate external voltages into digital data for processing. The subject develops both conversion methods through resistive-divider and binary-ladder DAC circuits, converter performance characteristics, and practical interfacing ICs such as DAC0808 and ADC0808/0809. Different conversion architectures reveal how resolution, accuracy, conversion speed, and circuit complexity influence converter selection.

Prerequisites for DAC, ADC, and Analog Signal Interfacing

Students should understand 8085 architecture, eight-bit binary representation, hexadecimal conversion, memory and I/O interfacing, and data-transfer instructions. Knowledge of voltage, current, resistance, reference voltages, Ohm's law, and basic electronic circuits is important. Familiarity with binary-weighted numbers, operational amplifiers, comparators, resistive networks, digital logic, and processor-controlled peripheral communication will help students understand converter circuits and interfacing arrangements.

Learning Objectives for Data Conversion Circuits and Converter ICs

Students should be able to explain digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital conversion principles, analyze resistive-divider and binary-ladder DAC circuits, compare DAC performance characteristics, explain DAC0808 operation and interfacing, identify microprocessor-compatible DAC arrangements, distinguish simultaneous, successive-approximation, counter-ramp, single-slope, and dual-slope ADC methods, describe ADC0808/0809 architecture and operation, understand software-controlled conversion using a DAC, and interpret digital output values, analog signals, resolution, and conversion timing.

Study Sequence for DAC and ADC Circuits and Programming

Begin with digital-to-analog conversion to understand how binary values represent analog output levels. Study resistive-divider and binary-ladder circuits before examining DAC performance criteria and the DAC0808. Progress to processor interfacing and the requirements for connecting digital output codes to analog circuits. Next, study analog-to-digital conversion, beginning with simultaneous comparison. Compare successive-approximation, counter-ramp, single-slope, and dual-slope techniques before examining the ADC0808/0809. Finish with software-controlled conversion using a DAC, connecting conversion algorithms with processor programming and hardware operations.

How DAC Networks, ADC Architectures, and Converter Performance Relate

DAC circuit configurations establish how binary inputs produce analog outputs, while performance criteria explain their limitations. DAC0808 and interfacing demonstrate practical digital-to-analog implementation. ADC fundamentals reverse this relationship by converting analog signals into binary values. Different ADC architectures achieve conversion through alternative comparison and timing methods. ADC0808/0809 provides a practical integrated implementation, while DAC-based software conversion connects digital-to-analog hardware with processor-controlled analog measurement.

Converting a Sensor Voltage to Digital Data and Recreating an Analog Output

Consider an 8085-based measurement system receiving a 2.5 V sensor signal within a 0–5 V input range. ADC0808 selects the required analog channel and performs eight-bit successive-approximation conversion. After conversion completes, the processor reads approximately 80H, representing a midscale digital value. The processor then sends 80H to a DAC0808 through an output interface. The DAC produces a corresponding midscale output current, which an external operational-amplifier circuit converts into an analog voltage. This demonstrates the complete measurement and signal-reproduction path.

Why ADC and DAC Circuits Are Essential in Microprocessor Applications

Digital processors cannot directly interpret continuously varying analog signals or generate arbitrary analog outputs through ordinary binary instructions. Data converters bridge this gap in measurement, instrumentation, automation, and control systems. Understanding their architectures and interfacing requirements is essential for selecting suitable converters, evaluating accuracy, and designing reliable processor-controlled analog applications.

Common Misconceptions About DAC Outputs and ADC Conversion Methods

'A DAC converts analog signals into binary values' is incorrect because DACs generate analog outputs, whereas ADCs produce digital representations. 'A binary ladder and resistive divider use identical circuit arrangements' is incorrect because their resistor networks organize digital weighting differently. 'DAC0808 directly provides a voltage output' is incorrect because it produces an analog current that typically requires an external current-to-voltage conversion circuit.

Summary of ADC and DAC Architectures and Interfacing

Data conversion connects digital processing with analog measurement and signal generation. DAC architectures establish how digital values produce analog outputs, while ADC techniques transform external signals into processor-readable representations. Their performance depends on circuit design, resolution, accuracy, and conversion time. Practical converter ICs demonstrate hardware implementation, while software-controlled conversion illustrates how the 8085 can coordinate analog measurement and digital processing.

Essential Data-Conversion, Resolution, and Converter-Performance Terms

  • DAC - A digital-to-analog converter that transforms binary input values into corresponding analog signals.
  • ADC - An analog-to-digital converter that produces digital representations of analog input signals.
  • Binary-Weighted Resistor Network - A DAC arrangement that uses differently weighted resistors to generate analog output levels.
  • R-2R Ladder - A resistor network using two resistor values to implement digital-to-analog conversion.
  • Resolution - The smallest change in analog input or output represented by one digital step.
  • Settling Time - The time required for a converter output to reach and remain within its specified error range.
  • Successive Approximation - An ADC conversion technique that determines digital bits through successive comparisons.
  • Quantization - The process of assigning a continuous analog value to a discrete digital representation.
