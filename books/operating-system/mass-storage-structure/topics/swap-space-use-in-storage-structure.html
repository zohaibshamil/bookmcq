Q1. A system receives disk I/O requests at cylinders 14, 37, 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183 with the read/write head currently at cylinder 53 and moving outward toward higher numbers. Using the SCAN algorithm, which cylinder will be serviced next?

🔹 A. 14
🔹 B. 65
🔹 C. 67
🔹 D. 183

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

The head is moving toward larger cylinder numbers, so the next request it encounters is the smallest cylinder that is still larger than 53. Among the pending requests, 65 is the first cylinder greater than 53, so it will be serviced next. Option B is correct. Option A (14) and C (67) are either behind the head or farther ahead, and D (183) is much farther, making them incorrect. This reflects how SCAN works by sweeping in one direction and picking the nearest upcoming request.

Q2. Why does the density of pending requests tend to be low for cylinders immediately after the disk head reverses direction in the SCAN algorithm?

🔹 A. Those cylinders have just been serviced, so few requests remain.
🔹 B. The operating system deprioritizes them.
🔹 C. They are physically farther from the motor.
🔹 D. They are reserved for system files.

💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

When the head reaches an end and reverses, the cylinders it just passed have been accessed moments ago, so most of the queued requests for those cylinders have already been satisfied. Consequently, very few new requests are waiting right behind the head, creating a low request density. Option A captures this effect. Options B, C, and D describe unrelated or incorrect reasons, such as OS policy or physical layout, which do not explain the observed low density.

Q3. Which statement best defines the C‑SCAN (Circular SCAN) disk‑scheduling algorithm?

🔹 A. The head moves back and forth, servicing requests in both directions.
🔹 B. The head moves from one end to the other, then jumps back to the start without servicing on the return.
🔹 C. Requests are served in the order they arrive.
🔹 D. The head always moves toward the nearest request.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

C‑SCAN is a variant of SCAN that sweeps the disk in a single direction, servicing all requests on the way to the far end, then instantly returns (or jumps) to the opposite end without servicing any requests during the return. This produces a consistent, one‑direction service pattern. Option B states this precisely. Option A describes ordinary SCAN, Option C describes FCFS, and Option D describes SSTF, none of which match the circular behavior of C‑SCAN.

Q4. Given a uniform distribution of requests across 200 cylinders, which region of the disk will on average experience the longest waiting time under the SCAN algorithm when the head is at the middle and moving toward the higher‑numbered cylinders?

🔹 A. Cylinders near the middle
🔹 B. Cylinders near the lower end
🔹 C. Cylinders near the higher end
🔹 D. All regions experience equal wait

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

When the head moves upward, it first reaches the higher‑numbered cylinders before reversing. Requests located near the far end (the highest cylinders) must wait for the head to travel all the way to that end and then reverse, which adds the greatest travel distance and thus the longest average wait. Option C correctly identifies this region. Options A and B describe areas that are reached sooner, and D is false because wait times differ across the disk.

Q5. What is the primary reason that C‑SCAN generally provides more uniform wait times than SCAN?

🔹 A. It services requests only on the return trip.
🔹 B. It ignores requests at the ends of the disk.
🔹 C. It always services requests in a single direction, eliminating the long pause after a reversal.
🔹 D. It prioritizes larger requests.

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

C‑SCAN moves the head in one direction only, servicing all requests on that pass and then jumping back to the start without servicing any on the return. Because the direction never changes, every request experiences a similar maximum distance before service, leading to more uniform waiting times. Option C states this key behavior. Option A is the opposite of C‑SCAN, Option B is inaccurate, and Option D describes a priority scheme that C‑SCAN does not implement.

Q6. A disk has 200 cylinders. The current head position is 120, moving toward lower numbers. The pending requests are at cylinders 10, 20, 30, 150, 160, 170. Under the SCAN algorithm, which request will be the last to be serviced before the head changes direction?

🔹 A. 10
🔹 B. 20
🔹 C. 30
🔹 D. 170

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

With the head moving downward, SCAN will service requests in decreasing order until it reaches the smallest pending cylinder, which is 10. After servicing 10, the head must reverse direction to address the higher‑numbered requests. Therefore, 10 is the final request before the reversal. Option A correctly reflects this ordering. Options B and C are serviced earlier in the downward sweep, and D (170) lies in the opposite direction, so it is not encountered until after the reversal.

Q7. Consider the same request set as in the previous question (10, 20, 30, 150, 160, 170) and a head at 120 moving toward higher numbers. Compute the total head movement for SCAN and for C‑SCAN. Which algorithm results in a smaller total movement?

🔹 A. SCAN moves 210 cylinders, C‑SCAN moves 310, so SCAN is smaller.
🔹 B. SCAN moves 340 cylinders, C‑SCAN moves 380, so SCAN is smaller.
🔹 C. SCAN moves 340 cylinders, C‑SCAN moves 260, so C‑SCAN is smaller.
🔹 D. Both move the same distance.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation:

For SCAN, the head travels from 120 up to 170 (50 cylinders), then reverses and goes down to 10 (160 cylinders), totaling 210 cylinders. For C‑SCAN, the head goes from 120 up to 170 (50), continues to the disk end at 200 (30), jumps back to 0 (200, no service), and moves up to 30 (30), giving 310 cylinders. Thus SCAN requires less movement, making option A correct. The other options contain incorrect distance calculations or claim equal movement, which do not match the computed totals.

Q8. In a multi‑programmed environment where many processes perform frequent swapping, how does using C‑SCAN instead of SCAN affect overall system throughput?

🔹 A. C‑SCAN reduces throughput because it adds extra head jumps.
🔹 B. C‑SCAN increases throughput by providing predictable latency, which allows the OS to schedule swaps more efficiently.
🔹 C. C‑SCAN has no effect on throughput compared to SCAN.
🔹 D. C‑SCAN decreases throughput by ignoring requests at disk ends.

💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation:

C‑SCAN delivers more predictable and uniform wait times because every request experiences a similar maximum distance before service. This predictability lets the operating system plan swap operations with tighter timing estimates, reducing idle CPU periods and improving overall throughput. Option B captures this benefit. Option A incorrectly assumes the jump cost outweighs benefits, Option C ignores the latency advantage, and Option D misstates C‑SCAN's behavior, as it does not ignore end‑range requests.

Q9. A database server experiences heavy write traffic, and the swap space is located on the same disk. Which scheduling algorithm would most likely minimize the time processes spend waiting for swap I/O, assuming the disk head frequently starts near the middle of the disk?

🔹 A. FCFS
🔹 B. SCAN
🔹 C. C‑SCAN
🔹 D. LOOK

💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation:

When the head often starts near the middle, C‑SCAN’s one‑direction sweep ensures that no request suffers a long pause after a reversal, providing the most consistent response time for swap I/O. This uniformity reduces the worst‑case waiting period for processes needing swap space. Therefore, C‑SCAN is the best choice. FCFS offers no ordering advantage, SCAN can cause longer waits for requests near the far end, and LOOK, while similar to SCAN, still reverses direction and can produce variable latencies.
