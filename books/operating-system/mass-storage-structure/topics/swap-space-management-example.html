What is Swap space management example?

Definition:
Swap space management involves allocating, tracking, and reclaiming swap regions using bitmap or free-list structures to handle page-in/page-out operations efficiently.

Example:
A bitmap with bit bk=1 b_k = 1 bk​=1 indicates swap slot k k k is occupied, enabling O(1) O(1) O(1) allocation and deallocation of contiguous page clusters.

Reason:
Efficient swap metadata management minimizes overhead during memory pressure events and prevents fragmentation that would degrade virtual memory performance.

Q1. What does the SCAN disk scheduling algorithm do?

A.It moves the disk arm outward to the highest‑numbered cylinder, then reverses direction and services requests on the way back. ✅
B.It selects the next request based on shortest seek time.
C.It services requests in the order they arrive, regardless of head movement.
D.It only services requests on the outward pass and ignores the return.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The SCAN algorithm, often called the elevator algorithm, makes the head travel to one end of the disk, servicing all pending requests it encounters, then reverses and continues servicing on the return trip. This behavior matches option A. Options B and D describe unrelated strategies, and option C inaccurately claims the algorithm ignores the return pass. In real systems, SCAN provides more predictable latency than purely random ordering, which is why it is frequently taught in operating‑system courses.

Q2. In C‑SCAN scheduling, what occurs when the head reaches the highest‑numbered cylinder?

A.The head immediately jumps to the lowest‑numbered cylinder without servicing any requests on the way back. ✅
B.The head reverses direction and continues servicing pending requests.
C.The head stops moving until new requests arrive.
D.The head continues in the same direction past the highest cylinder.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: C‑SCAN is a variant of SCAN that, after reaching the outermost track, returns instantly to the innermost track without handling any requests during the return. This “jump” is described by option A. Option B describes the ordinary SCAN reversal, while options C and D suggest behaviors that do not occur in C‑SCAN. Understanding this jump is crucial because it yields more uniform wait times for all requests, a key advantage in high‑throughput storage systems.

Q3. Why can the SCAN algorithm cause longer wait times for requests that lie near the middle of the disk?

A.Because the head must travel to an extreme end before it can come back to the middle cylinders. ✅
B.Because SCAN ignores requests that are not at the extremes.
C.Because SCAN always prioritizes the farthest requests first.
D.Because SCAN services requests in random order.
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: When the head is moving toward an end, any request that is positioned in the middle must wait until the head reaches the far end and then reverses direction, which adds extra travel distance. This is captured by option A. Options B, C, and D misrepresent SCAN’s deterministic behavior; SCAN does not ignore middle requests, nor does it prioritize farthest requests arbitrarily or randomize service order. This delay explains why some systems prefer C‑SCAN for more equitable latency.

Q4. A disk has 200 cylinders numbered 0‑199. The read/write head is currently at cylinder 50 and the pending request queue (in order of arrival) is: 180, 30, 75, 10, 160. Using the SCAN algorithm moving toward higher numbers first, which sequence will be serviced?

A.180, 160, 75, 30, 10 ✅
B.75, 160, 180, 30, 10
C.30, 10, 75, 160, 180
D.10, 30, 75, 160, 180
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Scanning upward first, the head services any request with a cylinder number greater than or equal to 50 as it moves outward: first 180, then 160. After reaching the highest request (180), the head reverses and services the remaining lower‑numbered requests in descending order: 75, then 30, then 10. This yields the order shown in option A. Options B, C, and D either misorder the upward pass or treat the reverse pass incorrectly, leading to inaccurate service sequences.

Q5. Which characteristic of C‑SCAN makes its wait‑time distribution more uniform than SCAN?

A.All requests are serviced in one direction before the head jumps back, eliminating the extra travel for returning requests. ✅
B.The head reverses direction only after all requests are completed.
C.C‑SCAN services requests only at the outermost cylinder.
D.C‑SCAN randomly selects the next request to minimize variance.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: C‑SCAN’s design forces the head to service requests only while moving in a single direction; once it reaches the final cylinder, it quickly returns to the start without handling any requests on the return trip. This eliminates the longer delays that arise for requests located near the opposite end of the disk in a simple SCAN, thereby creating a more uniform wait‑time profile, as described in option A. The other options either misstate the algorithm’s behavior or introduce random selection, which does not occur in C‑SCAN.

Q6. If a system experiences a burst of requests concentrated near cylinder 0 while the head is near the highest cylinder, which algorithm is likely to reduce the maximum response time?

A.C‑SCAN ✅
B.SCAN
C.FCFS
D.SSTF
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: When the head is at the far end and many requests are clustered at the opposite end (cylinder 0), C‑SCAN will quickly jump back to the start after reaching the high‑end cylinder, allowing those clustered requests to be serviced without the head having to travel back through the entire disk. This minimizes the worst‑case response time. SCAN would require the head to reverse direction and travel the full distance, increasing latency. FCFS and SSTF do not guarantee reduced maximum response time in this scenario.

Q7. Consider a uniform random distribution of pending requests across 500 cylinders. When the head reaches the outermost cylinder, which region has the lowest request density?

A.The cylinders just before the outermost cylinder ✅
B.The middle cylinders
C.The cylinders just after the outermost cylinder
D.All regions have equal density
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Because requests that have just been serviced are located near the outermost cylinder, the immediate region ahead of the head contains fewer pending requests, creating a temporary low‑density zone. This matches option A. The middle cylinders retain the average uniform density, and there are no cylinders beyond the outermost cylinder, making options B and C inaccurate. Uniform distribution does not imply equal density at every instant due to the dynamic servicing effect.

Q8. A disk controller uses SCAN and the current head position is at cylinder 120 of a 250‑cylinder disk. Pending requests are at cylinders 10, 30, 200, 220, and 240. Which request will be serviced next?

A.200 ✅
B.220
C.240
D.10
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Since SCAN moves the head toward the higher‑numbered cylinders first, the next request encountered after cylinder 120 is the smallest request greater than 120, which is at cylinder 200. Therefore option A is correct. Cylinder 220 and 240 are further ahead, and cylinder 10 lies in the opposite direction, so they are not the immediate next service target.

Q9. Why does the SCAN algorithm tend to favor requests that are near the current head position?

A.Because those requests are encountered early in the sweep, reducing their waiting time. ✅
B.Because SCAN gives priority to the newest requests.
C.Because SCAN randomly selects the closest request each time.
D.Because SCAN ignores requests far from the head.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: During a SCAN sweep, the head travels in a straight line across the disk, meeting any request that lies on its path. Requests that are already close to the head are encountered early in the sweep, so they experience shorter wait times. This logical consequence aligns with option A. The other options describe behaviors (newest‑first priority, random selection, or ignoring distant requests) that are not part of SCAN’s deterministic movement.

Q10. A system alternates between SCAN and C‑SCAN for different I/O workloads. For a workload where most requests arrive near the middle cylinder and few at the extremes, which algorithm is likely to achieve lower average seek time?

A.SCAN
B.C‑SCAN ✅
C.FCFS
D.SSTF
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: C‑SCAN maintains a constant direction of travel and avoids the extra travel distance incurred when the head must reverse direction after reaching an extreme. When requests cluster in the middle, C‑SCAN’s jump back to the start does not add significant extra movement because the head does not need to travel far to reach the next batch of requests. SCAN would still require a full reversal, potentially increasing average seek distance. Therefore option B is the most appropriate.

Q11. In a scenario where the disk head is at cylinder 0 and the pending request list is uniformly distributed, which algorithm ensures that the maximum distance the head travels between two consecutive services is minimized?

A.SSTF
B.SCAN
C.C‑SCAN ✅
D.FCFS
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: C‑SCAN moves the head from one end of the disk to the other, servicing requests along the way, and then instantly returns to the start without servicing any requests on the return trip. This guarantees that the longest distance the head travels without servicing a request is bounded by the full span of the disk, which is smaller than the potentially larger back‑and‑forth travel of SCAN or the random jumps of FCFS. SSTF could cause starvation, leading to larger gaps. Hence option C is correct.

Q12. If a disk with 1000 cylinders experiences a sudden surge of requests at cylinder 950 while the head is at cylinder 100, which scheduling algorithm would most quickly begin servicing those high‑numbered requests?

A.C‑SCAN
B.SCAN ✅
C.FCFS
D.SSTF
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: SCAN moves the head continuously toward the higher‑numbered cylinders, so it will encounter the surge at cylinder 950 after traveling from 100 upward, without needing to wait for a jump. C‑SCAN would first need to travel all the way to the highest cylinder and then jump back, adding extra delay. FCFS would service requests in arrival order, which may not prioritize the high‑numbered surge. SSTF might jump directly to 950, but it can cause starvation for other requests; the guaranteed forward sweep of SCAN makes it the most reliable choice here, matching option B.

Q13. Why might an operating system prefer C‑SCAN over SCAN for a high‑throughput web server that performs many sequential reads?

A.C‑SCAN provides a more predictable latency for each request, reducing variance. ✅
B.C‑SCAN minimizes the total number of seeks performed.
C.C‑SCAN always services the nearest request first.
D.C‑SCAN avoids servicing any requests at the outermost cylinder.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Web servers benefit from consistent response times; C‑SCAN’s policy of moving in one direction and instantly returning to the start eliminates the long pause that occurs when SCAN reverses direction, thereby delivering more uniform latency across all requests. Option A captures this advantage. While C‑SCAN does reduce variance, it does not necessarily minimize the total number of seeks (option B), nor does it always pick the nearest request (option C), and it certainly does service requests at the outermost cylinder (option D).

Q14. A disk with 400 cylinders receives requests at cylinders 20, 380, 150, 340, and 50. If the head is at cylinder 200 and the system uses SCAN moving toward lower numbers first, which request is served immediately after the head reaches cylinder 0?

A.20 ✅
B.50
C.150
D.340
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: Scanning downward, the head will first encounter the nearest lower‑numbered request after passing 200: cylinder 150, then 100‑ish (none), then 50, then 20, and finally reach cylinder 0. Once the head reaches 0, the next request in the upward sweep will be the smallest remaining request, which is at cylinder 20. Thus option A is correct. The other options represent requests that will be serviced later in the upward pass.

Q15. When using C‑SCAN, what is the effect on request starvation compared to SSTF?

A.C‑SCAN eliminates starvation because every request will eventually be reached in the forward sweep. ✅
B.C‑SCAN can still cause starvation for requests near the jump point.
C.C‑SCAN has the same starvation risk as SSTF.
D.C‑SCAN increases starvation for middle‑range requests.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: SSTF always selects the nearest pending request, which can cause far‑away requests to wait indefinitely if nearer requests keep arriving. C‑SCAN, by moving in one direction and then jumping back, guarantees that every request will be encountered during each full sweep, thus preventing starvation. Option A correctly describes this guarantee. Options B, C, and D incorrectly suggest that C‑SCAN suffers from the same or greater starvation issues, which is not the case.

Q16. Suppose a system implements a hybrid algorithm that uses SCAN during low load and switches to C‑SCAN under heavy load. What is the primary reason for this dynamic change?

A.To balance lower average seek time during light load with more uniform wait times during heavy load. ✅
B.To reduce power consumption during low load.
C.To increase the total number of serviced requests per second.
D.To prioritize writes over reads.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: During light traffic, SCAN’s shorter average seek distance can improve throughput, while under heavy traffic the uniform latency of C‑SCAN prevents long waits for requests at the far end of the disk. This trade‑off matches option A. Power consumption (option B) is not the main driver, nor is maximizing request count (option C) or read/write priority (option D) directly addressed by the algorithm switch.

Q17. A disk controller logs the following seek times (in milliseconds) for consecutive requests under SCAN: 4, 12, 7, 15, 3. Which of the following statements best explains the variability?

A.The head must travel to the end of the disk before reversing, causing longer seeks for far‑away requests. ✅
B.SCANNED requests are always serviced in the order they arrive, eliminating variability.
C.The controller randomly selects the next request, leading to unpredictable seek times.
D.All requests are at the same cylinder, so variability is due to hardware noise.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: SCANNED movement forces the head to travel all the way to the disk’s extreme before reversing direction, so requests that lie far from the current head position incur longer seek distances, which explains the observed variation. Option A captures this effect. The other options mischaracterize SCAN’s deterministic ordering (option B), introduce randomness that does not exist in SCAN (option C), or ignore the actual distribution of request locations (option D).

Q18. If a system experiences a continuous stream of requests uniformly distributed across cylinders, which algorithm provides the lowest worst‑case response time?

A.C‑SCAN ✅
B.SCAN
C.SSTF
D.FCFS
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: C‑SCAN’s policy of moving in a single direction and instantly returning to the start ensures that the longest interval between servicing any two consecutive requests is bounded by the full span of the disk, which is smaller than the worst‑case interval that can occur with SCAN (where the head must travel to an extreme and back) or with SSTF/FCFS, which can suffer from indefinite postponement. Therefore option A is correct.

Q19. Why does the density of pending requests tend to be lower just before the head reaches an end of the disk during a SCAN sweep?

A.Those cylinders have just been serviced, leaving fewer outstanding requests in that region. ✅
B.Requests are generated more frequently near the middle of the disk.
C.The operating system intentionally delays requests near the ends.
D.The disk hardware cannot accept new requests at the extremes.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: As the head moves outward, it services any request it encounters; consequently, the cylinders immediately ahead of the head have recently been cleared, creating a temporary low‑density zone. This reasoning aligns with option A. Options B, C, and D introduce unsupported behaviors that are not characteristic of SCAN operation, making them incorrect.
