What is Extensions of RAID structure?

Definition:
RAID extensions enhance basic levels with nested configurations, erasure coding, or software-defined features addressing limitations of traditional RAID implementations.

Example:
RAID 60 combines RAID 6 groups striped together providing both double-parity protection and improved rebuild performance across sub-arrays.

Reason:
Modern storage demands exceed classic RAID capabilities, requiring hybrid approaches that scale capacity, improve resilience, and adapt to heterogeneous hardware environments.

Q1. Using the C‑SCAN algorithm, a disk has cylinders numbered 0–199, the read‑head starts at cylinder 53, and the pending requests are 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, and 67. What is the total number of cylinders the head must travel to service all requests?

A.382 ✅
B.350
C.410
D.300
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The C‑SCAN algorithm moves the head upward, servicing all requests greater than the current position in sorted order, then jumps to the lowest cylinder and continues. Calculating the distances: 53→65 (12), 65→67 (2), 67→98 (31), 98→122 (24), 122→124 (2), 124→183 (59), 183→199 (16), jump 199→0 (199), 0→14 (14), 14→37 (23). Adding these gives 382 cylinders. Options B, C, and D underestimate or overestimate the travel, so they are incorrect. This total reflects the actual work the disk arm must perform in the given scenario.

Q2. How does the C‑LOOK scheduling algorithm differ from the standard C‑SCAN algorithm in terms of head movement after servicing the last request in one direction?

A.C‑LOOK continues to the maximum cylinder before reversing, while C‑SCAN stops at the last request.
B.C‑LOOK stops at the last request in the current direction and jumps directly to the first request, whereas C‑SCAN moves to the physical end of the disk before wrapping. ✅
C.C‑LOOK reverses direction without any jump, while C‑SCAN always jumps to the start.
D.C‑LOOK services requests randomly, while C‑SCAN follows a fixed order.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: In C‑SCAN the head always proceeds to the physical end of the disk before returning to the start, even if no requests exist beyond the last one. C‑LOOK improves on this by stopping at the furthest pending request in the current direction and then jumping directly to the nearest pending request at the opposite end. Therefore option B correctly describes the difference. Option A reverses the behaviors, option C misstates the jump mechanism, and option D incorrectly claims random servicing; those statements do not match the algorithm definitions.

Q3. A disk with 200 cylinders has pending requests at cylinders 10, 20, 80, and 90, and the read‑head is currently at cylinder 50 moving upward. Which scheduling algorithm will result in the smallest total head movement?

A.FCFS
B.SSTF
C.C‑LOOK ✅
D.C‑SCAN
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: Starting at cylinder 50 and moving upward, C‑LOOK will service 80 and 90, then jump directly to the lowest pending request at 10 and continue to 20, avoiding travel to cylinder 199. The total movement is 30 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 60 cylinders. FCFS would follow the arrival order, causing larger jumps, and SSTF would alternate between nearest requests, still covering more distance. C‑SCAN must travel to the end of the disk before wrapping, resulting in a much higher total. Thus C‑LOOK yields the smallest movement.

Q4. What is the definition of the C‑LOOK disk scheduling algorithm?

A.A variant of C‑SCAN that only travels as far as the furthest request in each direction before jumping to the opposite end. ✅
B.An algorithm that services requests in the order they arrive, without reordering.
C.A method that always moves the head toward the nearest request regardless of direction.
D.A technique that scans the entire disk surface twice before servicing any request.
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: The C‑LOOK algorithm is defined as a variant of C‑SCAN that only moves the head as far as the last request in the current direction before jumping to the first pending request on the opposite side. This definition matches option A. Option B describes a simple first‑come, first‑served approach, option C describes SSTF, and option D incorrectly states that LOOK scans the whole disk twice. Hence only option A correctly captures the algorithm’s behavior.

Q5. Why does the LOOK algorithm generally achieve a lower average seek time than the traditional SCAN algorithm on workloads with clustered requests?

A.LOOK ignores the direction of the head, reducing movement.
B.LOOK limits movement to the outermost cylinder, minimizing travel. ✅
C.LOOK stops the head at the last request in each direction instead of sweeping to the extreme cylinder, eliminating unnecessary travel.
D.LOOK processes requests in a random order, which statistically reduces distance.
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: LOOK improves on SCAN by eliminating travel to the extreme cylinders when no requests exist there. It stops the head at the outermost request in each direction, then reverses, so the arm only covers the range that actually contains pending requests. This reduction in unnecessary movement lowers the total distance the head travels, which directly decreases the average seek time for workloads where requests are clustered. Options A and D mischaracterize the algorithm’s direction handling, and option B incorrectly claims LOOK limits movement to the outermost cylinder, which is not the key advantage.

Q6. A real‑time video streaming server uses a disk with 5000 cylinders. At a critical moment, it receives two batches of read requests: Batch 1 at cylinders 4500‑4600 and Batch 2 at cylinders 100‑200. The head is at cylinder 2500 and must finish both batches before playback resumes. Which algorithm should the system employ to minimize the maximum latency of any request, and why?

A.FCFS, because it preserves request order.
B.SSTF, because it always chooses the nearest request.
C.SCAN, because it moves linearly across the entire disk.
D.C‑LOOK, because it services each batch without traveling to empty extreme cylinders, reducing the longest wait. ✅
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: In a real‑time video scenario, the goal is to keep the longest wait time as short as possible. C‑LOOK services each batch of requests without moving to empty extreme cylinders, jumping directly between the high‑range batch (4500‑4600) and the low‑range batch (100‑200). This avoids the large travel that SCAN or C‑SCAN would incur by sweeping the entire disk. FCFS would preserve arrival order, potentially delaying one batch, and SSTF might keep the head near the current position but could starve the distant batch. Therefore option D correctly identifies C‑LOOK as the optimal choice.
