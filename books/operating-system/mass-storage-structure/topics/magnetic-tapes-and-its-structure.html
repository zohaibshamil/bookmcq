What is Magnetic tapes and its Structure?

Definition:
Magnetic tapes are sequential-access storage media consisting of long plastic strips coated with magnetic particles, wound on reels or cartridges for archival data retention.

Example:
LTO-9 tape stores up to 18 TB native capacity with linear recording density DDD bits per inch along the tape length.

Reason:
Tapes offer extremely low cost per terabyte and offline air-gap security, making them ideal for long-term backup despite slow random access times.

Q1. What is the primary method by which data is stored on a magnetic tape?

A.Magnetic domains aligned on a rotating drum
B.Magnetic particles arranged in linear tracks ✅
C.Optical pits etched on the surface
D.Electrical charges on a semiconductor
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Magnetic tapes store information by magnetizing tiny particles along linear tracks that run the length of the tape. This is described by option B, which correctly identifies magnetic particles arranged in linear tracks. Option A refers to a rotating drum, a technique used in older magnetic drums, not tapes. Option C describes optical storage such as CDs or DVDs, which relies on laser‑etched pits. Option D mentions semiconductor charge storage, which applies to flash memory, not magnetic tape. Understanding the storage mechanism clarifies why tapes are suited for sequential data.

Q2. Why does magnetic tape provide a cost‑effective solution for long‑term data archiving compared to hard disks?

A.Higher data transfer rates
B.Lower per‑gigabyte cost and low power consumption ✅
C.Faster random access
D.Unlimited storage capacity
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Magnetic tape costs far less per gigabyte because the media itself is inexpensive to manufacture and can be stored without power, reducing operating expenses. Option B captures both the low material cost and the low energy use, which together make tape attractive for archiving. Option A is incorrect because tapes generally have slower transfer rates than modern disks. Option C is wrong because tapes are inherently sequential, not random‑access. Option D is false; tape capacity is limited by its physical length. The combination of low purchase price and minimal power draw explains the cost advantage.

Q3. How does increasing the linear tape density affect both storage capacity and the reliability of data retrieval?

A.Increases capacity while decreasing error rates
B.Increases capacity but may increase error rates ✅
C.Decreases capacity and improves reliability
D.No effect on capacity or reliability
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Increasing linear tape density packs more magnetic particles per unit length, thereby raising the total amount of data that can be stored on a given tape. However, tighter packing also makes the recorded bits more susceptible to interference and magnetic noise, which can raise the probability of read errors, so reliability may suffer. Option B correctly reflects this trade‑off. Option A reverses the relationship, suggesting error rates improve, which is unlikely. Option C incorrectly states capacity decreases. Option D claims no impact, ignoring the physics of magnetic recording. Recognizing both benefits and risks is essential when selecting tape density for critical archives.

Q4. A backup system writes data to a tape that moves at 120 mm/s. If the tape format writes at 20 MB/s, how many seconds will it take to store a 600 MB file, ignoring overhead?

A.15 s
B.20 s
C.30 s ✅
D.45 s
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: The time required equals the file size divided by the transfer rate: 600 MB20 MB/s=30 s\frac{600\text{ MB}}{20\text{ MB/s}} = 30\text{ s}20 MB/s600 MB​=30 s. Therefore the correct answer is 30 seconds (option C). Option A (15 s) would be the result of a 40 MB/s rate, which is unrealistic for the given tape speed. Option B (20 s) corresponds to a 30 MB/s rate, and option D (45 s) would require a 13.3 MB/s rate. This calculation shows how tape speed and transfer rate together determine backup duration.

Q8. What advantage does sequential access on magnetic tape give when streaming large video files?

A.Immediate random retrieval
B.Reduced latency for continuous read ✅
C.Higher storage density
D.Faster write speeds
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: Sequential access means the tape head moves past data in order, allowing a continuous stream of bytes to be read without the need to reposition the head for each block. This reduces latency for long, uninterrupted reads, which is ideal for streaming video (option B). Option A is incorrect because tapes cannot retrieve arbitrary locations instantly. Option C refers to density, not access behavior. Option D suggests write speed benefits, which are unrelated to the primary advantage for playback. Understanding sequential access explains why tapes were historically used for video and audio archiving.

Q9. During a data migration, a technician discovers that the existing tape drive uses a 1 inch per second tape speed while the new drive operates at 2 inches per second. B.2 hours

D.5 hours

The new drive writes at 20 MB/s, so time = 200 000 MB20 MB/s=10 000 s\frac{200\,000\text{ MB}}{20\text{ MB/s}} = 10\,000\text{ s}20 MB/s200000 MB​=10000 s (~2.78 h). The saved time is 20 000 s − 10 000 s = 10 000 s, which equals about 2.8 hours (option C). Options A and B underestimate the benefit, while option D overestimates it. This demonstrates how doubling tape speed can roughly halve the migration duration when other factors remain constant.
