What is Selecting a RAID level in RAID structure?

Definition:
Selecting a RAID level requires analyzing workload read/write ratios, failure tolerance needs, capacity requirements, and budget constraints to match appropriate configuration.

Example:
A read-heavy database may choose RAID 10 for fast rebuilds and read performance, while archival storage prefers RAID 6 for dual-failure protection.

Reason:
Each RAID level has distinct performance curves and failure modes; incorrect selection leads to either wasted resources or unacceptable risk exposure.

Q1. Recall: Which RAID level provides block-level striping with distributed parity, allowing the array to survive a single disk failure?

A.RAID 0
B.RAID 1
C.RAID 5 ✅
D.RAID 10
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: C

📖 Explanation: RAID 5 uses striping with distributed parity, so if one disk fails the data can be rebuilt from the parity information. RAID 0 has no redundancy, RAID 1 mirrors data but does not stripe, and RAID 10 combines mirroring and striping but requires at least four disks. Understanding this distinction helps choose RAID 5 when you need both capacity efficiency and fault tolerance.

Q2. Conceptual: Why does RAID 6 tolerate two simultaneous disk failures while RAID 5 tolerates only one?

A.RAID 6 stores two independent parity blocks per stripe ✅
B.RAID 5 stores two independent parity blocks per stripe
C.RAID 6 uses mirroring instead of parity
D.RAID 5 uses mirroring instead of parity
💡 Difficulty: easy | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: RAID 6 adds a second parity block to each stripe, providing two separate pieces of redundancy. This extra parity can reconstruct data even after two disks fail. RAID 5 only has one parity block, so a second failure would lose data. Recognizing the role of dual parity explains why RAID 6 is chosen for higher reliability in critical servers.

Q3. Conceptual: How does the write performance of RAID 0 compare to RAID 5, and what causes the difference?

A.RAID 0 writes are faster because no parity is calculated ✅
B.RAID 5 writes are faster because parity is pre‑computed
C.Both have identical write speeds
D.RAID 0 writes are slower due to striping overhead
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: RAID 0 writes data directly to multiple disks without any parity calculation, so the operation is quick. RAID 5 must read old data, compute new parity, and write both data and parity, adding latency. This extra processing makes RAID 5 slower for writes, though reads can be faster due to striping. Knowing this helps decide RAID 0 for performance‑critical, non‑critical workloads.

Q4. Conceptual: In a storage system requiring both high read throughput and redundancy, why might an administrator choose RAID 10 over RAID 5?

A.RAID 10 provides both mirroring and striping, improving read speed and fault tolerance ✅
B.RAID 5 offers better write performance than RAID 10
C.RAID 10 has lower storage overhead than RAID 5
D.RAID 5 can survive two disk failures, unlike RAID 10
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: RAID 10 combines mirroring (redundancy) with striping (parallel reads), delivering excellent read performance and the ability to survive multiple disk failures depending on disk placement. RAID 5’s parity can become a bottleneck for reads under heavy load and only tolerates a single failure. Therefore, RAID 10 is preferred when both speed and strong fault tolerance are essential.

Q5. Conceptual: Explain why a RAID 5 array with many small disks may suffer from the write‑hole problem, and how RAID 6 mitigates this issue.

A.The write‑hole occurs when a power loss corrupts parity; RAID 6 adds a second parity to protect against this ✅
B.RAID 5’s single parity is insufficient for any failure; RAID 6 uses mirroring instead
C.RAID 5 does not suffer from write‑hole; only RAID 6 does
D.RAID 6 eliminates the write‑hole by using faster disks
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: A

📖 Explanation: When a RAID 5 array experiences a sudden power loss during a write, the parity may not be updated, leaving inconsistent data—a write‑hole. With many small disks, write operations are frequent, increasing risk. RAID 6 adds a second, independent parity block, so even if one parity is corrupted, the other can reconstruct data, reducing the chance of data loss from a write‑hole. This extra protection is why RAID 6 is chosen for larger, more active arrays.

Q6. Application: A database server has four 2 TB drives and needs at least 4 TB usable space with redundancy. Which RAID level meets the requirement while maximizing performance?

A.RAID 0
B.RAID 1
C.RAID 5
D.RAID 10 ✅
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: With four 2 TB drives, RAID 10 mirrors two pairs and stripes across them, yielding 4 TB usable space (half the total) and providing redundancy. RAID 0 offers 8 TB but no fault tolerance. RAID 1 would give only 2 TB usable. RAID 5 would give 6 TB usable but has slower write performance and only tolerates one failure. RAID 10 thus satisfies the space and performance needs.

C.RAID 6
D.RAID 10
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: RAID 5 distributes parity across all disks, providing fault tolerance for one failed disk while using only one disk’s worth of space for parity. With ten 4 TB disks, usable capacity is . RAID 0 gives full 40 TB but no redundancy, RAID 6 tolerates two failures but costs two disks for parity, and RAID 10 would halve capacity. Therefore RAID 5 best meets the efficiency and single‑failure tolerance requirement.

B.RAID 6 ✅
C.RAID 10
D.RAID 0
💡 Difficulty: medium | ✅ Correct: B

📖 Explanation: RAID 6 adds two independent parity blocks per stripe, allowing the array to survive two disk failures. With eight 6 TB drives, usable capacity is . While RAID 10 also provides fault tolerance, it would reduce usable space to 24 TB and still only tolerate one failure per mirrored pair. RAID 5 cannot handle two failures. Hence RAID 6 balances redundancy and write performance for the scenario.

Q9. Application: A video editing workstation uses three 1 TB SSDs. The user wants the fastest possible write speed but also needs to protect against a single drive loss. Which RAID level should be chosen?

A.RAID 0
B.RAID 1
C.RAID 5
D.RAID 10 ✅
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: RAID 10 mirrors one drive and stripes the remaining two, giving both high write speed from striping and redundancy from mirroring. With three 1 TB SSDs, one drive is mirrored, leaving 2 TB usable and protecting against a single drive failure. RAID 0 offers the fastest writes but no protection. RAID 1 would limit capacity to 1 TB. RAID 5 provides redundancy but incurs parity overhead, slowing writes compared to RAID 10. Thus RAID 10 is optimal.

D.RAID 0

📖 Explanation: RAID 6 can tolerate two simultaneous disk failures by storing two parity blocks per stripe. With five 2 TB drives, usable capacity is , meeting the requirement exactly. RAID 5 would only survive one failure, RAID 10 would reduce usable space to 4 TB, and RAID 0 provides no fault tolerance. Therefore RAID 6 is the only configuration that fulfills both capacity and dual‑failure tolerance.

Q11. Application: A cloud service provider must design a storage pool using twelve 10 TB drives that can handle up to three concurrent disk failures while keeping write latency low. Which RAID configuration is most suitable?

A.RAID 5
B.RAID 6
C.RAID 10
D.RAID 60 ✅
💡 Difficulty: hard | ✅ Correct: D

📖 Explanation: RAID 60 combines multiple RAID 6 groups striped together, allowing the array to survive three or more disk failures while maintaining lower write latency than a single large RAID 6 group. With twelve 10 TB drives, the array can be organized as three RAID 6 groups of four drives each, then striped (RAID 0) across them, providing high performance and the ability to lose up to three disks (one from each group). RAID 5 cannot handle three failures, RAID 6 tolerates only two, and RAID 10 would halve capacity and still only protect against one failure per mirrored pair.
